# Healthy Little Minds Voice and Tone

## Purpose

This document defines the verbal identity of Healthy Little Minds. It explains how the organization sounds across every touchpoint so that people experience one recognizable voice regardless of who writes the words, which product they use, or which language and format carries the message.

Voice is the consistent character behind the words. Tone is how that character adapts to audience, task, channel, and emotional context. A story for a five-year-old, a classroom guide, a podcast introduction, and an error message should not sound identical. They should, however, feel as though they come from the same thoughtful, trustworthy source.

A consistent voice builds recognition. More importantly, it builds trust. Readers learn that Healthy Little Minds will not surprise them with blame, sensational claims, false certainty, or pressure. They can expect calm explanations, honest boundaries, and practical help.

Voice also contributes to emotional safety. Many people reach the platform while worried, frustrated, tired, confused, or uncertain about a child. Language can help them orient themselves and choose a constructive next step, or it can intensify fear and shame. Every writer is responsible for the likely emotional effect of a message as well as its literal meaning.

Consistent communication supports learning. Familiar patterns of explanation reduce cognitive effort. When readers do not need to decode the writer's attitude, they can give more attention to psychological ideas and their application.

This document complements `CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md`. The Content Style Guide explains how to plan, structure, write, and approve content. This document explains how Healthy Little Minds sounds while doing so. The two should be used together with the Project Charter, Product Principles, Writing Style handbook, Accessibility Standard, and relevant product guides.

The voice applies to website copy, the Emotion Library, Parents Hub, Teachers Hub, interactive tools, stories, audiobooks, worksheets, emails, notifications, product descriptions, marketing, social media, Will Talks, YouTube descriptions, errors, empty states, and AI-assisted content.

No voice preference overrides accuracy, accessibility, safeguarding, privacy, cultural respect, or child dignity. When a situation requires direct safety language, be direct. When evidence is uncertain, be honest. When an interface needs one clear word, clarity matters more than personality.

## Voice and Tone

Voice and tone are related but distinct.

- **Voice** is the stable personality of Healthy Little Minds.
- **Tone** is the appropriate expression of that personality in a particular moment.

The voice remains calm, warm, human, respectful, curious, encouraging, practical, hopeful, emotionally validating, evidence-informed, honest, and professional. Tone may become more concise during an error, more playful in a young child's story, more reflective in Will Talks, or more formal in professional guidance.

Adaptation should never become inconsistency. We do not become alarming because a topic is serious, childish because the audience is young, academic because the audience is professional, or promotional because a resource is for sale.

## Our Voice

The qualities below work together. Overusing any one of them can distort the voice. Warmth without honesty becomes empty reassurance. evidence without humanity becomes clinical. encouragement without respect becomes pressure. Calm without practical guidance becomes vague.

### Calm

Healthy Little Minds communicates with steadiness. We name difficult experiences without amplifying them, organize information clearly, and give readers enough space to think.

Calm does not mean emotionally flat. It means proportionate. We can say that an experience is painful, frightening, or urgent when that is accurate. We do not add alarm to capture attention.

Use calm language by:

- placing the most useful information early;
- choosing measured words instead of dramatic ones;
- separating immediate actions from background explanation;
- avoiding repeated warnings, capitals, and exclamation marks;
- making uncertainty understandable;
- offering one manageable next step.

**Not calm:** This alarming behaviour could be a warning sign you must never ignore.

**Healthy Little Minds:** A change in behaviour can have many causes. Notice how long it continues, how strongly it affects daily life, and whether the child may need additional support.

### Warm

We sound like one person helping another. We recognize effort, uncertainty, and context. Warmth appears through attention and care, not through excessive enthusiasm, pet names, emojis, or automatic reassurance.

Warm language acknowledges the reader's experience without claiming to know it exactly. It avoids cold institutional phrases and allows room for repair.

**Cold:** Users are advised to implement the following procedure.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Here is one approach you can try. Adapt it to the child and the situation.

Warmth should never obscure a boundary. “I can see you are angry, and I will not let you hit” is both warm and clear.

### Human

We use natural language and recognizable situations. We do not sound like a policy generator, a textbook, or a machine pretending to have feelings.

Human writing uses contractions where natural, acknowledges complexity, and chooses words people might actually say. It does not manufacture intimacy. Healthy Little Minds should not claim personal experience, emotions, or understanding it cannot possess.

**Robotic:** Your emotional state has been successfully recorded.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Your check-in has been saved.

**Artificially intimate:** We know exactly how hard this is for you.

**Healthy Little Minds:** This may be a difficult moment. You do not need to work through it all at once.

### Respectful

We treat children and adults as people with dignity, agency, knowledge, and context. We describe actions without turning them into identity. We guide without assuming carelessness, ignorance, or bad intent.

Respect means:

- speaking to readers, not down to them;
- preserving choice where choice is possible;
- keeping corrections specific and proportionate;
- avoiding moral labels for behaviour;
- recognizing families and educators as partners;
- stating important boundaries clearly;
- protecting privacy and avoiding forced disclosure.

**Disrespectful:** A defiant child refuses to follow simple instructions.

**Healthy Little Minds:** A child may refuse an instruction for several reasons, including confusion, distress, difficulty, or a need for control. The boundary may still matter; understanding the context helps the adult respond more effectively.

### Curious

We seek understanding before settling on an explanation. We distinguish observation from inference and invite readers to consider context.

Curiosity sounds like:

- “What tends to happen just before this?”
- “What might the child be communicating?”
- “Which part of the routine is difficult?”
- “What helped, even a little?”

Curiosity is not indecision. When safety or safeguarding is involved, we give clear instructions. We also avoid interrogating children or asking adults to investigate matters that require trained professionals.

### Encouraging

We help readers see a manageable way forward. We emphasize that emotional skills develop through teaching, practice, relationships, and repair.

Encouragement should increase capability rather than demand optimism. It does not promise that a technique will work or imply that persistent difficulty reflects a lack of effort.

**Empty encouragement:** You've got this! Everything will be fine.

**Healthy Little Minds:** You do not need to solve everything today. Choose one small step, try it consistently, and notice what helps.

### Practical

We connect knowledge to decisions, examples, routines, words, and next steps. Readers should understand what an idea may look like in daily life.

Practical writing explains:

- what to notice;
- what the idea means;
- what someone can try;
- how to adapt it;
- what to do if it does not help;
- when further support may be appropriate.

We respect real constraints. Advice should not assume unlimited time, money, staff, space, technology, or emotional capacity.

### Hopeful

We communicate that learning, connection, repair, and improvement are possible. Hope rests on realistic options, not guaranteed outcomes.

Healthy hope accepts that progress may be uneven and that some circumstances require sustained or specialist support. It does not frame every difficulty as an opportunity or ask people to feel grateful for hardship.

**False hope:** This simple technique will transform your child's confidence.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Practising this skill may help a child respond with greater confidence over time. Begin slowly and adapt the activity to their needs.

### Emotionally validating

We acknowledge emotions without judging, dismissing, or exaggerating them. Validation means recognizing that an experience makes sense from a person's perspective. It does not mean agreeing with every conclusion or accepting harmful action.

Use tentative observations when another person's internal state is not known:

- “It looks like that change was difficult.”
- “You may be feeling disappointed.”
- “Would you like to tell me what happened?”

Avoid “I know exactly how you feel” and fixed interpretations of faces, bodies, or behaviour. Emotions are real; our interpretation may be incomplete.

### Evidence-informed

We communicate psychological and educational knowledge in proportion to the evidence. We distinguish research findings, professional guidance, informed interpretation, lived experience, and editorial choice.

Evidence-informed language avoids authority theatre. We do not use scientific terminology, brain imagery, or citations to make ordinary advice sound more certain than it is.

**Overstated:** Science proves that gratitude rewires children's brains.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Some research suggests that gratitude activities may support aspects of wellbeing for some children. They should not be used to dismiss sadness, anger, unfairness, or difficult circumstances.

### Honest

We state uncertainty, limitations, tradeoffs, errors, and boundaries. We say when Healthy Little Minds provides education rather than professional care. We correct mistakes openly.

Honesty includes making costs, account requirements, data use, downloads, external links, and service limitations clear before someone acts. It also means admitting when a question cannot be answered from general information.

### Professional without sounding academic

Our writing is careful, reliable, and composed. It uses technical terms when they add necessary precision and explains them in plain language. Professionalism comes from sound judgment, not formality.

We avoid inflated prose, unnecessary jargon, citation dumping, and impersonal language. Professional readers deserve clarity too.

**Academic-sounding:** Affective dysregulation may occasion maladaptive behavioural manifestations.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Strong emotions can make it harder for a child to pause, process language, and choose what to do next.

## What We Are Not

The voice must never drift into the following patterns.

### Dramatic

Dramatic language enlarges a situation beyond the evidence. It consumes attention that readers need for understanding and action. Avoid “devastating,” “shocking,” “crisis,” and “catastrophe” unless they are literally accurate and necessary.

### Sensational

Sensational writing turns concern into a hook. Hidden signs, shocking truths, secret methods, and claims that “everything you know is wrong” weaken trust and distort psychology.

### Preachy

Preachy writing presents guidance as moral superiority. It tells people what good parents, caring teachers, or emotionally intelligent children must do. We explain reasons, acknowledge context, and leave room for informed judgment.

### Patronizing

Patronizing language assumes the reader cannot understand complexity or needs praise for basic participation. We simplify ideas, not people. Avoid baby talk, forced cheerfulness, and phrases such as “It's really easy!”

### Childish

Child-friendly does not mean childish. Children deserve accurate words, emotional complexity, and respect. Cute language, excessive rhyme, diminutives, and mascots should never obstruct understanding or make older children feel belittled.

### Sarcastic

Sarcasm depends on saying one thing while meaning another. It can be inaccessible, culturally fragile, and shaming. It is especially unsuitable in errors, behaviour guidance, emotional education, and child-facing content.

### Overly clinical

Clinical distance can make ordinary emotions sound disordered and readers feel observed rather than supported. Use diagnostic terminology only when accurate, necessary, and within scope. Healthy Little Minds is an educational platform, not a clinic.

### Fear-based

Fear may produce attention, but it undermines informed choice. Do not imply that one parenting mistake causes permanent damage or that a reader must act immediately unless a genuine safety risk requires urgent action.

### Manipulative

Do not use guilt, shame, false scarcity, hidden conditions, loaded choices, or emotional pressure. “Yes, I care about my child” is never an acceptable consent or subscription label.

### Sales-driven

The reader's need comes before conversion. Product value should be explained accurately. We do not inflate a problem so that a resource appears necessary, disguise advertising as education, or force promotional language into sensitive content.

### Robotic

Robotic language is generic, impersonal, and detached from the actual moment. Avoid phrases such as “your request has been processed” when “Your worksheet is ready” is clearer. Do not add fake empathy to compensate.

### Emotionally exaggerated

Not every success is amazing and not every inconvenience is upsetting. Match emotional intensity to the event. Excessive celebration can pressure children; excessive apology can make a minor error feel unsafe.

## Audience Adaptation

The voice stays recognizable while vocabulary, rhythm, detail, examples, and directness adapt to the audience.

### Children

Use concrete words, manageable sentences, warm choices, and recognizable situations. Be playful when play supports learning, but never silly at the child's expense. Do not make children responsible for adult emotions or imply that difficult feelings reduce their worth.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Anger can feel big. It may make your body hot or your hands tight. You can pause, move to a safe place, or ask a trusted adult for help.

For younger children, use one idea at a time and adult-guided options. For older children, allow more precise vocabulary, multiple perspectives, and reflection. Always provide a dignified way not to share private information.

### Parents and caregivers

Sound companionable, steady, and realistic. Recognize care without praising perfection. Put the immediate concern and next step early. Avoid blame and acknowledge that stress, time, culture, resources, and a child's individual needs affect what is possible.

**Healthy Little Minds:** If you reacted in a way you regret, repair still matters. Return when you are both calmer, acknowledge what happened, and decide what support could make a different response easier next time.

### Teachers and educators

Sound collegial, practical, and concise. Respect professional judgment and classroom constraints. Explain the psychology sufficiently, then translate it into routines, language, adaptations, and reflection.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Introduce the routine when the class is settled. Practise it briefly, explain its purpose, and offer a non-public option for children who do not want to share.

Do not imply that a teacher can solve systemic difficulty through attitude or one strategy.

### Professionals

Use precise terminology, transparent evidence, scope, references, and limitations. Keep sentences clear. Do not become more formal than the subject requires or assume that jargon demonstrates expertise.

Professional content may be denser, but it should still lead to practical interpretation and avoid unsupported certainty.

### General public

Use plain language and define necessary terms. Do not assume prior psychology knowledge, parenting experience, professional status, or one cultural context. Give readers a clear route to more detailed audience-specific guidance.

### Will Talks audience

Will Talks may be more conversational, reflective, and story-driven. The host can ask open questions, acknowledge uncertainty, and allow moments of emotional resonance. The voice remains responsible: personal stories are not general evidence, guests are not infallible, and difficult topics are not used for entertainment.

## Emotional Communication

When readers are emotionally activated, reduce demands. Lead with orientation and validation, then offer a small next step. Do not require someone to absorb a long explanation before receiving practical help.

### When users feel angry

Do not tell people to calm down or frame anger as a failure. Acknowledge the feeling while preserving boundaries around action.

**Avoid:** Stop being angry and think rationally.

**Use:** Anger can signal that something feels wrong, unfair, or blocked. If possible, pause before acting. Choose a response that protects you and other people.

### When users feel anxious

Use predictable structure and concrete information. Avoid adding possibilities the reader did not ask about. Never promise that nothing bad will happen.

**Avoid:** Don't worry—everything will be fine.

**Use:** It makes sense to want certainty. Begin with what you know now and choose one next step. If the worry is persistent or interfering with daily life, additional support may help.

### When users feel overwhelmed

Reduce choices and sequence the task. Separate what needs attention now from what can wait.

**Use:** You do not need to complete every step today. Start with the first one. You can save this page and return later.

### When users feel confused

Take responsibility for clarity. Restate the idea, provide an example, and offer a route to help.

**Avoid:** As explained above, simply follow the instructions.

**Use:** This step may be unclear. Choose the child's age range first; we will then show the relevant activities.

### When users feel guilty

Do not intensify guilt to motivate change or erase accountability with reassurance. Focus on repair and future support.

**Use:** Regret can show that you want to respond differently. Acknowledge what happened, repair where you can, and choose one change for next time.

### When users feel frustrated

Name the obstacle and help restore agency. In service errors, do not blame the user.

**Use:** We could not save your changes. Your text is still here. Check your connection and try again.

### When users feel sad

Allow sadness without rushing to make it positive. Use gentle direct language and manageable options.

**Avoid:** Look on the bright side.

**Use:** Sadness may need time, care, and company. You can talk with someone you trust, sit quietly, or choose one small comforting activity.

### When users feel hopeful

Support hope without converting it into a promise. Recognize progress specifically.

**Use:** You noticed what helped and tried it again. That kind of practice can make the skill easier to use over time.

Across emotional contexts, language should support regulation by being predictable, proportionate, non-judgmental, and actionable. It should not mirror or amplify the highest emotional intensity in the room.

## Educational Voice

Healthy Little Minds teaches by guiding, modelling, inviting, and encouraging reflection.

### Guide

Explain the route and why it matters. Give enough structure for action while allowing adaptation.

**Instead of:** Follow this method exactly.

**Use:** Begin with these steps. Adapt the wording and pace while preserving the purpose: helping the child feel safe enough to participate.

### Model

Show what a principle looks and sounds like. Use examples that are realistic rather than perfect.

**Example:** “I can see you want more time. The activity is ending now. Would you like to put away the pencils or the paper?”

### Invite

Use open questions and genuine options. Invitation is not a disguised command. If an action is required, say so respectfully rather than offering a false choice.

### Encourage reflection

Ask what happened, what influenced it, what helped, and what could change. Reflection should support learning, not confession or blame.

### Avoid lecturing

Do not pile up principles without application or speak as the final authority on another person's child, classroom, culture, or experience. Present credible evidence and boundaries, then respect professional and family knowledge.

### Avoid shame

Never imply that responsible adults always remain calm, that emotionally skilled children never struggle, or that asking for help is failure. Accountability and compassion can coexist.

## Voice Examples

The three levels below show the difference between merely correcting a problem and expressing the full Healthy Little Minds voice.

### Teaching emotions

**Bad:** Negative emotions are harmful and need to be controlled.

**Better:** All emotions provide information, but some can feel difficult.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Every emotion can tell us something about our needs or experience. A feeling is not wrong, even when it is uncomfortable. We can learn ways to express it safely and choose what to do next.

### Giving parenting advice

**Bad:** Good parents stay calm during tantrums.

**Better:** Try to stay calm when your child is upset.

**Healthy Little Minds:** A steady response can help, but no parent remains calm all the time. If you become overwhelmed, pause when it is safe, return to the child, and repair the moment together.

### Teacher strategies

**Bad:** This behaviour hack will transform your classroom.

**Better:** Use a visual routine to improve transitions.

**Healthy Little Minds:** A simple visual routine can make transitions more predictable. Introduce it when the class is settled, practise it together, and adapt it for children who need a different cue.

### Behaviour guidance

**Bad:** Ignore attention-seeking behaviour.

**Better:** Avoid rewarding disruptive behaviour with attention.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Before deciding how to respond, consider what the behaviour may be communicating. The child may need help, connection, clarity, or a more workable way to join in. Maintain the boundary while teaching and noticing the behaviour you want the child to use instead.

### Introducing psychology

**Bad:** Neuroscience proves that deep breathing resets the brain.

**Better:** Deep breathing can help people calm down.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Slow breathing can help some people feel more settled by changing the pace of the body's stress response. It does not work for everyone; offer another option if focusing on breathing feels uncomfortable.

### Error messages

**Bad:** Oops! You entered an invalid value.

**Better:** Enter a valid email address.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Enter an email address in the format name@example.com.

### Success messages

**Bad:** Amazing! You're a superstar!

**Better:** Success! Your worksheet is ready.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Your worksheet is ready to download.

### Subscription messages

**Bad:** Join now so you never fail your child again.

**Better:** Subscribe for the latest parenting tips.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Receive practical emotional-learning resources and new activity updates. Choose how often you hear from us, and unsubscribe at any time.

### Calls to action

**Bad:** Unlock the secret now!

**Better:** Learn more.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Explore ways to respond to frustration.

### Podcast introductions

**Bad:** Today's incredible guest will reveal the truth about childhood trauma.

**Better:** Today we discuss childhood trauma with an expert.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Today we explore what trauma-informed practice can—and cannot—mean in everyday classrooms. Our guest shares research, experience, and practical questions educators can take back to their teams.

### Book descriptions

**Bad:** A life-changing story every child must read.

**Better:** A helpful story about managing worry.

**Healthy Little Minds:** A gentle story about a child who notices worry, asks for support, and takes one small step. Designed for shared reading with children aged seven to ten.

### Interactive prompts

**Bad:** Tell us exactly why you feel sad.

**Better:** Write about your sadness.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Would you like to draw, write, or choose a word for how you feel? You can also skip this question.

## Microcopy

Microcopy should be concise, specific, and complete enough to support action. Personality is secondary to usability.

### Buttons

Begin with a clear verb and name the outcome: “Download worksheet,” “Save changes,” “Play story,” or “Try again.” Avoid “Submit,” “Go,” and “Click here” when a more specific label is possible.

### Menus

Use familiar destination names consistently. Do not make core navigation playful or metaphorical. “Parents Hub” is clearer than “For grown-ups.”

### Labels

Use visible labels that remain understandable when fields contain data. Label optional fields as optional. Do not rely on placeholder text as the only label.

### Tooltips

Use tooltips for brief supplementary help, not essential instructions. Write a complete phrase or sentence and keep the triggering control accessible by keyboard and touch.

### Form validation

Explain what needs attention and how to fix it. Avoid “invalid,” “wrong,” and error codes without explanation.

**Use:** Choose at least one topic, or select “No preference.”

### Loading messages

State what is happening without pretending the system is thinking or feeling.

**Use:** Loading your saved resources…

For longer waits, explain that work is continuing and whether the user can leave safely.

### Empty states

Explain why the area is empty and offer a useful next action without implying failure.

**Use:** You have not saved any stories. Explore the story library to find one you may want to return to.

### Search

Use a descriptive field label such as “Search resources.” For no results, repeat the search term where safe and suggest specific adjustments.

**Use:** No resources matched “sleep routine.” Check the spelling or try a broader term such as “sleep.”

### Downloads

Name the file and format before download when helpful. If a file is large, state its size. Confirm when the download begins or completes only if the browser experience makes that useful.

### Login

Use “Log in” for the action and “login” only as a noun or modifier. Explain authentication errors without revealing whether a particular account exists when privacy or security requires discretion.

**Use:** We could not log you in with those details. Check them and try again, or reset your password.

### Membership

Explain benefits, cost, renewal, cancellation, data use, and limitations plainly. Do not use emotional pressure or make the free route deliberately confusing.

### Language switching

Display language names in the language itself where practical. Tell users whether switching changes the current page or returns them to a language home page. Do not use flags as the sole language label.

### Confirmation dialogs

Name the action and consequence. Use specific buttons.

**Title:** Delete this saved activity?

**Message:** This removes the activity from your account. You can find and save it again later.

**Actions:** Delete activity / Keep activity

Avoid “Are you sure?” with “Yes” and “No.”

### 404 pages

Be clear and helpful, not whimsical at the user's expense.

**Use:** We could not find this page. The link may be out of date. Search the resource library or return to the home page.

### Maintenance pages

Explain what is unavailable, whether saved work is safe, what users can still do, and when to check again if a reliable estimate exists. Do not invent an estimate.

### Offline states

State that the device appears offline, clarify what remains available, and provide a retry action.

**Use:** You appear to be offline. Saved downloads are still available. Reconnect to load new resources.

## Marketing Voice

Healthy Little Minds promotes resources by making their usefulness clear. We educate first and sell second.

Marketing should accurately describe:

- who a resource is for;
- what need it addresses;
- what it contains;
- how it can be used;
- what it does not do;
- price, access, format, and accessibility information;
- evidence or review status where relevant.

Do not enlarge a reader's fear so the product appears necessary. Avoid countdown timers, false scarcity, hidden renewal, guilt-based choices, inflated testimonials, unsupported comparisons, and guarantees about behaviour, wellbeing, or learning.

**Sales-driven:** Fix emotional outbursts before they damage your child's future.

**Healthy Little Minds:** This guide explains why emotional outbursts may happen and offers practical ways to support safety, connection, and skill-building.

Campaign language may be lively, but it must remain calm and accurate. “New” is useful information; “revolutionary” rarely is. Testimonials should be representative, consented, contextualized, and never treated as evidence of effectiveness.

## Social Media Voice

Social platforms change length, pacing, visual context, and audience expectation. They do not change our values. Every post should remain useful, respectful, accessible, and emotionally responsible.

### LinkedIn

Use a professional, reflective voice. Share practical implications, research context, organizational learning, and resources for educators and partners. Avoid corporate clichés, self-congratulation, and engagement bait.

### Instagram

Lead with one clear idea and use captions to add nuance. Carousels should teach a coherent sequence rather than split a sentence across slides. Provide alt text and do not make graphics the only source of essential information.

### Facebook

Use accessible explanations and community-relevant resources for parents, educators, and general audiences. Invite discussion with specific, non-invasive questions. Do not request public disclosure about children.

### YouTube

Titles and thumbnails should accurately represent the video. Descriptions should identify the topic, audience, speakers, practical value, chapters, transcript, references, and support information where relevant. Avoid shocked faces, alarming claims, and misleading curiosity gaps.

### Threads

Use a conversational, thoughtful style. Short sequences may unpack one idea or misconception. Keep qualifications close to claims and do not let brevity create false certainty.

### X

Be concise and factual. Link to fuller context when a topic cannot be represented responsibly in one post. Avoid reactive argument, pile-ons, quote-post shaming, and reducing sensitive issues to slogans.

### TikTok

Use direct spoken language, clear captions, and one practical learning goal. Trends may be used only when they fit naturally and preserve dignity. Do not dramatize symptoms, imitate distress for entertainment, or present a rapid video as diagnosis or treatment.

Across all platforms:

- adapt the format, not the truth;
- make images and video accessible;
- protect children's privacy;
- moderate harmful misinformation and personal attacks;
- distinguish education, opinion, experience, and promotion;
- never trade nuance or dignity for reach.

## Will Talks

Will Talks has a slightly more conversational, reflective, and story-driven tone than written guidance. It may include pauses, personal observations, gentle humour, uncertainty, and the natural rhythm of dialogue.

The host should sound curious rather than performatively certain. Questions should help guests explain meaning, evidence, limits, and practical relevance. Lived experience should be heard with respect and identified as experience, not automatically generalized.

The podcast may hold emotional complexity for longer than a short article. It should not turn pain into spectacle, force disclosure, or present a dramatic narrative as the price of attention. Sensitive episodes need appropriate content notes, support information, and careful editing.

An introduction should orient listeners: what the episode explores, why it matters, who is speaking, and what listeners may take away. A conclusion should summarize carefully and connect to practical resources without becoming a sales pitch.

**Healthy Little Minds introduction:** Today we are talking about what repair can look like after a difficult moment between an adult and a child. We will explore why repair matters, what it cannot solve on its own, and a few words adults can adapt in real life.

Conversational does not mean unreviewed. Claims, descriptions, titles, transcripts, show notes, and promotional excerpts remain subject to evidence, safeguarding, accessibility, and editorial standards.

## AI Communication

AI-assisted content must sound recognizably like Healthy Little Minds, but imitation of tone is not enough. The content must also be accurate, useful, inclusive, accessible, and within scope.

AI may help generate alternatives, simplify a draft, reorganize an explanation, check consistency, or adapt approved content for a format. A human reviewer must assess every output.

AI must never:

- invent evidence, citations, quotations, experience, or certainty;
- diagnose or profile a child or adult;
- exaggerate psychology to sound authoritative;
- use generic empathy as a substitute for practical help;
- become cold, clinical, robotic, or falsely intimate;
- introduce fear, guilt, urgency, or promotional pressure;
- flatten cultural and individual differences;
- claim to understand feelings or possess lived experience;
- approve its own work for publication.

Prompts should identify audience, context, emotional state, purpose, evidence boundary, format, and applicable handbooks. Reviewers should check whether the draft merely contains voice adjectives or actually expresses them through choices.

**Generic AI:** Navigating your child's big emotions can be an incredibly challenging journey, but remember that you are not alone!

**Healthy Little Minds:** Strong emotions can be difficult for both children and adults. Begin with safety and connection. When everyone is more settled, you can return to what happened and practise a next step together.

Human accountability cannot be delegated. Do not enter confidential or identifying information into an unapproved AI system.

## Voice Checklist

### Identity

- [ ] The writing sounds calm, warm, human, respectful, and practical.
- [ ] Curiosity appears before unsupported conclusions.
- [ ] Encouragement is grounded rather than automatic.
- [ ] Hope does not become a promise.
- [ ] Professionalism comes from care and precision, not academic language.

### Emotional responsibility

- [ ] The tone matches the reader's likely emotional state.
- [ ] The message supports orientation and agency rather than intensifying emotion.
- [ ] Difficult feelings are validated without exaggeration.
- [ ] Boundaries remain clear and respectful.
- [ ] There is no fear, shame, guilt, false urgency, sarcasm, or manipulation.

### Audience and context

- [ ] Vocabulary, detail, rhythm, and examples suit the audience.
- [ ] Child-friendly language remains accurate and dignified.
- [ ] Parent content avoids blame and perfectionism.
- [ ] Teacher content respects expertise and constraints.
- [ ] Professional content remains clear and usable.
- [ ] Channel conventions have not changed the core identity.

### Accuracy and education

- [ ] Claims are evidence-informed and uncertainty is visible.
- [ ] Observation, interpretation, evidence, and opinion are distinguished.
- [ ] The writing does not diagnose or present education as therapy.
- [ ] Examples model the principle in realistic language.
- [ ] The reader has a clear, manageable next step.
- [ ] Limits and routes to further support are stated where needed.

## Definition of Done

A piece of writing is complete only when it sounds recognizably like Healthy Little Minds even if the logo, colours, illustrations, and product name are removed.

That means the writing:

- remains calm without becoming distant;
- feels warm without manufacturing intimacy;
- respects the reader without avoiding clear guidance;
- validates emotions without exaggerating or diagnosing them;
- explains evidence without performing expertise;
- encourages action without pressure;
- offers hope without promises;
- adapts to audience and channel without losing its identity;
- helps the reader feel more informed, respected, and capable.

A grammatically correct message is not done if it is alarming, patronizing, vague, manipulative, inaccessible, or emotionally careless. A friendly message is not done if it is inaccurate. A practical message is not done if it sacrifices dignity.

Any unresolved issue involving misinformation, safeguarding, emotional manipulation, accessibility, privacy, discrimination, or unsupported psychological claims blocks publication. Reduce scope or revise the message rather than lower the standard.

## Closing Statement

Trust is built one sentence at a time. The Healthy Little Minds voice should consistently help people feel informed, respected, and capable—not judged, pressured, or overwhelmed.
