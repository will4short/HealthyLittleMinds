# Healthy Little Minds Psychology Editorial Standard

## Purpose

This document is the definitive editorial standard for every psychological, developmental, educational, and mental health claim published by Healthy Little Minds. It governs how knowledge is found, evaluated, interpreted, translated, reviewed, published, corrected, and retired.

Healthy Little Minds exists to improve psychological literacy. Psychological literacy means more than knowing terms. It means understanding emotions, behaviour, development, relationships, and evidence well enough to make wiser everyday decisions while recognizing the limits of general information.

The platform educates. It does not diagnose. It does not treat. It does not provide crisis intervention, medical advice, legal advice, or individualized clinical judgment. Its responsibility is to make evidence-informed psychology understandable and useful without presenting education as professional care.

This boundary protects readers and the integrity of the project. A clear article may help a parent notice a pattern, a teacher adapt a classroom routine, or a child find words for a feeling. It cannot determine why one person is struggling, rule out a condition, assess risk, or prescribe treatment. Those tasks require appropriate professionals, context, consent, and accountability.

All contributors share responsibility for the claims they publish. A claim may appear in a heading, illustration, caption, story, interface prompt, podcast conversation, social post, marketing sentence, worksheet instruction, or AI-generated summary. Short formats do not receive a lower truth standard.

This handbook complements the existing governance documents. It does not restate their general writing, voice, design, or accessibility rules. It supplies the specialist standard for psychology content. When psychology accuracy, safety, or scope conflicts with engagement, brevity, visual appeal, or publishing speed, accuracy and safety take precedence.

## Editorial Philosophy

### Evidence before opinion

Recommendations and explanations should begin with the best available relevant evidence, not a contributor's preference, a popular framework, an attractive metaphor, or a compelling anecdote. Professional and lived experience can add context, reveal implementation challenges, and identify questions. They should not be presented as proof of general effectiveness.

Evidence must match the claim. A study showing an association cannot support a causal statement. Research involving adults cannot automatically support claims about children. A short-term laboratory effect cannot establish lasting classroom impact. A citation is not decoration; reviewers must be able to trace each important claim to an appropriate source.

Evidence before opinion does not mean research alone determines practice. Responsible education integrates research with developmental fit, culture, circumstances, professional expertise, feasibility, and the perspectives of affected people. The editorial task is to distinguish these inputs rather than blend them into false certainty.

### Humility before certainty

Psychology studies complex people in changing environments. Findings are probabilistic, measures are imperfect, and individual outcomes vary. Healthy Little Minds should communicate confidence in proportion to what is known.

Humility means stating limitations, naming credible alternatives, and correcting errors. It does not mean making every sentence vague. Strong evidence can support clear guidance; immediate safety procedures may require direct instructions. The objective is calibrated confidence, not universal caution.

Writers should resist certainty that makes content sound authoritative but less true. “May,” “can,” “is associated with,” and “evidence suggests” are useful when they express a real condition. They should not be added mechanically to conceal weak reasoning.

### Practicality before complexity

Readers need knowledge they can use. Explain enough theory to support sound decisions, then connect it to observation, language, routines, adaptation, or help-seeking. Detail belongs where it changes interpretation or action.

Practicality is not simplification at any cost. A memorable instruction is harmful if it erases context or turns a possibility into a rule. When a concept cannot be reduced responsibly, use layered explanation: a plain-language summary, a worked example, key limitations, and optional detail.

### Context before simplification

Emotions and behaviour occur within relationships, development, culture, environments, disability, stress, resources, and history. Context should be considered before a single explanation is offered.

Writers should ask who was studied, where, under what conditions, using which measures, and compared with what. They should also ask what happens before and after the behaviour, what demands are present, and what the person may be communicating. Context prevents ordinary variation from becoming pathology and group averages from becoming predictions about individuals.

### Safety before engagement

No headline, hook, story, image, notification, or call to action may heighten fear, shame, or diagnostic concern to gain attention. Sensitive content should be discoverable without becoming sensational. Necessary warnings should be specific and proportionate.

Engagement metrics cannot justify overstated risk, simplified trauma narratives, symptom quizzes, or alarming claims about parenting and brain development. When a truthful qualification makes a message less shareable, retain the qualification.

### Education before diagnosis

Healthy Little Minds explains patterns, concepts, skills, and possible routes to support. It does not determine whether an individual meets diagnostic criteria. Descriptions should help readers observe and understand without encouraging self-diagnosis or diagnosis of children, relatives, students, colleagues, or public figures.

Educational content may explain what a diagnosis generally means and why assessment can help. It should identify overlap, variation, and alternative explanations. It should never suggest that a checklist, story, social post, or interactive tool can replace a qualified assessment.

### People before labels

Labels may help people access support, describe identity, or share experience. They must not replace the person. Avoid defining a child by behaviour, diagnosis, difficulty, or presumed motive.

Use community-informed terminology and respect individual preferences, including differences between person-first and identity-first language. Describe support needs and barriers precisely. Do not assume that people sharing a diagnosis share one personality, learning style, ability, or future.

## Scope

Healthy Little Minds provides general education in areas relevant to children's emotional wellbeing and the adults who support them.

### Within scope

#### Emotional literacy

Content may help people notice, name, differentiate, express, and discuss emotions. It should present emotion words as tools, not tests, and should acknowledge cultural and individual differences in experience and expression.

#### Child development

Content may explain broad developmental patterns, variation, environmental influences, and developmentally appropriate support. Age ranges are guides rather than deadlines.

#### Parenting education

Content may explain relationships, communication, routines, boundaries, co-regulation, modelling, play, and help-seeking. It should support judgment rather than prescribe one correct family practice.

#### Classroom psychology

Content may translate learning, motivation, attention, relationships, behaviour, and emotional development into classroom practice. It should respect educator expertise and real conditions.

#### Behaviour support

Content may help readers observe behaviour, consider multiple functions or influences, modify environments, teach skills, maintain boundaries, and seek appropriate support. Possible explanations must remain hypotheses unless properly assessed.

#### Social-emotional learning

Content may support intentional teaching and practice of emotional, interpersonal, and decision-making skills. It should not equate competence with quiet compliance or erase cultural differences.

#### Communication

Content may model listening, emotion language, conflict repair, collaborative problem-solving, and respectful limits. Scripts are examples, not formulas.

#### Emotional regulation

Content may explain how people notice and respond to emotional activation, use support, and develop strategies over time. Regulation should not be defined as constant calm or suppression.

#### Resilience

Content may discuss adaptation, relationships, resources, meaning, skills, and systems that support coping. It must not use resilience to place responsibility for adversity solely on individuals.

### Outside scope

Healthy Little Minds does not provide:

- clinical diagnosis or differential diagnosis;
- individualized psychological assessment;
- psychotherapy or treatment protocols;
- medical advice, medication guidance, or interpretation of physical symptoms;
- emergency, crisis, or suicide intervention;
- legal, safeguarding, or regulatory advice for an individual case;
- forensic opinions, risk predictions, or custody recommendations;
- personalized recommendations based on sensitive profiling;
- claims that a platform activity prevents, cures, or treats a condition.

Content touching these areas should explain the educational boundary and direct readers to appropriate qualified or emergency support. Signposting must be relevant to the user's region and periodically verified. A disclaimer does not make otherwise unsafe content acceptable.

## Evidence Standards

### Evidence hierarchy

Evidence quality depends on the question, not only study design. A useful working hierarchy is:

1. High-quality systematic reviews and meta-analyses of relevant studies.
2. Evidence-based guidelines from accountable professional or public bodies.
3. Multiple well-designed, replicated peer-reviewed studies.
4. Strong individual studies appropriate to the claim.
5. Established developmental, clinical, or educational psychology textbooks that synthesize evidence.
6. Consensus statements and transparent reviews by major psychology, health, education, or government organizations.
7. Well-conducted qualitative research for experience, meaning, context, and implementation.
8. Expert interpretation and professional experience.
9. Lived experience, case examples, and anecdotal reports.

Lower placement does not mean valueless. Qualitative research may be the best evidence for how a practice is experienced. Lived experience may reveal harms missed by trials. The hierarchy indicates what each source can support, not whose knowledge matters.

### Acceptable sources

Preferred sources include peer-reviewed journals; systematic reviews; meta-analyses; professional guidelines; transparent government guidance; publications from major accountable psychology, health, child-development, and education organizations; and established developmental or educational psychology texts.

Sources should be assessed for authorship, methods, sample, measures, comparison, effect size, uncertainty, relevance, recency, conflicts of interest, funding, and consistency with the broader field. Publication in a journal or association with a university is not sufficient by itself.

### Evidence matching

The evidence should match:

- the population and developmental stage;
- the outcome being claimed;
- the setting and culture;
- the intervention or exposure;
- the time period;
- the strength and direction of the wording.

For example, evidence that an activity improves children's emotion-word recognition immediately after a lesson does not establish lasting wellbeing, behaviour, or academic outcomes.

### Systematic reviews and meta-analyses

These sources can offer a broad view, but their quality varies. Reviewers should examine search methods, inclusion criteria, study quality, heterogeneity, publication bias, and whether pooled studies are sufficiently comparable. A precise pooled estimate does not repair weak underlying research.

### Professional and government guidance

Guidance can support practice and safety statements when the issuing body is accountable, methods are transparent, and jurisdiction is relevant. Guidance may reflect policy, feasibility, ethics, and evidence together. Do not present a policy recommendation as a universal scientific fact.

### Textbooks

Textbooks are useful for established concepts and background synthesis. Use recent, reputable editions and verify fast-changing or contested claims against current primary or review literature. Textbooks should not be the only source for important intervention claims.

### Weaker evidence

Single small studies, non-peer-reviewed reports, preprints, conference abstracts, commercial white papers, surveys with unclear sampling, testimonials, expert commentary, and anecdotes require caution. They may identify emerging questions but rarely justify broad recommendations.

Popular articles, podcasts, social posts, and AI summaries are discovery tools, not final evidence. Trace claims to original sources. Never cite a secondary source merely because the original is inconvenient to access.

### Conflicting evidence

When credible studies disagree:

- define the exact question and outcome;
- compare populations, settings, methods, measures, and duration;
- examine study quality and sources of bias;
- identify whether results differ in magnitude, direction, or interpretation;
- seek current systematic reviews or guidelines;
- state the disagreement clearly;
- avoid selecting only evidence that supports the preferred conclusion.

If uncertainty materially affects action, recommend the lowest-risk proportionate option and explain why. Sometimes the responsible conclusion is that evidence is insufficient for a recommendation.

### Recency and review

Newer is not automatically better, and older evidence is not automatically obsolete. Foundational findings may remain useful; clinical guidance and contested topics may change quickly. Each resource requires a review date based on risk, evidence stability, and likely change.

### Citation integrity

Every material claim should be traceable. Citations must support the sentence attached to them. Verify quotations against the original. Record full source details and access dates where relevant. Retracted, corrected, or superseded work must not remain in use without explicit justification.

## Translating Research

Research translation is an act of interpretation. The goal is not to remove complexity but to reveal the part of it readers need.

### Begin with the public question

Define the decision the reader is trying to make. Then identify which evidence can inform it. Do not start with an interesting study and manufacture a problem around it.

### Preserve the claim's boundaries

State who was studied, what was measured, what comparison was made, and how long outcomes were followed when those details affect meaning. Avoid turning “was associated with” into “caused,” “participants reported” into “proved,” or “on average” into “for every child.”

### Explain uncertainty

Translate confidence intervals, inconsistent findings, indirect evidence, and methodological limits into ordinary language. Do not remove every qualification to improve flow.

**Research language:** The confidence interval included small or negligible effects.

**Public education:** The studies do not show clearly whether the effect is meaningful in practice.

### Translate statistics responsibly

Use absolute numbers alongside percentages where possible. Give the denominator and time period. Explain baseline risk. Avoid relative-risk framing that makes small differences appear large.

**Misleading:** The programme doubled improvement.

**Responsible:** Improvement was reported by 4 in 100 children without the programme and 8 in 100 with it during the study period. The research does not tell us whether the change lasts.

Do not report precise numbers when the underlying estimate is uncertain. Avoid statistics without source, population, date, or definition.

### Use realistic examples

Examples should show how a finding may inform a choice while preserving alternatives. Do not create a fictional case that appears to prove the research. State when examples are composites.

### Avoid sensationalization

Do not describe preliminary work as a breakthrough, a correlation as a hidden cause, or a small effect as transformation. Brain-based findings require particular care: imaging does not automatically explain behaviour or establish an educational method.

### Separate mechanism from metaphor

Metaphors can aid memory, but readers should not mistake them for literal biology. “Upstairs brain,” “emotional brain,” “reptilian brain,” and similar models can misrepresent distributed, interacting processes. If a metaphor is used, state what it helps explain and where it stops being accurate.

### From finding to recommendation

Before turning a finding into advice, ask:

- Is the evidence causal and applicable?
- Is the proposed action similar to what was studied?
- Are benefits meaningful and harms considered?
- Is the action feasible and culturally appropriate?
- Does it require trained delivery?
- Is a less burdensome option available?

Research can be accurate while the resulting recommendation is still unjustified.

## Developmental Psychology

Development is shaped by biology, relationships, learning, culture, opportunities, adversity, disability, health, and systems. It is uneven within and between people.

### Early childhood

Emphasize relationships, co-regulation, play, sensory and physical experience, language development, imitation, repetition, and predictable routines. Young children often need adults to structure choices and provide regulation support.

Avoid expecting verbal insight, impulse control, perspective-taking, or independent coping beyond developmental capacity. Do not treat common variation as disorder or describe ordinary dependency as manipulation.

### Middle childhood

Children may develop broader emotion vocabulary, growing perspective-taking, rule understanding, and capacity to reflect. Abilities remain context-dependent and uneven. Peer relationships, school demands, identity, and competence become increasingly important.

Do not assume that a child who can explain a skill while calm can use it during distress. Teaching should include modelling, rehearsal, environmental support, and repair.

### Adolescence

Adolescents deserve language that respects autonomy, privacy, identity development, and increasing reasoning capacity. Content should consider peer relationships, belonging, sleep, academic pressure, digital life, family change, and cultural expectations.

Avoid stereotypes that portray adolescents as irrational, hormonal, risky, or incomplete adults. Developmental neuroscience must not be used to dismiss young people's reasoning or remove their agency.

### Parents and caregivers

Adult developmental history, stress, culture, resources, mental health, and relationships may influence caregiving. Content can support reflection without diagnosing adults or treating parenting style as a fixed identity.

Do not imply that one interaction permanently determines attachment or development. Emphasize patterns, repair, available support, and broader context.

### Teachers

Developmental guidance should help educators set realistic expectations and design support. It should not encourage teachers to diagnose or assign fixed ability. State how age, language, disability, culture, previous opportunity, and classroom conditions may change what is observed.

### Milestones and age ranges

Milestones are broad reference points, not deadlines or tests of worth. State the source, population, range, and intended use. Avoid rigid tables that invite diagnosis. When a pattern may warrant professional discussion, describe the functional concern and appropriate route without alarming language.

## Mental Health Communication

Mental health topics require accurate terminology, proportionate scope, non-stigmatizing language, and clear support boundaries.

### Anxiety

Distinguish normal fear and worry from an anxiety disorder. Describe persistence, intensity, context, avoidance, distress, and interference rather than using one symptom as proof. Avoid telling readers simply to avoid anxiety or force exposure. Educational suggestions should be gradual, safe, and not presented as treatment.

### Depression

Do not use “depressed” as a synonym for temporary sadness. Depression involves a pattern assessed over time and may include emotional, cognitive, behavioural, and physical changes. Content discussing warning signs, self-harm, or suicide requires qualified review and verified regional support information.

Avoid false reassurance, motivational slogans, and advice suggesting that gratitude, exercise, or positive thinking alone treats depression.

### Trauma

Use trauma carefully. A difficult or stressful experience is not automatically trauma, and behaviour alone cannot establish a trauma history. Trauma-informed educational practice can emphasize safety, predictability, choice, relationships, and avoidance of re-traumatization without asking educators to identify or treat trauma.

Do not invite public disclosure or encourage untrained investigation. Follow safeguarding procedures when a child discloses harm.

### ADHD

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition requiring qualified assessment across context and time. Avoid reducing it to distraction, high energy, poor discipline, screen use, or one classroom behaviour. Discuss strengths without romanticizing the condition or minimizing impairment.

Educational content may explain supportive environments, clear instruction, movement, task structure, and reasonable adjustments. It must not determine whether a child has ADHD or recommend medication decisions.

### Autism

Use respectful, community-aware language and recognize preferences for identity-first and person-first wording. Autism is not a behaviour style, epidemic, or puzzle to solve. Avoid deficit-only descriptions, fixed stereotypes, functioning labels, and claims that one intervention suits all autistic people.

Content may discuss communication access, sensory needs, predictability, interests, autonomy, and inclusive environments. Do not promote cures or imply that appearing non-autistic is the goal.

### Behaviour

Describe observable behaviour before interpretation. Consider communication, skill, environment, relationships, sensory needs, learning history, development, distress, and safety. “Attention-seeking,” “manipulative,” “lazy,” and “defiant” often conceal assumptions.

Understanding context does not remove boundaries. Guidance should combine dignity, safety, skill teaching, environmental adaptation, and proportionate consequences where appropriate.

### Emotional regulation

Regulation is not permanent calm, obedience, or suppression. It includes noticing, tolerating, expressing, influencing, and recovering from emotional states with varying levels of support. Co-regulation is relational support, not adult control.

Avoid universal strategy lists. Breathing, movement, sensory input, language, solitude, or connection may help different people in different moments.

### Stress and burnout

Stress is a response to demands and context, not automatically a disorder. Burnout should not be used casually for ordinary tiredness or as a diagnosis outside its accepted scope. Content should consider workload, control, support, resources, discrimination, and systems—not place all responsibility on individual resilience.

### Attachment

Attachment theory concerns patterns of relationship and protection; it should not be reduced to parenting types, online quizzes, or deterministic labels. Avoid diagnosing attachment styles from isolated behaviour or using attachment language to blame caregivers.

Explain that relationships can change and repair matters. Clinical attachment disorders are distinct from popular attachment-style discussions and require professional assessment.

### Referral language

Recommend professional discussion when distress is persistent, severe, escalating, associated with risk, or interfering substantially with daily life; when development or functioning raises sustained concern; or when general strategies are not enough. Name the relevant professional or service category where possible and acknowledge regional variation.

Emergency wording should be reserved for immediate danger and direct people to verified local emergency or crisis support. Healthy Little Minds must not attempt to manage a crisis through general content.

## Diagnostic Language

Healthy Little Minds does not diagnose readers or people described by readers.

Avoid:

- “You have anxiety.”
- “This means your child is autistic.”
- “Your student is attention-seeking.”
- “These are signs of trauma.”
- “Take this quiz to discover your attachment style.”
- “A narcissistic parent will always…”

Prefer:

- “Some children may experience…”
- “This behaviour can have many possible explanations…”
- “One possibility is…, but more context is needed.”
- “It may be worth discussing this with a qualified professional if…”
- “A professional assessment considers patterns across time, settings, development, and daily impact.”

Symptom descriptions must include overlap and alternatives where relevant. Do not create checklist content that implies a threshold is diagnostic. If validated screening is ever discussed, explain its purpose, limits, population, scoring governance, and need for professional interpretation.

Do not diagnose public figures, fictional characters presented as real examples, podcast guests, or people mentioned in news stories. Do not invite users to diagnose family members or students.

Headings, metadata, search snippets, images, and social captions must preserve the same boundaries as body text. “Five signs your child has ADHD” is unacceptable even if the article later adds a disclaimer.

## Uncertainty

Psychology evolves through replication, criticism, improved measurement, new populations, and revised theory. Responsible communication shows what is known, what remains uncertain, and why that matters.

### Confidence language

Use language proportionately:

- **High confidence:** “A substantial body of evidence supports…”
- **Moderate confidence:** “Evidence generally suggests…”
- **Limited confidence:** “Some studies suggest, but findings are limited…”
- **Unclear:** “Current evidence does not establish whether…”
- **Emerging:** “Researchers are exploring…”

These labels require judgment and should be supported internally, not selected for tone.

### Limitations

State limitations that could change interpretation: small or unrepresentative samples, short follow-up, self-report measures, high dropout, lack of control group, inconsistent definitions, unblinded ratings, conflicts of interest, indirect populations, or uncertain clinical significance.

Do not list limitations ritualistically. Explain their consequence: “Because families chose whether to join, we cannot know whether the programme caused the difference.”

### Ongoing debate

Represent credible disagreement fairly. Avoid false balance when one view lacks meaningful support, and avoid presenting consensus where none exists. Name which part is debated: definition, mechanism, measurement, effect size, implementation, or ethics.

### Individual differences

Group research estimates tendencies; it does not predict one person's response. Include alternatives and adaptation guidance. Avoid personality typing and fixed learning-style claims that turn variation into categories unsupported by evidence.

### Cultural differences

State when evidence is concentrated in particular countries, languages, school systems, income groups, ethnic groups, or family structures. Do not assume that a translated measure or intervention has the same meaning and effect elsewhere.

### Updating confidence

When evidence changes, revise the conclusion and explain material changes. Updating is responsible science, not inconsistency. Archive decision records so contributors understand why wording changed.

## Harm Reduction

Editorial review should identify foreseeable harm before publication and monitor harm afterward.

### Stigma

Avoid language that portrays mental health conditions, disability, or neurodivergence as frightening, burdensome, tragic, or morally suspect. Do not equate diagnoses with violence, unreliability, or inability to learn and relate.

### Blame

Do not blame children for skills they have not learned, families for conditions beyond their control, or educators for systemic shortages. Accountability can be described without reducing complex outcomes to one person's fault.

### Fear and shame

Never imply that ordinary mistakes permanently damage a child. Avoid before-and-after narratives that shame readers into compliance. Safety messages should specify the risk and response rather than create generalized alarm.

### Misinformation

Common myths should be corrected without repeating them so prominently that they become more memorable than the correction. Lead with accurate information, then briefly explain the misconception.

### Overconfidence

Overconfident advice can delay assessment, create unrealistic expectations, and encourage harmful implementation. Match recommendations to evidence and specify when professional delivery or supervision is required.

### False reassurance

Do not promise that an issue is normal, harmless, or temporary without enough context. Use conditional reassurance: “Many children experience brief worries; seek support if worry persists, intensifies, or limits daily life.”

### Privacy and disclosure

Do not ask children or adults to disclose sensitive experiences publicly or through unsecured tools. Case examples require consent or careful anonymization. AI systems must not receive identifiable personal data without an approved lawful process.

### Harm review questions

Before publication, ask:

- Could this lead someone to diagnose, delay care, or abandon appropriate care?
- Could it increase shame, stigma, conflict, coercion, or fear?
- Could a strategy be used as punishment or control?
- Could a child be pressured to disclose?
- Could cultural assumptions exclude or mislead?
- Could a headline be understood more strongly than the evidence?
- What happens if the advice fails?
- Who carries the burden of the recommended action?

High-risk concerns block publication until resolved.

## Inclusive Psychology

Psychological knowledge is shaped by who is studied, who defines outcomes, which measures are used, and which contexts receive resources.

### Culture

Emotion concepts, expression, independence, interdependence, eye contact, family roles, authority, help-seeking, and classroom participation vary. Avoid treating one cultural pattern as healthy by default. Local reviewers should assess meaning, feasibility, and unintended implications.

### Family diversity

Do not assume two biological parents, marriage, shared housing, stable contact, or one caregiving role. Use precise inclusive language and recognize extended family, foster care, adoption, guardianship, and community care where relevant.

### Language

Psychological terms do not always have direct equivalents. Translation should preserve function and meaning rather than English wording. Measures, emotion lists, and metaphors require cultural and linguistic validation; translation alone does not establish equivalence.

### Disability and neurodiversity

Consider communication, sensory access, cognition, mobility, fatigue, and assistive technology from the start. Do not define wellbeing solely by proximity to non-disabled behaviour. Include disabled and neurodivergent perspectives in review.

### School systems

Class size, curriculum, discipline policy, staffing, resources, referral routes, and teacher authority vary. Classroom recommendations must identify assumptions and offer adaptations. Do not export one system's terminology or legal requirements as universal.

### Generalization

Before generalizing a finding, compare the research population with the intended audience. State important gaps. Use “in the studies reviewed” rather than “children” when evidence concerns a narrow group.

Inclusion does not require claiming every principle is culturally relative. Child dignity, protection from harm, accessibility, and evidence integrity remain commitments. Cultural responsiveness requires dialogue and context, not avoidance of ethical judgment.

## Practical Application

Every psychology resource should answer:

> What can someone realistically do next?

Application should be proportional to the evidence. Strong evidence and low risk may support direct guidance. Limited evidence may support cautious options, observation, or questions for professional discussion rather than a recommendation.

A practical section should identify:

- the objective;
- who the action is for;
- the appropriate context;
- preparation, time, and materials;
- a small first step;
- language or examples where useful;
- choice and adaptation;
- what to observe;
- common mistakes;
- when to stop or seek help.

Avoid universal “hacks,” rigid scripts, and strategy lists without rationale. Explain function so users can adapt form responsibly. A technique that helps during mild frustration may be unsuitable during severe distress or immediate danger.

Application should not shift systemic problems onto individuals. Advice about teacher wellbeing should acknowledge workload and organizational conditions. Advice about family routines should not assume stable housing, flexible work, or financial resources.

When evidence cannot support an action, say so. Improving understanding, reducing stigma, and helping readers form better questions can themselves be useful outcomes.

## Age Appropriateness

### Children

Use concrete, non-diagnostic language and one idea at a time. Explain emotions as experiences rather than identities. Avoid deterministic brain claims and forced personal disclosure. Include trusted-adult support and multiple ways to participate.

### Adolescents

Respect autonomy, privacy, identity, and growing capacity for complexity. Avoid childish visuals and dismissive accounts of adolescent reasoning. Provide credible choices and clear support pathways, especially for sensitive topics.

### Parents and caregivers

Connect explanation to daily observation and manageable action. Avoid perfectionism, blame, and deterministic attachment or brain-development claims. Distinguish normal variation from patterns worth discussing professionally.

### Teachers

Explain classroom relevance, developmental variation, environmental influence, reasonable adaptations, and scope. Teachers should not be positioned as diagnosticians or therapists. Guidance must work under realistic classroom conditions.

### General audiences

Define terms, avoid assumed expertise, and provide layered detail. Mixed-audience content should offer a plain-language summary and clear routes to child-, parent-, teacher-, or professional-specific guidance.

Age labels should never substitute for developmental review. Consider language, disability, culture, prior knowledge, emotional demand, and whether adult facilitation is required.

## AI Use

AI may assist with research organization, preliminary summaries, readability, draft structure, example generation, terminology comparison, and consistency checks. It is a tool, not an evidence source or reviewer.

AI may:

- organize notes from verified sources;
- compare findings across supplied papers;
- identify claims requiring citations;
- suggest plain-language alternatives;
- draft examples for human review;
- flag inconsistent scope or terminology;
- prepare structured evidence tables.

AI may never:

- invent evidence, statistics, quotations, references, or links;
- fabricate or complete missing citation details;
- overstate findings or hide uncertainty;
- diagnose, screen, profile, or predict individuals;
- replace psychology, educational, accessibility, or localization review;
- interpret confidential personal data without an approved process;
- approve its own output.

Contributors must open and verify original sources. Plausible citations are not verified citations. AI summaries may omit methods, reverse comparisons, merge separate findings, or invent consensus.

Prompts should provide the approved sources, audience, intended claim, format, scope, and governing standards. Outputs should be treated as untrusted drafts. Record material AI involvement according to project governance. Human review is mandatory, and accountability remains with the named owner and approvers.

## Review Process

Psychology content follows a traceable workflow:

**Research**

↓

**Outline**

↓

**Draft**

↓

**Psychology review**

↓

**Educational review**

↓

**Accessibility review**

↓

**Localization review**

↓

**Publication**

↓

**Periodic evidence review**

### Research

Define the public question, audience, scope, possible harms, and evidence needed. Search systematically enough for the risk and claim. Record sources, search dates, exclusions, limitations, conflicts, and unanswered questions.

### Outline

Map claims to sources before prose makes them harder to inspect. Identify the practical objective, key qualifications, scope boundary, support routes, and review needs. Remove claims that add interest without sufficient evidence.

### Draft

Write from the approved evidence map. Distinguish evidence, interpretation, example, and recommendation. Preserve limitations in headings, summaries, captions, metadata, and calls to action—not only body text.

### Psychology review

A reviewer with suitable subject knowledge checks definitions, evidence selection, claim strength, causal language, developmental fit, diagnostic boundaries, risk, citations, and recommendations. Higher-risk content requires appropriately qualified professional review.

The writer should not be the sole psychology approver. Disagreement should be documented and escalated to the accountable owner or an additional specialist.

### Educational review

An educator or audience specialist checks comprehension, relevance, developmental suitability, practicality, unintended interpretation, and whether examples work in real contexts. Scientific accuracy alone does not make content educationally useful.

### Accessibility review

Review cognitive load, structure, terminology, media alternatives, interaction, document accessibility, and support for varied communication and learning needs. Simplification must not introduce inaccuracy.

### Localization review

Review cultural assumptions, terminology, examples, measures, service pathways, and applicability of evidence. Qualified human reviewers approve each localization. High-risk content requires relevant local specialist input.

### Publication

Confirm approvals, version, owner, review date, sources, disclosures, links, support information, and consistency across formats. Verify that promotional copy represents the approved content accurately.

### Periodic evidence review

Review according to risk and evidence stability, and sooner after major guidelines, retractions, corrections, credible complaints, or emerging harm. Changes must propagate to translations, downloads, media, social posts, and related resources.

### Corrections and withdrawal

Provide a visible route for reporting concerns. Triage misinformation, safety, stigma, privacy, and accessibility issues urgently. Material corrections require a version record. Withdraw content when harm cannot be promptly mitigated or its evidence basis is no longer defensible.

## Examples

### Research summaries

**Poor:** Scientists have proven that mindfulness transforms children's brains.

**Better:** Research suggests mindfulness may help some children manage stress.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Some studies report small improvements in attention or stress after structured mindfulness activities. Results vary, and many studies are short or use different programmes. Mindfulness is one possible educational practice, not a treatment or a strategy that suits every child.

### Teacher advice

**Poor:** Ignore attention-seeking students so the behaviour stops.

**Better:** Avoid reinforcing disruptive behaviour with attention.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Describe what happens before and after the behaviour before deciding its purpose. The student may need connection, help, clarity, movement, or a more workable way to participate. Maintain necessary boundaries while teaching and noticing an appropriate way to ask for support.

### Parent guidance

**Poor:** Secure attachment requires parents to respond correctly every time.

**Better:** Consistent responses support secure attachment.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Children benefit from patterns of responsive, dependable care. No caregiver responds perfectly. Repairing after difficult moments and seeking support when patterns become hard to manage are also important parts of relationships.

### Will Talks

**Poor:** Our expert reveals the trauma response behind difficult behaviour.

**Better:** Our guest explains how trauma can influence behaviour.

**Healthy Little Minds:** This episode explores how adversity may influence behaviour and why behaviour alone cannot tell us that a child has experienced trauma. We discuss predictable, relationship-based classroom support and the limits of the teacher's role.

### Social media

**Poor:** Five signs your child has anxiety.

**Better:** Five behaviours that may be linked with anxiety.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Worry can appear in different ways, and the same behaviour can have many explanations. Here are five patterns worth noticing—especially when they persist, cause distress, or interfere with daily life—and when to consider professional support.

### Emotion pages

**Poor:** Anger is caused by the amygdala taking over.

**Better:** Anger activates the brain's threat response.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Anger may arise when something feels unfair, blocked, threatening, or painful. It can change attention, body sensations, and the urge to act. These responses involve interacting brain and body systems; no single brain area explains every experience of anger.

### Marketing

**Poor:** Prevent lifelong emotional problems with our essential worksheet.

**Better:** Help your child develop emotional skills with this worksheet.

**Healthy Little Minds:** This worksheet helps children practise noticing and naming possible body signals of emotion with a trusted adult. It is an educational activity, not an assessment or treatment tool.

### Diagnostic language

**Poor:** If your child avoids school, they have school anxiety.

**Better:** School avoidance can be a sign of anxiety.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Avoiding school can have many explanations, including anxiety, bullying, learning difficulty, sensory needs, illness, or events at home or school. Speak with the child and school without assuming one cause. Persistent or severe distress is worth discussing with an appropriate professional.

### Development

**Poor:** Children should control tantrums by age four.

**Better:** Most four-year-olds are developing greater emotional control.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Many children develop more ways to communicate and recover from strong feelings during the preschool years, but progress varies by child and context. Adults still play an important role in safety, co-regulation, language, and predictable routines.

### Statistics

**Poor:** The intervention reduced problems by 50%.

**Better:** Problems were 50% less common after the intervention.

**Healthy Little Minds:** During the study, the outcome occurred in 2 of every 100 children receiving the programme and 4 of every 100 in the comparison group. The absolute difference was 2 children in 100; longer-term effects are uncertain.

### Resilience

**Poor:** Resilient children bounce back from anything.

**Better:** Resilience helps children recover from difficulty.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Resilience describes adaptation in the presence of difficulty. It is supported by relationships, resources, opportunities, skills, and wider systems; it is not a fixed trait or a reason to expect children to manage adversity alone.

### Regulation activity

**Poor:** Deep breathing will calm every child.

**Better:** Encourage the child to take deep breaths.

**Healthy Little Minds:** Slow breathing helps some children feel more settled. Offer it as one option rather than a command. Movement, quiet, sensory support, or connection may work better for others.

## Editorial Checklist

### Scope and purpose

- [ ] The resource has a defined educational purpose and audience.
- [ ] It remains within Healthy Little Minds' scope.
- [ ] It does not diagnose, treat, prescribe, or provide crisis intervention.
- [ ] Clinical, medical, legal, safeguarding, and emergency boundaries are clear.
- [ ] Any support pathway is verified for the relevant region.

### Evidence

- [ ] Each material claim is supported by appropriate evidence.
- [ ] Original sources have been opened and verified.
- [ ] Evidence matches the population, setting, outcome, exposure, and time period.
- [ ] Study quality, relevance, recency, and conflicts have been considered.
- [ ] Systematic reviews and guidelines have been checked where available.
- [ ] Weaker evidence is identified and not used for strong claims.
- [ ] Conflicting evidence is represented fairly.
- [ ] Retracted, corrected, or superseded sources have been checked.
- [ ] Citations support the exact attached claim.

### Interpretation

- [ ] Association is not presented as causation.
- [ ] Group averages are not presented as individual predictions.
- [ ] Statistical significance is not confused with practical importance.
- [ ] Absolute numbers, denominators, and time periods are given where useful.
- [ ] Mechanism, metaphor, and established fact are distinguished.
- [ ] Findings are not generalized beyond the evidence.
- [ ] Recommendations follow logically from the findings.

### Uncertainty

- [ ] Confidence is proportionate and understandable.
- [ ] Important limitations and their consequences are explained.
- [ ] Ongoing debate is represented without false balance.
- [ ] Individual and cultural differences are considered.
- [ ] Unknowns are not concealed to improve engagement.

### Mental health and diagnosis

- [ ] Ordinary emotions are not pathologized.
- [ ] Diagnostic terms are accurate, necessary, and within scope.
- [ ] No reader, child, student, public figure, or fictional example is diagnosed.
- [ ] Symptom overlap and alternative explanations are acknowledged.
- [ ] Education is not described as therapy or treatment.
- [ ] Referral language is proportionate and actionable.
- [ ] High-risk topics have received qualified specialist review.

### Development and inclusion

- [ ] Developmental expectations allow meaningful variation.
- [ ] Milestones are not presented as rigid deadlines.
- [ ] Culture, language, disability, neurodiversity, family, resources, and school systems are considered.
- [ ] Community terminology and preferences have been reviewed.
- [ ] Examples avoid stereotypes, stigma, tokenism, and deficit-only framing.
- [ ] The research population is not treated as universal.

### Harm and ethics

- [ ] The content does not use fear, shame, blame, or sensationalism.
- [ ] Foreseeable misuse has been considered.
- [ ] Strategies cannot easily become coercion, punishment, or forced disclosure.
- [ ] False reassurance and overconfidence have been removed.
- [ ] Privacy and consent are protected.
- [ ] Benefits, burdens, risks, and alternatives are proportionate.

### Practical education

- [ ] The reader can identify a realistic next step.
- [ ] Application strength matches evidence strength.
- [ ] Context, preparation, adaptations, and limits are explained.
- [ ] The resource says what to notice and what to do if the suggestion does not help.
- [ ] Systemic problems are not placed solely on individuals.
- [ ] Examples support understanding without pretending to prove the claim.

### Accessibility and localization

- [ ] Technical terms are defined without loss of accuracy.
- [ ] Structure and language support cognitive accessibility.
- [ ] Visuals, audio, interactions, and downloads preserve the claim's qualifications.
- [ ] Translation notes identify terms and concepts without direct equivalents.
- [ ] Local evidence, services, norms, and school contexts are reviewed.
- [ ] Qualified human localization review is complete.

### AI and governance

- [ ] AI-assisted claims, summaries, citations, and examples have been independently verified.
- [ ] No confidential or identifiable data entered an unapproved system.
- [ ] Material AI involvement is documented where required.
- [ ] Psychology, educational, accessibility, and localization approvals are recorded.
- [ ] The resource has an owner, version, publication date, and review date.
- [ ] Correction, feedback, and withdrawal routes exist.

Any unresolved issue involving misinformation, diagnosis, safety, stigma, safeguarding, privacy, accessibility, or fabricated evidence blocks publication.

## Definition of Done

Psychological content is complete only when it is all of the following.

### Scientifically responsible

The evidence is appropriate, verified, interpreted accurately, and cited honestly. Claims reflect uncertainty, context, and limits. Recommendations do not exceed the findings.

### Educationally useful

The intended audience can understand why the information matters and how it relates to everyday decisions. Complexity is translated without becoming distortion.

### Emotionally safe

The content avoids stigma, fear, shame, blame, false reassurance, diagnostic suggestion, and manipulative engagement. It protects dignity and states support boundaries.

### Accessible

Language, structure, media, interactions, and documents work for varied cognitive, sensory, motor, language, and literacy needs. Accessibility review is complete.

### Localized

Source content is translation-ready, and localized versions preserve scientific meaning while adapting terminology, context, examples, and support pathways through qualified human review.

### Reviewed

Appropriate psychology, educational, accessibility, localization, safeguarding, and editorial reviewers have approved the current version. Decisions and unresolved limitations are documented.

### Actionable

Readers know what they can realistically do next, what to observe, how to adapt, and when general information is no longer enough. Action remains proportionate to evidence.

### Consistent with Healthy Little Minds values

The resource puts people before labels, safety before engagement, education before diagnosis, and evidence before opinion. It contributes to trust over the long term.

A disclaimer cannot compensate for content that fails these conditions. If the standard cannot yet be met, reduce scope, seek expertise, or delay publication.

## Future Evolution

Psychology is a living science. Concepts, measurements, evidence, professional guidance, cultural understanding, and ethical expectations change. Healthy Little Minds should expect to revise content rather than defend outdated wording for consistency's sake.

Review priorities should reflect potential harm, rate of change, audience reach, and dependence on contested evidence. Diagnostic and treatment-adjacent topics, crisis signposting, trauma, neurodevelopment, and high-reach resources generally require more frequent review than stable foundational explanations.

Monitor guideline changes, systematic reviews, replications, retractions, corrections, regulatory updates, professional debate, and feedback from affected communities. User reports of harm or exclusion deserve investigation even when existing evidence appears settled.

Version history should explain material changes. Corrections should reach every format and localization. Archived content should not appear current. When confidence changes, say so plainly.

The standard itself should evolve through documented proposals and appropriate expert, educational, accessibility, and community review. New tools may improve evidence discovery and maintenance, but automation must not replace accountable judgment.

Long-term trust depends less on never being wrong than on using disciplined methods, acknowledging limits, correcting errors, and continuing to learn.

## Closing Reflection

Healthy Little Minds exists to help people understand psychology—not to impress them with it.

Knowledge becomes valuable only when it helps children, parents, teachers, and communities make wiser, kinder, and more informed everyday decisions.
