# Healthy Little Minds Localization Guidelines

## Purpose

This document is the definitive localization and internationalization handbook for Healthy Little Minds. It governs how content and experiences are adapted across languages, cultures, educational systems, developmental expectations, regional services, and everyday contexts.

Localization is more than replacing words in one language with words in another. Translation transfers language. Localization preserves purpose and meaning while adapting the way that meaning is expressed and used. It asks whether an example feels familiar, whether a classroom routine is possible, whether a psychology term carries the intended implications, whether an interface is natural to navigate, and whether a family or teacher can act on the guidance in their context.

Every supported edition should feel native, respectful, and complete. Users should not feel that they are reading a secondary copy of an English product. At the same time, Healthy Little Minds must remain one recognizable platform with shared educational purpose, psychology standards, voice, accessibility, and product quality.

This handbook applies to the website, Emotion Library, Parents Hub, Teachers Hub, stories, Will Talks, worksheets, audiobooks, dashboards, interface content, emails, notifications, marketing, AI-assisted content, and future mobile applications. It applies from initial content planning through publication and maintenance.

Healthy Little Minds currently supports English, Traditional Chinese for Taiwan, Simplified Chinese, Japanese, and Korean. The system must also be prepared for future languages, including languages with different scripts, directionality, grammatical structures, cultural assumptions, and accessibility needs. New languages should not require the platform to be conceptually or structurally rebuilt.

This document complements existing editorial, psychology, voice, design, and accessibility standards. It does not lower or duplicate them. A localized resource must meet the same standard as its source while receiving the additional linguistic and cultural review necessary for its audience.

## Localization Philosophy

### Meaning before literal translation

The purpose and intended effect of a message matter more than matching its words in the same order. A literal sentence may be grammatically possible yet unnatural, ambiguous, overly formal, culturally unsuitable, or psychologically misleading.

Translators and reviewers should first identify:

- what the content means;
- what the reader needs to understand or do;
- which emotional effect is intended;
- which qualifications must remain;
- which terms have controlled meanings;
- which elements can be adapted.

Meaning before literal translation does not authorize invention. Psychology claims, safety information, consent conditions, and product capabilities must not change. The localized expression may differ, but its substance must remain traceable to the approved source.

### Educational effectiveness before surface equivalence

A translation succeeds when the intended audience can understand, remember, and use it. Surface equivalence—the closest possible word or structure—is not enough. Examples, instructions, reading level, classroom language, and adult-child dialogue must work in the target context.

Linguistic accuracy remains essential. This principle means that accuracy includes function, implication, and educational usefulness, not merely dictionary correspondence. When a direct equivalent is obscure, use a natural explanation and introduce the canonical term where readers need it.

### Cultural respect before familiarity

Localization should feel relevant without relying on stereotypes. Replacing names, foods, or holidays is not sufficient if the underlying family roles, school expectations, or assumptions about emotion remain foreign.

Cultural respect requires curiosity, local expertise, and willingness to retain difference. Not every concept needs to be made familiar by forcing it into a local analogy. Sometimes the respectful choice is to explain a less familiar idea clearly and allow the audience to evaluate it.

### Consistency before convenience

Canonical terms, navigation labels, recurring characters, content categories, warnings, and interface actions should remain consistent across a language edition. Translators should not choose a new equivalent each time because it sounds convenient in one sentence.

Consistency supports learning, search, accessibility, and trust. It does not require mechanical repetition where grammar or tone needs variation. Approved variants should be documented with context.

### Accessibility before perfection

Localization must preserve access from the beginning. A linguistically elegant sentence that is unnecessarily complex, impossible to resize, unreadable in captions, or unclear to a screen-reader user is not complete.

When constraints conflict, protect meaning, safety, and accessibility. Do not shrink text, remove labels, truncate qualifications, or publish images of translated text to preserve an original layout. Adjust the design.

### One platform, many cultures

Healthy Little Minds is one connected educational ecosystem. Shared principles should make movement between languages and products coherent. Cultural adaptation should make each edition locally useful without turning it into an unrelated product.

The stable core includes:

- child dignity and wellbeing;
- evidence-informed psychology;
- educational rather than diagnostic scope;
- calm, warm, respectful communication;
- practical action;
- accessibility and privacy;
- transparent uncertainty;
- reusable product patterns.

Examples, terminology, school references, family language, illustrations, services, formats, and delivery may change when context requires it.

## Translation vs Localization

Translation renders meaning from one language into another. Localization adapts the complete experience for a particular audience and region.

Translation asks, “What does this sentence say?” Localization also asks:

- Would people naturally say it this way?
- Does the example exist in this context?
- Does the school role have an equivalent?
- Does the term carry stigma or clinical meaning?
- Is the action practical and culturally appropriate?
- Does the interface fit local reading and input habits?
- Are support services and legal references correct?
- Does the content still sound like Healthy Little Minds?

### Example: family activity

**Source:** Talk about the day during the school run.

**Direct translation:** Preserves “school run” even where children commonly walk, cycle, use school transport, or travel independently.

**Localized meaning:** Talk during a regular transition, such as the journey home, after-school snack, or evening routine.

The educational principle—using an ordinary low-pressure moment—remains stable. The daily context changes.

### Example: classroom role

**Source:** Speak with the school counselor.

A literal translation may imply a role that does not exist, has a different title, or carries different responsibilities. Localization identifies an appropriate local role, such as a homeroom teacher, guidance teacher, school psychologist, pastoral lead, nurse, or regional support service. If no direct equivalent exists, explain the function rather than inventing one.

### Example: emotional language

**Source:** Name the feeling.

In some contexts, direct individual emotional disclosure may feel unfamiliar or too exposed. A localized activity might invite children to choose a character's possible feeling, describe a body signal, or discuss what someone in a story might need. The emotional-literacy objective remains; the participation route adapts.

### Why direct translation fails in psychology

Psychology terms carry histories, clinical associations, popular meanings, and cultural assumptions. “Regulation,” “resilience,” “attachment,” “trauma,” “wellbeing,” and “behaviour” may not map cleanly to one term. A direct equivalent may sound medical, moral, academic, or unnatural.

Educational language also depends on institutional context. Grade levels, discipline systems, lesson structures, parent-teacher relationships, and specialist roles vary. A perfect sentence about the wrong system remains unusable.

For this reason, localization begins during source creation. Writers should avoid idioms, unexplained institutions, wordplay, concatenated interface strings, culturally narrow examples, and layouts that assume English length and order.

## Languages

Language guidance identifies common considerations, not fixed traits of all speakers. Each edition still requires qualified language, cultural, educational, and psychology review.

### English

English is not culturally neutral. Healthy Little Minds may reach readers using British, Nigerian, Philippine, North American, and other varieties of English. The English source should use plain, internationally understandable language and document region-specific terms.

Avoid assuming that terms such as “reception,” “kindergarten,” “primary school,” “counselor,” “form tutor,” “special educational needs coordinator,” or “GP” have universal meaning. Use a general functional description where the audience is international, or identify the region clearly.

Use one approved spelling convention within a resource and its related assets. Do not allow automated tools to change quoted titles, proper nouns, or regional terms. Dates should avoid ambiguous numeric-only formats. Measurements, currency, emergency numbers, school phases, and services require regional handling.

English source content should be translation-ready. Prefer complete sentences, explicit subjects, clear pronoun references, and descriptive labels. Avoid phrasal verbs and idioms when a simpler equivalent exists. Do not depend on humour, rhyme, alliteration, or a pun for instructional meaning.

### Traditional Chinese (Taiwan)

Traditional Chinese for Taiwan requires more than script conversion. Use terminology, punctuation, educational roles, mental health language, and everyday expressions natural to Taiwan. Simplified-to-Traditional character conversion cannot produce a localized edition.

Review:

- Taiwan-specific usage and character choices;
- school stages, class roles, counseling and support structures;
- natural levels of formality between adults and children;
- full-width punctuation and appropriate spacing;
- line breaking that does not separate punctuation incorrectly;
- names, examples, routines, and services;
- whether psychology terminology is recognized, overly clinical, or stigmatizing.

Chinese may express meaning with fewer visible characters than English while still requiring greater conceptual explanation. Do not assume shorter text needs less space. Mixed Chinese and Latin text, numbers, URLs, abbreviations, and product names require visual review.

Avoid importing Mainland Chinese terms into Taiwan content without review. Where regional terminology differs, use the Taiwan-preferred term and document the mapping in the glossary. Traditional Chinese content must be reviewed by proficient users familiar with Taiwan, not only by someone able to read the script.

### Simplified Chinese

Simplified Chinese requires its own terminology, tone, educational references, and regional review. It is not Traditional Chinese with character substitution in either direction.

Review:

- established Simplified Chinese psychology and education terms;
- school roles, grade conventions, family-school communication, and support services;
- natural digital interface language;
- punctuation, line breaking, and mixed-script content;
- regional sensitivities and legal or service references;
- whether examples assume one city, income level, or school model.

The geographic audience for Simplified Chinese may extend beyond one jurisdiction. Regional claims and support pathways must state where they apply. If one localized edition serves multiple regions, use broadly understandable language and provide region-specific modules where necessary.

Automated script conversion is useful only as an internal comparison aid. It cannot replace translation, localization, or review. Identical-looking terms may carry different frequency, connotation, or professional use.

### Japanese

Japanese localization must account for script choice, register, sentence structure, educational conventions, and the relationship between speaker and audience. Hiragana, katakana, kanji, and Latin characters should be used according to natural contemporary practice and audience age.

Review:

- politeness and formality appropriate to the product and audience;
- whether child-facing kanji requires furigana or a simpler expression;
- school-year terminology, homeroom structures, club activities, and support roles;
- sentence length and information order;
- line breaks, punctuation, and prohibited line-start or line-end characters;
- words that sound clinical, imported, childish, or unusually direct;
- adult-child dialogue for naturalness and dignity.

Japanese can omit subjects that English states explicitly. Translators must restore or omit information naturally without making responsibility ambiguous. Interface labels should use familiar action language rather than literal English product terminology.

Direct emotional disclosure may require careful framing depending on context. Activities can use stories, observation, private reflection, or choices while preserving the emotional-learning objective. This is an adaptation option, not a claim that Japanese users share one communication style.

### Korean

Korean localization must address register, honorifics, sentence endings, spacing, educational terminology, and natural interaction between adults and children. Tone choices can change perceived warmth, authority, and distance.

Review:

- appropriate speech level for parents, teachers, professionals, and children;
- consistency of honorific and role terms;
- age and school-stage terminology;
- line breaking and word spacing;
- mixed Hangul, Latin text, numbers, and product names;
- psychology terms that may sound medical or stigmatizing;
- classroom practices and support pathways.

Do not vary register unpredictably across headings, body text, buttons, and notifications. Child-facing content should be warm and clear without becoming babyish. Teacher guidance should sound collegial, not commanding. Parent guidance should avoid language that suggests blame or loss of authority.

Korean text may expand differently from English and may wrap more frequently at narrow widths. Review actual components, downloads, captions, and mobile screens rather than approving text in a spreadsheet alone.

### Future languages

Each new language requires a written language profile before production begins. The profile should cover:

- language and regional scope;
- script and text direction;
- plural, gender, case, and inflection considerations;
- name, date, time, number, and address conventions;
- text expansion and line breaking;
- typography and available typeface support;
- input methods and search behaviour;
- educational system and common roles;
- psychology and mental health terminology;
- voice, register, and audience relationships;
- accessibility and assistive-technology support;
- local reviewers and ownership;
- legal, safeguarding, privacy, and support pathways.

Right-to-left languages, complex scripts, languages using grammatical gender, and languages with multiple regional standards must be supported through adaptable architecture rather than exceptions added after content is complete.

Do not add a language merely because machine translation is available. Launch requires sustainable human review, terminology management, QA, maintenance ownership, and enough localized pathways to provide a coherent experience.

## Cultural Adaptation

Cultural adaptation changes contextual elements while protecting the psychological and educational purpose.

### Examples

Examples should be understandable, plausible, and varied. Adapt transport, housing, weather, leisure, technology access, school schedules, and community settings where they affect use. Do not assume that every child has a private bedroom, garden, personal device, or quiet study area.

An example need not mirror the majority to be valid. The goal is not to erase difference but to avoid making one narrow life pattern the unmarked default.

### Stories

Stories may require adapted names, settings, school routines, dialogue, gestures, food, clothing, celebrations, and relationship expectations. Preserve character identity, plot function, emotional arc, and learning objective unless an approved localized adaptation changes them deliberately.

Do not replace surface details while retaining an implausible conflict or adult-child interaction. Story localization should involve a literary translator or editor where narrative voice, rhythm, humour, or read-aloud quality matters. Audiobook adaptations require spoken-language review and performance direction.

### School systems

Adapt grade labels, school phases, staff roles, class size assumptions, lesson schedules, discipline systems, referral processes, and family communication. When a resource is designed for multiple systems, describe the function: “the staff member responsible for student wellbeing” may be more portable than one job title.

Do not imply that a teacher can perform a role reserved locally for clinicians, counselors, or safeguarding leads.

### Family relationships

Use inclusive roles and avoid assuming household composition, marriage, biological relationships, or one caregiving hierarchy. Adapt forms of address and dialogue naturally while preserving children's dignity and caregiver partnership.

Cultural respect does not justify guidance that humiliates, threatens, or harms children. Where local norms and project principles create tension, seek qualified local and safeguarding review rather than silently weakening the standard.

### Holidays and celebrations

Use celebrations when they genuinely support the context, not as decorative cultural markers. Verify meaning, practice, timing, and diversity within a region. Provide neutral alternatives when a classroom activity should not require religious or cultural participation.

### Food

Adapt food examples for familiarity, allergies, dietary practices, affordability, and cultural meaning. Do not use one cuisine to signify foreignness. In sensory or emotion activities, ensure participation does not depend on access to a particular food.

### Names

Choose natural, varied names with local review. Do not match marginalised identities repeatedly with difficulty, poverty, or need. Names in recurring stories should remain consistent across products and audio pronunciation guides.

### Classroom practices

Adapt group discussion, public participation, eye contact, independent work, movement, seating, teacher address, and parent contact to local conditions. Preserve the purpose, such as increasing predictability or providing choice, even when the visible routine changes.

### Daily routines

Adapt meal times, school journeys, sleep arrangements, after-school care, religious practices, work schedules, and family responsibilities where relevant. Avoid suggesting that one routine is developmentally ideal without evidence and context.

### Adaptation record

Material adaptations should be documented with the source element, localized decision, reason, reviewer, and effect on related content. This protects consistency and allows local insight to improve future source content.

## Psychology Across Cultures

Psychological knowledge should be presented with cultural humility. Cultural humility means acknowledging the limits of one's perspective, examining power and assumptions, and involving people with relevant local knowledge.

### Emotions

Languages divide and describe emotional experience differently. Some emotion terms lack direct equivalents; others carry different intensity, social meaning, body associations, or moral judgment. Emotion lists should not be treated as universal taxonomies.

Preserve the educational function: helping people notice, communicate, understand, and respond. Allow multiple words, uncertainty, body descriptions, stories, and non-verbal options. Never infer an emotion solely from facial expression or culturally specific behaviour.

### Parenting

Parenting expectations differ around independence, obedience, sleeping, feeding, family hierarchy, discipline, emotional expression, and extended-family involvement. Do not position one culturally dominant model as the universal standard.

Translate evidence carefully. State where studies were conducted and distinguish principles—such as safety, dependable care, repair, and non-humiliating boundaries—from one prescribed style of interaction.

### Teacher authority

Teacher-student relationships and authority differ across systems. Guidance should respect the legitimate teaching role while preserving student dignity and participation. “Collaborative” should not be translated as absence of structure; “clear authority” should not become coercion.

### Discipline

Discipline language can carry punishment, teaching, social, or moral meanings. Define the intended principle. Healthy Little Minds supports guidance that teaches skills, maintains safety, repairs relationships, and uses proportionate boundaries. It does not normalize humiliation, fear, or violence because a practice is familiar.

### Communication styles

Directness, silence, disagreement, eye contact, public praise, emotional disclosure, and help-seeking vary. Adapt the route without assigning fixed traits to a culture. Offer private, written, story-based, or indirect reflection where public individual disclosure would undermine participation.

### Mental health language

Mental health terms may be medicalized, stigmatized, unfamiliar, or used differently in public speech. Use locally recognized professional terminology where precision is needed and plain explanation where it improves access. Do not avoid accurate terms solely because stigma exists; introduce them responsibly and without diagnosis.

### Universal principles and local expression

Evidence integrity, child dignity, safety, accessibility, non-diagnostic education, and respect remain stable. Their expression may vary. Local adaptation should neither impose one culture nor use cultural difference to excuse harm or unsupported claims.

When evidence comes mainly from another context, state that limitation. Local professional knowledge and community perspectives should inform whether the guidance is feasible and how uncertainty is communicated.

## Educational Systems

Resources should adapt to educational systems without fragmenting the whole product.

The source should define educational functions rather than rely only on institution-specific labels:

- learner age or developmental stage rather than grade alone;
- classroom teacher rather than a local title alone;
- designated wellbeing or safeguarding staff rather than one job name;
- small-group activity rather than a fixed lesson period;
- local professional support rather than one service brand.

Localization can then map these functions to regional terms and processes.

Adapt where necessary:

- school-entry age and phase structure;
- grade and year naming;
- academic calendar and daily timetable;
- class size and staffing;
- curriculum expectations;
- assessment practices;
- inclusion and special-education systems;
- discipline and safeguarding procedures;
- school counseling and psychology access;
- parent-school communication;
- technology and printing access.

Core learning objectives, psychology claims, safeguarding principles, and accessibility standards should not be rewritten casually. Use modular regional notes, examples, metadata, and support links instead of duplicating entire resources when only part differs.

Resources must not claim compliance with local policy without current qualified review. Legal and policy information requires jurisdiction, effective date, owner, and scheduled review.

## User Interface Localization

Interface localization is part of product design. Text should be reviewed inside the actual component and user journey.

### Buttons

Translate the action and outcome, not the English grammar. Use familiar concise verbs. Ensure adjacent buttons remain clearly distinct, particularly for confirmation and cancellation.

### Menus and navigation

Use canonical labels that match page titles and search terminology. Do not shorten labels until their destination becomes ambiguous. Navigation order may need review, but localization should not rearrange core information architecture without a documented usability reason.

### Forms

Adapt name, address, phone, date, time, and input expectations. Do not force all users into an English personal-name structure. Labels, helper text, required-state wording, consent, and privacy explanations must be complete in every language.

### Alerts and notifications

Preserve severity and action. A warning should not become alarming; an error should not sound like blame. Keep essential information in the message rather than relying on colour or an icon.

### Validation

Explain what needs correction using locally appropriate examples. Error rules must match accepted local input. Do not tell users their valid name, address, or phone number is invalid because the product model is too narrow.

### Empty states

Explain the state and an appropriate next step. Avoid humour that depends on wordplay or makes the user feel behind.

### Error messages

State what happened, whether work is safe, and how to recover. Technical codes may be included for support but should not replace explanation. Review tone in context; literal apologies may sound excessive or insufficient in different languages.

### Search

Search should recognize local terminology, spelling variation, scripts, character forms, spacing conventions, synonyms, and common input methods. Search result labels and no-result guidance must be localized. The terminology glossary should inform synonyms without collapsing distinct psychology concepts.

### Dashboard

Review headings, metrics, dates, empty states, filters, status terms, sorting, truncation, and chart labels. Data labels must not rely on abbreviation that is unfamiliar locally. Numbers and dates require unambiguous local formatting.

### Text expansion and flexibility

Design for expansion, contraction, and different break opportunities. Do not set English text length as the allowed maximum. Components should tolerate longer labels, multi-line buttons where appropriate, larger text, and mixed scripts without overlap or loss.

Avoid embedding text in images, concatenating translated fragments, or assuming words can be truncated at arbitrary character counts. Placeholders must not carry essential labels.

## Typography and Layout

The Typography and Layout System documents govern visual standards. Localization adds script-specific responsibilities.

### Text expansion

Allow enough space for translated strings, accessibility resizing, and natural phrasing. Short source strings can expand significantly because context must be made explicit. Test realistic maximums rather than ideal samples.

### Line breaking

Respect language-specific rules for punctuation, words, particles, and prohibited breaks. Automated wrapping must be visually reviewed in headings, buttons, cards, captions, tables, and printable materials.

### Vertical rhythm

Different scripts and typefaces have different perceived density and line-height needs. Preserve readability and hierarchy rather than forcing identical line counts. A localized page may be longer while remaining consistent.

### Mixed-language interfaces

Names, URLs, citations, acronyms, numbers, and product terms may introduce Latin characters into CJK text. Review baseline, spacing, weight, punctuation, and screen-reader pronunciation. Avoid unexplained language switching.

### Responsive layouts

Test narrow mobile widths, tablet, desktop, zoom, large text, and orientation changes for each language. A layout that works in English is not evidence that it works elsewhere. Content should reflow without horizontal scrolling except where essential, such as some data tables with an accessible alternative.

### Typeface support

Use typefaces with complete, high-quality glyph support for the language and intended weights. Avoid fallback mixtures that produce inconsistent characters. Typography should support learning and legibility rather than decorative regional styling.

## Illustrations

The Illustration Guidelines govern purpose, dignity, style, representation, and accessibility. Localization review determines whether a visual remains understandable and respectful.

Illustrations may require adaptation when meaning depends on:

- school uniforms, classroom layouts, or staff roles;
- gestures, body language, or eye contact;
- food, housing, transport, or weather;
- holidays, religious practices, or public symbols;
- family structure or forms of address;
- written signs, worksheets, phones, or books inside the image;
- colour or icon associations;
- safety practices or local services.

Illustrations can remain shared when the concept is clear, representation is inclusive, and local review identifies no misleading detail. Universality should not mean culturally blank imagery or one culture presented as neutral.

Do not adapt skin tone, clothing, or props as a superficial substitute for deeper contextual review. Recurring characters should retain identity across editions unless a deliberate regional adaptation has an approved character specification.

All text within illustrations should be externalized where possible. Translated images require accessible text alternatives and source files. AI-generated localized images receive human cultural, anatomy, representation, psychology, and accessibility review.

## Accessibility

Localization must preserve and, where possible, improve accessibility. The Accessibility Standard applies equally to every language.

### Screen readers

Set the correct page language and identify language changes within content. Use semantic structure and native controls. Test with screen readers commonly used by the target audience where feasible. Verify pronunciation of acronyms, mixed scripts, symbols, numbers, and interactive labels.

### Alt text

Translate the image's purpose in context rather than translating the source alt text mechanically. If the localized illustration changes, update the alternative accordingly. Decorative images should remain hidden from assistive technology.

### Captions and transcripts

Captions should be timed, readable, complete, and natural. Translate meaningful sound and speaker identification. Transcripts should preserve the spoken meaning while using readable structure. Dubbing or voice-over requires equivalent review and should not erase clinically or culturally important qualifications.

### Reading complexity

Plain language differs by language. Sentence length alone does not determine accessibility. Review vocabulary frequency, grammar, script difficulty, register, assumed knowledge, and audience age. Child-facing Japanese may require furigana; other languages may need explanatory wording or audio support.

### Printable resources

Test glyph rendering, line breaks, writing space, reading order, contrast, grayscale, print margins, answer fields, and ordinary local paper sizes. A translated worksheet may need a redesigned page count rather than smaller text.

### Cognitive and motor access

Keep instructions predictable and provide multiple means of participation. Input methods, keyboards, handwriting, character selection, and speech input differ. Do not impose time limits or interaction patterns that disadvantage a script or assistive technology.

Accessibility defects block localized publication even when the source version is accessible.

## AI Translation

AI may assist localization, but it is not an approver, cultural authority, or reliable source of canonical terminology.

AI may:

- produce a first translation draft from approved source content;
- compare source and target for omissions or inconsistent numbers;
- suggest terminology candidates for human decision;
- identify repeated strings and possible inconsistency;
- back-translate selected passages as one review aid;
- help prepare reviewer questions.

AI may never:

- publish content without proficient human review;
- decide cultural appropriateness on its own;
- invent local services, policies, evidence, citations, or professional titles;
- convert scripts and present the result as localization;
- change psychology claims or safety instructions silently;
- translate confidential personal information in an unapproved system;
- approve its own output.

Back-translation cannot prove quality. Two inaccurate translations may appear consistent. Human reviewers should assess the target as natural content in its own right and compare it with the approved source.

Prompts and source packages should include audience, region, context, glossary, tone, character limits, product location, non-translatable terms, and psychology or safeguarding notes. Generated output remains an untrusted draft until reviewed.

Material AI involvement should be recorded according to project governance. Human accountability remains with the localization owner and approvers.

## Terminology Management

Canonical terminology prevents conceptual drift across products and languages.

The terminology system should record:

- source term and definition;
- approved equivalent by language and region;
- part of speech and grammatical notes;
- audience and register;
- approved variants and prohibited terms;
- context and example sentence;
- psychology or accessibility notes;
- reviewer, approval date, and version;
- related search synonyms;
- terms intentionally retained in the source language.

### Emotion names

Define the intended emotion concept before choosing a word. Record differences in intensity, social meaning, and clinical association. Do not force one-to-one mappings where a language distinguishes or combines concepts differently. Search and cross-linking may support multiple natural terms.

### Psychology terms

Terms such as regulation, attachment, resilience, trauma, anxiety, depression, neurodiversity, and co-regulation require psychology review. Distinguish clinical use from public use. Definitions and qualifications must remain consistent across articles, stories, tools, and marketing.

### Teacher vocabulary

Map school phases, staff roles, classroom routines, inclusion terms, and support processes by region. Use the term educators actually recognize while documenting the source function.

### Parent vocabulary

Prefer natural, respectful language over institutional or clinical wording. Record inclusive equivalents for parent, caregiver, guardian, family, and trusted adult. Avoid variants that assign blame or one family structure.

### UI labels

Buttons, states, permissions, privacy choices, account terms, downloads, and errors need controlled labels. The same action should not receive unrelated translations across products.

### Navigation

Primary destination names should remain stable and match headings, search, breadcrumbs, and page metadata. Changes require product and localization review because they affect discoverability.

Terminology disagreements should be resolved through evidence of usage, audience testing, subject expertise, and documented decisions—not seniority or personal preference. Glossaries are living resources and should be updated with version control.

## Review Workflow

Localization follows a traceable workflow:

**Source Content**

↓

**Translation**

↓

**Cultural Review**

↓

**Psychology Review**

↓

**Accessibility Review**

↓

**Quality Assurance**

↓

**Publication**

↓

**Periodic Review**

### Source content

The source must be approved, stable, structured, and localization-ready. Supply metadata, glossary terms, audience, context, screenshots, character constraints, links, asset list, evidence notes, pronunciation, and identified cultural assumptions. Do not localize a moving draft without clear version control.

### Translation

A proficient translator works from the package and records questions or adaptations. Psychology, child-facing, literary, and interface content should be assigned to translators with relevant experience. Translation memory may support consistency but must not override current context.

### Cultural review

A reviewer familiar with the target region assesses naturalness, examples, relationships, school context, stigma, services, representation, and likely interpretation. This is a substantive review, not proofreading.

### Psychology review

A qualified subject reviewer verifies that claims, diagnostic boundaries, uncertainty, emotional concepts, developmental meaning, and practical guidance remain intact. High-risk localized content requires relevant professional expertise.

### Accessibility review

Review structure, language complexity, typography, alt text, captions, transcripts, documents, input, interaction, and assistive-technology behaviour in the target language.

### Quality assurance

Test the complete experience, not only strings. Verify links, truncation, wrapping, sorting, search, forms, numbers, dates, downloads, audio, captions, screen-reader language, metadata, responsive layout, and fallback states. Use linguistic and functional QA with real devices where possible.

### Publication

Confirm approvals, source version, localized version, owner, dates, language metadata, indexing, alternate-language links, support pathways, and rollback plan. Do not publish isolated navigation paths that lead mostly to untranslated content without clear labels.

### Periodic review

Review after source changes, terminology decisions, evidence updates, policy or service changes, user feedback, and scheduled intervals. Localized editions should not silently fall behind. Track parity and prioritize safety-critical differences.

### Corrections

Local users need an accessible route to report errors in their language. Triage errors by risk. Psychology, safeguarding, privacy, and action-changing errors require urgent review and propagation across related assets.

## Examples

The examples below use English descriptions to show the decision pattern. Actual target-language wording requires qualified review.

### Emotion pages

**Literal translation:** Select the exact emotion shown by this face.

**Localized translation:** Choose the emotion that best matches the face.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** What might this person be feeling? Choose one possibility or add your own. A face can have more than one explanation.

The standard corrects the psychological assumption rather than translating it.

### Teacher resources

**Literal translation:** Ask the school counselor to observe the student.

**Localized translation:** Ask the locally named school support professional to observe the student.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Follow the school's support process and speak with the appropriate staff member. Name the local role in the regional edition and explain what to do where no equivalent role exists.

### Parent guides

**Literal translation:** Use the school run to talk about feelings.

**Localized translation:** Talk about feelings during the journey home from school.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Choose a regular, low-pressure moment—such as the journey home, a snack, or an evening routine—and invite conversation without requiring it.

### Stories

**Literal translation:** The character brings a peanut-butter sandwich to a school with a different lunch culture.

**Localized translation:** Replace it with a common local meal.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Adapt the food, lunch setting, and dialogue together while preserving the scene's function: the child notices discomfort and asks a trusted adult for support.

### Will Talks

**Literal translation:** Today we unpack the elephant in the room.

**Localized translation:** Today we discuss the issue everyone avoids.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Today we discuss why adults may avoid conversations about children's anger and how to begin those conversations with care.

The standard removes an idiom and makes the educational purpose explicit.

### User interface

**Literal translation:** Submit.

**Localized translation:** Confirm.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Name the actual outcome, such as “Save reflection,” “Download worksheet,” or “Send message,” using natural target-language wording.

### Error message

**Literal translation:** Invalid input.

**Localized translation:** The information entered is incorrect.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Enter a date in the local displayed format. Preserve the user's data and show a valid example.

### Classroom participation

**Literal translation:** Every child tells the class about a time they felt sad.

**Localized translation:** Each child shares a sad experience with the group.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Children may discuss a story character, use a fictional example, share privately, or pass. The route may adapt culturally; forced disclosure is never required.

### Parenting language

**Literal translation:** Give your child autonomy.

**Localized translation:** Let your child decide independently.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Offer a meaningful choice within a clear boundary, such as which task to begin first. Explain autonomy as supported participation, not absence of family guidance.

### Mental health support

**Literal translation:** Call your GP if anxiety continues.

**Localized translation:** Contact a doctor if anxiety continues.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Identify the locally appropriate first contact and verified route. Explain that persistent, severe, or daily-life-limiting anxiety is worth discussing with a qualified health or education professional.

### Marketing

**Literal translation:** A must-have worksheet for every parent.

**Localized translation:** An essential worksheet for all families.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** A printable activity for practising emotion words with a trusted adult. State age guidance, language, format, accessibility, and limitations without urgency or universal claims.

### Notification

**Literal translation:** Great job! You completed emotional regulation.

**Localized translation:** Well done! You finished the regulation lesson.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Activity complete. You can review your choices or try another practice. Avoid claiming that completion equals mastery.

## Localization Checklist

### Purpose and scope

- [ ] The localized content has the same approved purpose and audience as the source.
- [ ] The source version is stable, identifiable, and approved.
- [ ] Material adaptations are documented.
- [ ] Psychology, safety, privacy, and consent meaning remain unchanged.
- [ ] Regional scope is clear.

### Language quality

- [ ] The text is accurate, natural, and complete.
- [ ] Grammar, punctuation, spelling, register, and rhythm suit the language.
- [ ] No source text, placeholder, or unintended language remains.
- [ ] Idioms and wordplay have been adapted or replaced.
- [ ] Pronouns and implied subjects are unambiguous.
- [ ] Read-aloud content sounds natural when spoken.
- [ ] A proficient human reviewer has approved the language.

### Voice and editorial quality

- [ ] The content remains calm, warm, respectful, practical, and honest.
- [ ] It does not become more clinical, childish, alarming, or promotional.
- [ ] Reading complexity suits the audience.
- [ ] Instructions and calls to action are clear and non-manipulative.
- [ ] Healthy Little Minds terminology is consistent.

### Cultural adaptation

- [ ] Examples, names, food, routines, transport, housing, and celebrations are plausible.
- [ ] Family structures and relationships are inclusive.
- [ ] School roles and classroom practices fit the region or are explained functionally.
- [ ] Communication style and participation routes preserve dignity.
- [ ] No stereotype substitutes for local understanding.
- [ ] Local reviewers have assessed sensitive implications.

### Psychology and education

- [ ] Psychological concepts preserve their approved meaning and limits.
- [ ] Diagnostic and clinical boundaries remain clear.
- [ ] Evidence is not overgeneralized to the target culture.
- [ ] Mental health terminology is accurate and non-stigmatizing.
- [ ] Developmental expectations are not made more rigid.
- [ ] Practical guidance works in the local educational and family context.
- [ ] Support and referral information is locally verified.

### Terminology

- [ ] Canonical emotion, psychology, education, parent, and UI terms are used.
- [ ] New terms or variants have been approved and added to the glossary.
- [ ] Clinical and everyday meanings are distinguished.
- [ ] Navigation, headings, search, and links use consistent terms.
- [ ] Search synonyms include natural local variations without merging distinct concepts.

### Interface and functionality

- [ ] Buttons and labels identify the correct action or destination.
- [ ] Forms accept local names, addresses, numbers, dates, and input methods.
- [ ] Validation examples match accepted local formats.
- [ ] Text does not truncate, overlap, or disappear.
- [ ] Menus, dialogs, alerts, errors, empty states, and offline states are localized.
- [ ] Search, sorting, filtering, and indexing work in the target language.
- [ ] Language switching preserves context or explains what will happen.
- [ ] No essential text is embedded in an image.

### Typography and layout

- [ ] Typeface coverage and glyph quality are complete.
- [ ] Line breaking and punctuation follow language rules.
- [ ] Text expansion and contraction are supported.
- [ ] Hierarchy and vertical rhythm remain readable.
- [ ] Mixed-script content has been reviewed.
- [ ] Mobile, tablet, desktop, zoom, and large-text layouts work.
- [ ] Printable resources fit appropriate local paper sizes without shrinking text.

### Illustrations and media

- [ ] Gestures, clothing, classrooms, homes, food, symbols, and written details are appropriate.
- [ ] Representation is meaningful and avoids tokenism.
- [ ] Recurring characters remain consistent.
- [ ] Audio pronunciation, pacing, and performance are natural.
- [ ] Captions and transcripts are complete and synchronized.
- [ ] Visual changes are reflected in alt text and descriptions.

### Accessibility

- [ ] Page and inline language metadata are correct.
- [ ] Semantic structure and reading order are preserved.
- [ ] Screen-reader output has been tested where feasible.
- [ ] Alt text conveys localized purpose.
- [ ] Captions, transcripts, and audio alternatives are available.
- [ ] Cognitive complexity and script difficulty suit the audience.
- [ ] Keyboard, touch, zoom, reflow, and input methods work.
- [ ] Accessibility defects from the source have not been reproduced knowingly.

### AI and review

- [ ] AI output has received full proficient human review.
- [ ] No AI-invented service, policy, citation, role, or term remains.
- [ ] Script conversion has not been mistaken for localization.
- [ ] Confidential information was not processed in an unapproved system.
- [ ] Cultural, psychology, accessibility, and functional reviews are recorded.

### Publication and maintenance

- [ ] Source and localized versions are linked by version.
- [ ] Owner, reviewers, dates, region, and next review are recorded.
- [ ] Metadata, alternate-language links, previews, and indexing are correct.
- [ ] Links, downloads, services, and contact routes work.
- [ ] Feedback and correction routes are available in the target language.
- [ ] A process exists for source updates and urgent corrections.
- [ ] The localized journey is coherent rather than an isolated translated page.

Any unresolved issue involving psychological meaning, safety, safeguarding, privacy, accessibility, discrimination, or action-changing mistranslation blocks publication.

## Definition of Done

Localized content is complete only when it is all of the following.

### Accurate

It preserves the approved meaning, claims, qualifications, instructions, data, consent, and safety information. No content is omitted or added without review.

### Natural

It reads and sounds as content created for the target audience, not as a visible translation. Grammar, terminology, register, rhythm, and interface conventions are appropriate.

### Culturally appropriate

Examples, relationships, school references, routines, services, and participation methods work in context without stereotypes. Adaptation preserves the educational and ethical core.

### Accessible

Language, script, typography, layout, media, documents, inputs, and interaction support varied needs and assistive technologies. Accessibility has been tested in the localized experience.

### Psychologically consistent

Psychology terms, evidence, diagnostic boundaries, uncertainty, and practical guidance retain the standards of the approved source. Cultural differences are acknowledged without unsupported generalization.

### Reviewed

Qualified linguistic, cultural, psychology, accessibility, and functional reviewers have approved the current version according to risk. AI or machine output has not bypassed human judgment.

### Aligned with the Healthy Little Minds voice

The localized edition remains calm, warm, human, respectful, curious, encouraging, practical, hopeful, evidence-informed, honest, and professional without sounding imported or academic.

A localized asset is not done merely because every source string has a target string. It must work as a complete educational experience. If the required quality cannot yet be sustained, reduce launch scope rather than publish a language edition that users cannot trust.

## Future Evolution

The platform should add languages through reusable foundations rather than page-by-page redesign.

The durable system should support:

- externalized content and interface strings;
- complete locale metadata;
- script-appropriate typography and directionality;
- flexible components and responsive layouts;
- local date, number, name, and input formats;
- terminology and translation-memory governance;
- linked source and localized versions;
- modular regional services and school-system notes;
- language-specific search and discovery;
- accessible captions, transcripts, audio, and documents;
- staged release and fallback without hidden language changes.

Before adding a language, assess community need, content scope, local ownership, reviewers, terminology, support pathways, technical readiness, accessibility, and maintenance capacity. A small coherent launch is preferable to a broad incomplete one.

New-language work should improve the whole platform. If a source concept cannot be translated clearly, the source may itself be unclear. If a component fails with longer text or another script, the component is not yet global. Localization findings should feed back into content, design, psychology, and accessibility standards.

Regional variants should be introduced when differences materially affect meaning or action, not for every minor preference. Shared language resources can use regional modules where services, education, law, or terminology diverge.

Success should be measured through comprehension, task completion, trust, accessibility, search quality, resource use, correction rates, and feedback from local users—not by translated word count alone.

Healthy Little Minds should remain one connected ecosystem as it grows. Every language deserves equal care, transparent limitations, and a sustainable path for correction and improvement.

## Closing Reflection

Every child, parent, and teacher deserves to feel that Healthy Little Minds was created for them, regardless of language or country.
