# Healthy Little Minds Content Style Guide

## Purpose

This guide is the master editorial standard for written content across Healthy Little Minds. It translates the project's governing principles into decisions that writers, editors, educators, psychologists, designers, developers, translators, and AI assistants can apply sentence by sentence.

Consistent writing builds trust. Readers should not have to learn a new vocabulary, structure, or emotional register each time they move from an article to a worksheet, story, tool, email, podcast page, or application. Consistency helps people recognise where they are, understand what is expected, and focus on learning rather than interpreting the interface or the writer.

Clear writing also supports learning. Psychological ideas can be difficult because they concern internal experiences, relationships, development, context, and uncertainty. Good editorial practice makes those ideas easier to notice, remember, discuss, and apply without making them less accurate.

Writing affects accessibility. Sentence structure, headings, word choice, instructions, labels, and layout can either reduce or increase barriers for readers with cognitive disabilities, dyslexia, limited literacy, attention differences, visual impairments, language-learning needs, or limited time. Accessibility is therefore part of writing quality, not a separate technical check.

Writing also affects emotional safety. Readers may arrive worried about a child, uncertain about themselves, frustrated by behaviour, or carrying experiences of distress. Content should help them understand and act without intensifying fear, shame, blame, or helplessness. Calm language does not minimise difficulty. It creates enough space to face difficulty clearly.

This guide applies to all public and internal-facing content intended to shape a user experience, including:

- website and landing pages;
- the emotion library;
- Parents Hub and Teachers Hub resources;
- stories, audiobooks, worksheets, and interactive tools;
- newsletters and service emails;
- navigation, buttons, forms, notifications, errors, and empty states;
- podcast descriptions and Will Talks materials;
- product descriptions and download metadata;
- future mobile applications;
- content drafted, translated, summarized, or adapted with AI.

The guide does not replace specialist standards. Psychology claims still require evidence review. Child-facing work still requires developmental review. Accessible documents still require accessibility testing. Localized work still requires linguistic and cultural review. Where standards conflict, clarity, accuracy, accessibility, safety, and child dignity take precedence over stylistic preference.

## Writing Philosophy

Healthy Little Minds writing should teach rather than impress. Readers should remember the idea and what they can do with it, not the sophistication of the writer. Authority comes from accuracy, usefulness, transparency, and respect—not from technical vocabulary or certainty.

### Teach rather than impress

Start with the reader's need. Explain the idea in the order needed to understand and apply it. Include detail because it changes understanding or action, not because the writer knows it.

Avoid ornamental language, academic performance, unexplained theory, and long introductions that delay the useful point. A knowledgeable writer can make a difficult idea clear without making it shallow.

### Reduce cognitive load

Readers have limited attention and working memory. Present one meaningful step at a time. Use predictable structure, descriptive headings, concise paragraphs, and examples at the point they are needed. Remove words that do not change meaning.

Do not make readers remember information across distant sections when a short reminder would help. Do not present ten choices when three organized routes will do. Progressive disclosure is preferable to overwhelming completeness on the first screen.

### Respect readers

Write to people as capable participants. Do not patronise children, shame parents, lecture teachers, or use professional readers as an excuse for poor clarity. Explain without assuming ignorance and guide without taking away agency.

Respect includes being honest about limits. Say when evidence is mixed, when a strategy may need adaptation, when professional help may be appropriate, and when Healthy Little Minds cannot answer an individual question.

### Be psychologically accurate

Preserve distinctions that matter. An emotion is not a diagnosis. A behaviour is not a character trait. A common experience is not universal. A helpful educational strategy is not treatment. Accuracy includes appropriate uncertainty, context, and scope.

### Encourage action

Substantial content should help readers decide what to notice, say, try, discuss, download, or explore next. Action must be proportionate and voluntary. Never manufacture urgency to force a click or imply that responsible readers must use a particular product.

### Remain calm and hopeful

Calm writing names difficulty without escalating it. Hopeful writing shows that learning, support, repair, and improvement are possible without promising a simple outcome. Use grounded encouragement rather than reassurance that cannot be guaranteed.

### Avoid manipulation and sensationalism

Do not exploit fear, guilt, uncertainty, or concern for children. Do not use shocking headlines, hidden consequences, false scarcity, inflated statistics, or language suggesting that one mistake causes permanent harm. Engagement never outranks wellbeing or truth.

### Prioritize clarity over cleverness

A clear heading is better than a pun. A familiar word is better than a fashionable substitute. A direct button label is better than a playful mystery. Creativity belongs in stories and learning experiences where it supports meaning; it should not obstruct navigation, consent, safety, or instruction.

## Plain Language

Plain language means readers can find what they need, understand it the first time, and use it. It does not mean speaking to adults as though they are children or removing necessary nuance.

### Use everyday vocabulary

Prefer the familiar word when it carries the same meaning:

| Avoid | Prefer |
|---|---|
| utilize | use |
| facilitate | help or make possible |
| commence | begin |
| demonstrate | show |
| prior to | before |
| in the event that | if |
| a significant number of | many |
| with regard to | about |

Use specialist terms when readers need them to communicate with educators, clinicians, or other professionals. Define the term immediately and then use it consistently.

**Poor:** Co-regulation constitutes the interpersonal modulation of affective arousal.

**Improved:** Co-regulation is the support one person gives another to help them manage strong feelings and return to a more settled state.

**Best practice:** Co-regulation is the support a trusted adult gives a child while the child is experiencing a strong feeling. The adult may slow their voice, reduce demands, offer simple choices, or stay nearby. Over time, repeated support can help children develop ways to regulate with greater independence.

The best-practice version defines the term, gives observable examples, and avoids promising a fixed result.

### Keep sentences purposeful

Aim for one main idea per sentence. Most sentences should be short or moderate in length. Longer sentences are acceptable when the relationship between ideas would become less clear if split.

**Poor:** When children become emotionally dysregulated, which can occur for many different reasons and can present in different ways depending on their age and circumstances, adults should attempt to remain calm while considering possible triggers and avoiding responses that might escalate the situation.

**Improved:** Children may become overwhelmed for many reasons, and this can look different at different ages. Begin by slowing your own response. Consider what may have happened and avoid adding demands that could increase distress.

Vary sentence rhythm. A sequence of very short sentences can feel abrupt or childish. A sequence of long sentences can hide the main point.

### Prefer active voice

Active voice usually identifies responsibility and makes instructions easier to follow.

**Poor:** The activity should be completed after the feelings cards have been distributed.

**Improved:** Give each child a feelings card, then begin the activity.

Passive voice is appropriate when the action matters more than the actor, the actor is unknown, or naming the actor would distract from the point.

**Appropriate:** The worksheet was updated in July 2026.

Do not use passive voice to hide responsibility.

**Poor:** A mistake was made when the child's information was shared.

**Improved:** We shared the child's information incorrectly. We have removed it and are reviewing what happened.

### Explain technical terms

Introduce a term in this order where practical:

1. Use the everyday explanation.
2. Name the technical term.
3. Give an example.
4. Explain why it matters.

**Example:** A child may avoid a task because they expect it to feel difficult or uncomfortable. Psychologists sometimes call this avoidance. Avoidance can bring short-term relief, but it may also prevent the child from learning that they can manage the task with support.

Do not define one technical term with three more technical terms. Do not assume a familiar-sounding psychology word has one agreed meaning.

### Prefer concrete examples

Abstract statements become useful when connected to a situation, action, or phrase.

**Poor:** Validate the child's emotion.

**Improved:** Name what you have noticed without claiming to know exactly how the child feels: “It looks like that change was difficult. Would you like a moment or some help?”

Examples illustrate possibilities. They are not universal scripts. Use phrases such as “You might say” rather than “Always say.”

### Remove filler and duplication

Delete openings such as “It is important to note that” when the sentence works without them. Avoid repeating the introduction in the conclusion. Summaries should help recall or action, not restate every paragraph.

**Poor:** It is worth remembering that it is important for adults to remember to remain calm.

**Improved:** Begin by calming your own response.

## Readability

Readability is the ease with which a particular reader can understand and use content. It depends on vocabulary, syntax, structure, typography, prior knowledge, emotional state, context, and format. A numerical reading score can identify possible difficulty, but it cannot judge accuracy, dignity, coherence, or developmental suitability.

Use readability tools as prompts, never as automatic approval. Do not replace an accurate familiar phrase with an unnatural one merely to lower a score. Test important content with representative readers whenever possible.

### Children

Child-facing content must match developmental understanding, not merely chronological age. Use concrete language, short sequences, repetition with purpose, and examples from recognisable experiences. Explain one emotional skill at a time. Provide options rather than tests with one “correct” feeling.

Children should never need to disclose personal experiences to complete public or classroom content. Avoid abstract warnings, sarcasm, idioms, and language that makes adults' approval the sole reason for emotional skills.

### Parents and caregivers

Parents need clear explanations, realistic examples, and manageable next steps. Assume care and good intent without assuming time, money, family structure, literacy, or confidence. Explain professional terminology that families may encounter.

Write for a reader who may be tired or worried. Put the immediate answer early. Separate what can be tried at home from signs that additional support may be useful. Do not make ordinary uncertainty sound dangerous.

### Teachers and educators

Teachers need practical detail, classroom conditions, adaptation options, and a credible rationale. Use educational terminology when it supports professional use, but avoid unnecessary academic language. Make time, materials, age range, and implementation steps easy to scan.

Respect teachers' expertise and constraints. Do not imply that one strategy works for every child or that a difficult outcome proves poor teaching.

### Professionals

Content for mental health, education, design, or technical professionals may use established specialist terms, references, and greater conceptual density. Clarity remains mandatory. Professional audiences should not have to decode inflated prose.

State scope, definitions, evidence quality, and implementation conditions. When a document serves mixed audiences, lead with a plain-language summary and place technical detail in a clearly labelled section.

### Reading level decisions

Choose complexity according to the youngest or least specialised intended reader who must act independently. Do not set one universal reading age for all content. Record the intended audience and, for child-facing resources, the developmental assumptions behind the language.

When content cannot be simplified without losing essential meaning, add support: a glossary, illustration, audio version, worked example, summary, or guided adult activity.

## Structure

Structure should answer the reader's questions in a useful order. Begin with the need, establish meaning, provide action, and make next steps visible.

### Headings

Use one clear page title. Organize subheadings in a logical hierarchy without skipping levels for visual effect. Headings should describe the content that follows.

**Poor:** A storm in a teacup

**Good:** Helping a child manage frustration

**Best practice:** What to do when frustration interrupts learning

Questions are useful headings when they match a reader's likely question. Avoid making every heading a question or using headings as complete paragraphs.

### Introductions

An introduction should usually tell readers:

- what the resource covers;
- who it is for;
- why it may help;
- what the reader will be able to do;
- any essential safety or scope boundary.

Do not begin with a broad history of psychology when the reader needs help with tomorrow's transition. Avoid suspense. Put the useful premise first.

### Summaries

Use summaries for long, complex, or action-oriented content. A summary may appear at the beginning as “In brief” or at the end as “What to remember.” Keep it faithful to the full content and include its main qualification.

**Poor summary:** Anxiety is easy to overcome with the right approach.

**Better summary:** Children can learn ways to respond to anxious feelings. Support should be gradual, compassionate, and adapted to the child; persistent or severe anxiety may require professional help.

### Bullet lists

Use bullets for parallel items that do not need a fixed order. Begin each item with the same grammatical form. Keep related items together and introduce the purpose of the list.

Do not use a list to disguise disconnected ideas. Do not create a bullet for every sentence on a page.

### Step-by-step instructions

Use numbered steps when order matters. Begin each step with a clear action. Include preparation before step one and explain what to do if the expected result does not occur.

**Example:**

1. Choose a quiet moment, not the middle of a conflict.
2. Describe what you noticed without assigning motive.
3. Ask one open question and allow time for an answer.
4. Agree on one small next step.
5. Return to the conversation later if either person needs a pause.

### Callout boxes

Use callouts for information readers should notice without interrupting the main explanation. Give each callout a functional label such as “Safety note,” “Try this,” “Remember,” or “Evidence note.”

Do not overuse callouts or use colour alone to distinguish their purpose. Never hide essential safety information behind an optional expansion.

### Examples

Place examples near the principle they illustrate. Include context and show realistic imperfection. Use diverse names and settings carefully, without suggesting that a demographic identity explains behaviour.

Label fictional and composite examples when readers might otherwise believe they describe a real person. Remove identifying details from real cases and obtain appropriate permission.

### Reflection prompts

Reflection prompts should invite curiosity and future action, not confession or self-criticism.

**Avoid:** Why did you fail to stay calm?

**Prefer:** What made it difficult to stay calm in that moment? What support could make a different response easier next time?

For children, always provide a dignified way not to answer. Do not ask for sensitive disclosures in worksheets that may be seen by peers or displayed publicly.

## Emotional Tone

Healthy Little Minds communicates with calm, warmth, encouragement, respect, evidence, and practical usefulness. Tone changes with context, but dignity and accuracy remain stable.

### Calm

Use measured words and proportionate emphasis. State the issue, explain what is known, and identify a next step. In urgent content, be direct without becoming alarming.

**Sensational:** This hidden sign could mean your child is in serious trouble.

**Calm:** A change in behaviour can have many causes. Notice how long it lasts, how strongly it affects daily life, and whether the child needs additional support.

### Warm and encouraging

Warmth comes from recognising experience, offering useful support, and leaving room for learning. It does not require exclamation marks, pet names, or constant cheerfulness.

**Poor:** Don't worry—you've got this!

**Better:** You do not need to solve everything at once. Begin with one small change and notice what helps.

### Respectful and non-judgmental

Describe behaviour and circumstances without assigning moral character. Do not divide people into good and bad parents, difficult and easy children, or caring and uncaring teachers.

**Poor:** A defiant child refuses to follow simple rules.

**Better:** A child may refuse an instruction when the task feels unsafe, unclear, difficult, or beyond their current capacity. The limit may still matter; understanding the context helps the adult respond more effectively.

### Emotionally validating without exaggeration

Validation recognises that an experience makes sense from the person's perspective. It does not mean agreeing with every interpretation or removing every boundary.

**Example:** “I can see that you are angry about stopping the game. It is okay to be angry. I will not let you hit.”

Avoid claiming to know exactly how someone feels. Use tentative observations and invite correction.

### Tone to avoid

Do not use:

- fear, guilt, or shame to motivate action;
- false urgency or scarcity;
- exaggerated reassurance;
- diagnostic language for ordinary experiences;
- humour about distress, disability, family circumstances, or behaviour;
- commands where choice is possible;
- inspirational clichés that dismiss structural difficulty;
- adversarial language about “winning” against emotions or children;
- praise that implies worth depends on compliance;
- repeated exclamation marks, capitals, or alarm symbols.

## Inclusive Language

Inclusive language helps people recognise themselves without being reduced to one characteristic. It avoids assumptions, represents meaningful variation, and respects how communities describe themselves.

### Family structures

Use “parent or caregiver” when the role may be held by a parent, grandparent, foster carer, guardian, older relative, or another trusted adult. Use “family” without assuming two parents, marriage, biological relationship, shared housing, or a particular gender.

Specific wording is appropriate when the content genuinely concerns a defined role, such as guidance for foster carers. Do not replace every precise term with a generic one when precision matters.

**Avoid:** Ask Mum and Dad to help.

**Prefer:** Ask a parent, caregiver, or trusted adult to help.

### Disability and neurodiversity

Describe barriers and support needs rather than defining a person by limitation. Respect both person-first language (“child with autism”) and identity-first language (“autistic child”), recognizing that preferences differ among people and communities. Follow the individual's stated preference when known.

Do not use diagnostic terms as metaphors or insults. Avoid “suffers from” unless describing a person's own chosen language and actual suffering. Do not call people “brave” merely for participating in ordinary life.

Never assume that a diagnosis predicts one learning style, personality, capacity, or behaviour. Avoid unsupported categories such as “visual learner” when presented as fixed psychological types.

### Culture and language

Do not treat one culture as the default and others as variations. Avoid universal claims about eye contact, independence, emotional expression, discipline, family involvement, or respect. Explain context when an example depends on a local practice.

Use names, families, schools, clothing, food, and settings from varied cultures without tokenism. Cultural identity should not be introduced only in content about difference or difficulty.

Do not imitate accents, use unfamiliar words for decoration, or expect children to represent a whole culture. Localized content requires local review, not merely translated words.

### Gender and sexuality

Use gender-neutral language when gender is irrelevant. Use “they” for a person whose gender is unknown. Do not assume children's interests, emotional expression, family roles, or behaviour from gender.

When gender or sexuality is relevant, use current respectful terminology, protect privacy, and avoid implying that identity is a problem to solve. Child-facing content must remain developmentally appropriate without erasing the families and identities children encounter.

### Socioeconomic diversity

Do not assume access to money, private space, printers, devices, transport, time, paid services, or stable internet. Offer low-cost and no-cost alternatives. Avoid examples in which responsible participation requires purchases.

Write about poverty and resource constraints without blame. Do not present middle-class routines or one school system as universal. Where support depends on a local service, explain that availability varies.

### Inclusive examples

Review the whole body of content, not only individual pages. Representation should be varied across ordinary stories, success, learning, conflict, leadership, and play. Do not repeatedly assign marginalised characters the role of needing help while others provide it.

## Child-Friendly Writing

Child-friendly writing respects children's intelligence while matching language, length, abstraction, and emotional demand to development. It should support agency and connection, not secure obedience through fear or praise.

### Ages 4–6

Use short concrete sentences, familiar experiences, repetition, rhythm, and one instruction at a time. Name body sensations carefully and avoid suggesting that every child experiences an emotion in the same way. Pair words with clear illustrations or adult-guided action.

**Poor:** Regulate your emotional response by engaging in diaphragmatic breathing.

**Good:** Put a hand on your tummy. Breathe in slowly. Breathe out slowly.

**Best practice:** “Would you like to try a slow breath with me? We can smell the flower, then cool the soup.” Provide another option for children who find breath-focused activities uncomfortable.

Use choices the adult can genuinely honour: “Would you like to sit here or by the bookshelf?” Avoid false choices such as “Would you like to tidy up?” when tidying is required.

### Ages 7–10

Children in this range can usually follow longer sequences, compare perspectives, and learn more precise emotion words, but development varies. Use direct explanations, relatable scenarios, questions with more than one acceptable answer, and opportunities to practise.

**Poor:** Negative emotions make us behave badly.

**Good:** Strong feelings can make it harder to pause and choose what to do. Feelings are not bad, but some actions can hurt people.

**Best practice:** “Anger can tell us that something feels unfair or wrong. When anger is strong, pausing can help us choose an action that protects us and other people.”

Avoid making emotional insight a quiz. A child may not know what they feel, may use different words, or may not want to share.

### Parents writing or speaking to children

Encourage observation, curiosity, boundaries, and repair. Adults should use fewer words when a child is overwhelmed and return to explanation after the child is more settled.

**Instead of:** Stop being dramatic. There is nothing to be scared of.

**Try:** I can see this feels scary. I am here. Shall we take one small step together?

**Instead of:** You're okay.

**Try:** You are safe with me. Your knee hurts, and we can look after it.

Do not script adults to promise outcomes they cannot control. “I will stay with you while we find help” is more trustworthy than “Nothing bad will happen.”

### Teachers speaking to students

Teacher language should preserve dignity, especially in public. Describe the required action, provide a reason where helpful, and offer a route back into learning.

**Instead of:** Why are you always so disruptive?

**Try:** The class needs to hear these instructions. You can listen here or move to the quiet desk. We can talk after I finish.

**Instead of:** Good girl for sitting quietly.

**Try:** You found a place where you could focus and begin your work.

Avoid requiring eye contact, public apologies, or immediate emotional explanations. Corrections should be private where possible, specific, proportionate, and followed by reconnection.

## Psychology Writing

Psychology content should be evidence-informed, responsible, and transparent about what is known. Writers must neither inflate ordinary educational content into clinical authority nor reduce complex evidence to memorable but misleading claims.

### Introduce concepts in context

Begin with the experience or decision the concept helps explain. Define the term, give an example, state its practical relevance, and identify important limits.

**Poor:** The amygdala hijacks the rational brain during a tantrum.

**Good:** Strong emotion can make it harder for a child to process language, consider options, and control an immediate response.

**Best practice:** When a child is highly distressed, they may find it harder to process long explanations or choose between many options. Use fewer words, reduce unnecessary demands, and return to problem-solving when the child is more settled. This is a practical description, not a diagnosis or a complete account of what is happening in the brain.

### Distinguish evidence from opinion

Signal the basis of a statement:

- “Research suggests” for a pattern supported by relevant research;
- “Professional guidance recommends” for a named guideline;
- “One possible explanation is” for a reasoned hypothesis;
- “In this activity, we use” for a Healthy Little Minds editorial choice;
- “Some families find” for experience that should not be presented as evidence of effectiveness.

Do not attach “research shows” to a claim without a suitable source. A quotation from an expert remains expert opinion unless it accurately represents wider evidence.

### Avoid overclaiming

State population, context, time, and limitations when they affect interpretation. Distinguish correlation from causation and group findings from individual prediction.

Avoid “proves,” “guarantees,” “always,” “never,” “scientifically designed” without explanation, “rewires the brain,” and exact outcome promises. Do not use neuroscience language merely to make advice sound authoritative.

### State uncertainty

Uncertainty is not weakness. It helps readers make proportionate decisions.

**Poor:** This behaviour means the child has anxiety.

**Improved:** Avoiding an activity can have many causes, including fear, uncertainty, difficulty, sensory discomfort, or lack of interest.

**Best practice:** Notice when the avoidance occurs, how long it has continued, and how much it affects daily life. A qualified professional can help if worry is persistent, severe, or interfering with the child's participation.

### Do not diagnose readers

Educational pages may describe patterns and explain when an assessment could be useful. They must not tell a reader that they or a child has a disorder. Screening tools must be validated, governed, appropriately framed, and never presented as diagnosis.

Avoid diagnostic labels in titles designed to attract worried readers. Provide crisis, safeguarding, or professional support information when scope requires it, using locally verified routes.

### Education is not therapy

Stories, worksheets, breathing activities, emotion tools, and classroom strategies may support learning and wellbeing. They do not become therapy because they use psychological ideas. Do not call educational content treatment, intervention, or cure unless it meets the relevant professional and evidence standards.

State boundaries plainly: “This resource provides general educational information. It does not replace assessment or care from a qualified professional.” Do not repeat disclaimers so often that they obstruct useful content; place them where scope could reasonably be misunderstood.

## Action-Oriented Writing

Every substantial resource should reduce the distance between knowledge and everyday action. After explaining an idea, help readers decide what to do next.

A useful action sequence is:

1. **Notice:** Identify an observable pattern without rushing to judgment.
2. **Understand:** Offer one or more plausible explanations with appropriate uncertainty.
3. **Choose:** Select a small, relevant response.
4. **Try:** Explain how to carry it out in context.
5. **Reflect:** Notice what changed and what did not.
6. **Adapt:** Modify the response or seek further support.

Actions should be specific enough to attempt and small enough to fit real life.

**Vague:** Build emotional resilience at home.

**Actionable:** At the end of the day, ask, “What was difficult, and what helped you keep going?” Listen before offering advice.

**Best practice:** Choose one calm moment this week. Ask, “What was difficult, and what helped even a little?” Accept “I don't know” as an answer. Share a brief example from your own day if the child wants help getting started.

State time, materials, adult role, child choices, and adaptations where relevant. Explain what to do if an activity increases distress or does not help. Do not treat completion as proof of learning.

Calls to seek professional help should be actionable too. Explain the kind of professional or school contact that may be appropriate, what information could be useful to bring, and how urgency changes the next step. Verify all local pathways.

## Cross-Linking

Cross-linking should create a coherent learning path, not a promotional funnel. Link when another resource answers the reader's likely next question, offers a different mode of learning, supports practice, or provides audience-specific guidance.

Articles may connect naturally to:

- a story that lets children explore the idea safely;
- a worksheet that supports practice or reflection;
- an interactive tool that guides a defined task;
- another emotion that is commonly confused or experienced alongside the topic;
- Parents Hub guidance for home application;
- Teachers Hub guidance for classroom application;
- a podcast or Will Talks episode that adds explanation or perspective.

Introduce the relationship in plain language.

**Promotional:** Don't miss our amazing anger worksheet!

**Useful:** To practise identifying early signs of anger, use the body-signals worksheet with a trusted adult.

**Useful:** Teachers can explore classroom adaptations in “Responding to frustration during independent work.”

Use descriptive link text that makes sense out of context. Avoid “click here,” “learn more,” and repeated links with identical labels leading to different destinations. Do not interrupt a sensitive explanation with unrelated recommendations.

Cross-links require maintenance. Broken, outdated, inaccessible, untranslated, or contextually inappropriate links weaken trust. Localized pages should link to equivalent localized resources when available and clearly label another language when not.

## Calls to Action

A call to action should describe the next step accurately. It should be proportionate to the reader's need and should not create pressure.

Acceptable calls to action include:

- Try this activity
- Explore another feeling
- Download the worksheet
- Read the teacher guide
- Listen to the episode
- Save this resource
- Review the steps
- Ask for support
- Continue the story
- Start again

Use sentence case. Begin with a specific verb. Keep button text concise while ensuring the surrounding context explains the outcome. If an action downloads a file, opens another site, requires an account, or starts audio, make that clear before activation.

Avoid:

- You need this now
- Fix your child's behaviour today
- Unlock the secret
- Don't miss out
- Transform your classroom instantly
- Click here
- Yes, I care about my child
- No, I prefer to struggle

Never frame refusal as morally inferior. Consent choices should be neutral and equally understandable. Primary and secondary actions should reflect user importance, not organizational preference.

## Interface and Service Content

Interface writing is part of the product, not decoration added after design. Labels, helper text, validation, notifications, errors, empty states, and confirmations should help users act with confidence.

### Buttons and labels

Name the action or destination. Use “Download worksheet,” not “Submit,” when downloading is what happens. Keep terminology consistent between navigation, headings, and controls.

Do not rely on icons alone for unfamiliar actions. Accessible names must match or include visible labels.

### Forms

Use visible labels and concise helper text. Explain why sensitive information is requested and whether it is optional. Put formatting guidance before submission rather than revealing it only as an error.

**Poor:** Invalid input.

**Good:** Enter an email address in the format name@example.com.

### Error messages

An error should explain what happened, whether the user's work is safe, and what they can do. Do not blame the user or use playful language when something has failed.

**Poor:** Oops! You did something wrong.

**Good:** We could not save your changes. Your text is still here. Check your connection and try again.

If the service caused the problem, say “We could not” rather than “You failed.” Provide a route to support when retrying may not work.

### Empty states

Empty states should explain the current condition and an appropriate next action. Do not make a user feel behind.

**Poor:** Nothing here yet!

**Good:** You have not saved any resources. Explore the library to find articles, stories, and worksheets you may want to return to.

### Confirmations

Confirm the completed action and any next step.

**Poor:** Success!

**Good:** The worksheet has been downloaded.

For destructive or consequential actions, name what will happen and whether it can be reversed. Do not use vague confirmations such as “Are you sure?”

## Format-Specific Guidance

### Emotion library

Each emotion page should explain the emotion without labelling it good or bad. Include possible body signals and situations as examples, not universal signs. Connect recognition with safe expression, regulation, communication, and support. Never imply that one facial expression reliably reveals an internal state.

### Parents Hub

Lead with the parent's likely concern, normalize reasonable uncertainty, and offer a manageable response. Avoid blame, perfectionism, and promises. Separate general education from signs that additional support may be useful.

### Teachers Hub

State the classroom objective, context, time, materials, steps, adaptations, cautions, and reflection route. Every substantial resource should answer, “What can I do tomorrow morning?” Follow the Teachers Hub handbook.

### Stories and audiobooks

Let meaning emerge through character, action, and consequence rather than explanatory lectures. Preserve emotional complexity. Characters can make mistakes without losing worth. Audiobook scripts should sound natural when spoken and provide pronunciation guidance, pacing, and accessible transcripts.

### Worksheets and interactive tools

Give a clear purpose and explain how to begin. Use one instruction per step. Provide alternatives to writing, fine motor actions, timers, dragging, and public disclosure where relevant. Completion should not be framed as diagnosis, assessment, or proof of emotional competence.

### Emails and newsletters

Use accurate subject lines and a clear purpose. Put the most useful information early. Limit competing calls to action. Make subscription preferences and unsubscribe routes easy to understand. Never use anxiety or family concern to increase opens.

### Podcasts and Will Talks

Descriptions should state the topic, speakers, practical value, important limits, and available transcript. Distinguish personal experience from general evidence. Link claims and related practical resources. Avoid presenting a guest's credentials as proof that every statement is correct.

### Product descriptions

Describe intended user, purpose, format, contents, accessibility, language, and practical use. Do not use inflated claims such as “life-changing,” “essential,” or “guaranteed.” Make cost, account requirements, download format, and usage terms clear before commitment.

## Editorial Examples

The following examples show how standards combine in common situations.

### Explaining behaviour

**Poor:** Attention-seeking children misbehave because they want a reaction.

**Good:** Some behaviour may be an attempt to gain connection, help, predictability, or a response from an adult.

**Best practice:** Describe what you observe before deciding why it happened. If a child repeatedly interrupts during independent work, consider several possibilities: the task may be unclear, the child may need connection, or working alone may be difficult. Respond to the immediate need while teaching a more workable way to ask for help.

### Supporting a worried parent

**Poor:** If you ignore these signs, your child's anxiety could get worse.

**Good:** Notice whether worry is persistent or affecting daily activities.

**Best practice:** Worry is a normal part of childhood, but some children need more support. Notice how often it occurs, how intense it feels, and whether it limits sleep, school, play, or relationships. If the worry persists or causes significant distress, speak with an appropriate health or education professional.

### Giving classroom advice

**Poor:** Use a calm corner to stop meltdowns.

**Good:** A calm space can offer fewer demands and sensory input when a child feels overwhelmed.

**Best practice:** Introduce the calm space when the class is settled. Explain that anyone may use it for support, not punishment. Offer simple options and let the child leave when ready. Review whether the space helps; some children regulate better through movement, connection, or a different sensory environment.

### Describing evidence

**Poor:** Science proves gratitude makes children happier.

**Good:** Some studies associate gratitude activities with improved wellbeing.

**Best practice:** Some research suggests that brief gratitude practices may support aspects of wellbeing for some children. Effects vary, and gratitude should not be used to dismiss sadness, anger, unfairness, or material difficulty.

### Encouraging reflection

**Poor:** Write down what you did wrong and promise not to do it again.

**Good:** Think about what happened and what you could try next time.

**Best practice:** When you feel ready, choose one way to reflect: draw what happened, talk with a trusted adult, or finish the sentence “Next time, I could…”. You may take a break and return later.

### Writing an error state

**Poor:** Uh-oh! Your story vanished.

**Good:** We could not load your story.

**Best practice:** We could not load the story. Check your connection and try again. You can also return to the story library.

### Writing a call to action

**Poor:** Discover the secret to emotionally intelligent children now!

**Good:** Read about emotional literacy.

**Best practice:** Explore five ways to practise emotion words during everyday conversations.

### Writing about mistakes

**Poor:** Good parents stay calm.

**Good:** Staying calm can help, but every parent has difficult moments.

**Best practice:** A calm response can make connection easier, but no adult remains calm all the time. If you react in a way you regret, pause, repair the relationship, and consider what support could help next time.

### Writing a child-facing prompt

**Poor:** Tell the class about a time you were deeply sad.

**Good:** Think of a feeling you know.

**Best practice:** Choose a feeling from the cards. You can talk about a character, make up an example, or pass. You do not need to share a private experience.

## Localization and Translation Readiness

Write source content so meaning can travel. Avoid idioms, puns, culturally specific metaphors, unexplained school structures, ambiguous pronouns, and strings that depend on English word order. Do not concatenate fragments in interfaces.

Provide translators with audience, context, character limits, definitions, screenshots where relevant, and notes for terms that must remain consistent. Allow text expansion and script-specific punctuation and line breaking. Do not shorten a translation until meaning is lost merely to preserve an English layout.

Localization adapts examples, educational terminology, family references, services, safeguarding routes, names, and cultural assumptions. A literal translation can be grammatically correct and still be unsafe or unusable.

Machine translation and AI can support drafting but cannot approve publication. A proficient human reviewer familiar with the audience and subject must review every localized version. Psychology, safeguarding, legal, clinical, and crisis information requires appropriate local verification.

## AI-Assisted Content

AI assistants may help outline, reorganize, simplify, compare terminology, generate alternatives, identify missing context, and prepare drafts. They do not remove the need for source verification, editorial judgment, specialist review, accessibility review, or local review.

Every AI-assisted draft should be checked for:

- invented facts, quotations, statistics, citations, and links;
- overconfident psychological or educational claims;
- diagnostic inference or individualized advice;
- stereotypes and hidden cultural assumptions;
- generic reassurance and manipulative emotional language;
- inconsistent terminology and fabricated product features;
- inaccessible structure or interaction instructions;
- text copied too closely from protected sources;
- disclosure of personal or confidential information.

AI should not approve its own output. A named human remains accountable for publication. Material AI involvement should be recorded according to project governance. Never enter identifiable information about a child, family, educator, or user into an unapproved AI system.

## Content Checklist

### Purpose and audience

- [ ] The content has one clear purpose and a defined primary audience.
- [ ] The reader's likely need appears before organizational background.
- [ ] The format is appropriate to the learning or service task.
- [ ] The content adds value rather than duplicating an existing resource.
- [ ] Scope, limits, and intended use are clear.

### Clarity and readability

- [ ] Everyday vocabulary is used where it preserves meaning.
- [ ] Technical terms are necessary, defined, and used consistently.
- [ ] Sentences carry one main idea and paragraphs have a clear purpose.
- [ ] Active voice identifies action and responsibility where appropriate.
- [ ] Headings describe the content and follow a logical hierarchy.
- [ ] Lists, tables, examples, and callouts improve understanding.
- [ ] Readability suits the intended audience and context.
- [ ] Important content has been tested with representative readers where feasible.

### Tone and emotional safety

- [ ] The tone is calm, warm, respectful, practical, and proportionate.
- [ ] Difficulty is acknowledged without sensationalism.
- [ ] Hope is grounded and does not promise outcomes.
- [ ] No fear, shame, guilt, false urgency, or manipulation is used.
- [ ] Children, families, and educators retain dignity and agency.
- [ ] Emotional validation does not remove appropriate boundaries.

### Psychology and evidence

- [ ] Psychological terms and claims are accurate and within scope.
- [ ] Evidence, professional guidance, experience, and opinion are distinguished.
- [ ] Sources directly support the claims attached to them.
- [ ] Uncertainty, limitations, context, and individual variation are stated.
- [ ] The content does not diagnose, prescribe, or present education as therapy.
- [ ] Professional or urgent support is signposted where appropriate.
- [ ] Required specialist review has been completed.

### Inclusion and accessibility

- [ ] Family, disability, neurodiversity, culture, gender, and income assumptions have been reviewed.
- [ ] Examples represent people without stereotypes or tokenism.
- [ ] Instructions offer accessible alternatives where needed.
- [ ] Links are descriptive and headings support navigation.
- [ ] Media has captions, transcripts, descriptions, or text equivalents.
- [ ] The content works with screen readers, zoom, reflow, keyboard input, and mobile layouts as applicable.
- [ ] Safety information is visible and not dependent on colour or interaction.

### Action and connection

- [ ] The reader knows what to notice, try, discuss, or do next.
- [ ] Actions are specific, manageable, and adaptable.
- [ ] Time, materials, choices, and likely difficulties are explained where relevant.
- [ ] Reflection supports learning rather than judgment.
- [ ] Cross-links answer a likely next question or support practice.
- [ ] Calls to action describe outcomes accurately and without pressure.

### Localization and maintenance

- [ ] Idioms, puns, local assumptions, and ambiguous wording have been removed or documented.
- [ ] Translator context and terminology guidance are available.
- [ ] Local examples, services, safeguards, and educational terms are verified.
- [ ] Localized content has received qualified human review.
- [ ] Links, downloads, dates, owners, and review schedules are current.
- [ ] AI involvement has been reviewed and documented where required.

### Final editorial review

- [ ] The content follows the Project Charter, Product Principles, Voice & Tone, Writing Style, and relevant product handbook.
- [ ] Spelling, punctuation, capitalization, numbers, references, and formatting are consistent.
- [ ] Metadata, titles, descriptions, and preview text are accurate.
- [ ] Errors, empty states, and edge cases have been written and tested.
- [ ] A named person is accountable for approval and future review.

## Definition of Done

A page or content asset is not complete merely because the draft is written, the design is attractive, or the main path works. It is complete only when all applicable standards below are met.

### Psychologically accurate

Claims, definitions, examples, and recommended actions are evidence-informed, proportionate, and within educational scope. Evidence is distinguished from opinion. Uncertainty is visible. The content does not diagnose readers, promise outcomes, or present educational support as treatment. Required expert review is recorded.

### Accessible

The content can be perceived, understood, navigated, and used by people with varied visual, hearing, motor, cognitive, language, and literacy needs. Structure is semantic; interactions are operable; media alternatives are complete; documents are accessible; and manual testing complements automated checks.

### Readable

The intended audience can find the main point, understand the explanation, and follow the next step without unnecessary effort. Vocabulary, sentence structure, density, examples, and format suit the audience and context. Complexity is supported rather than concealed.

### Localized

The source is translation-ready. Published localizations preserve meaning, evidence, dignity, function, and accessibility while adapting terminology, examples, services, and cultural assumptions. Qualified human reviewers have approved the language and context.

### Cross-linked

Relevant stories, worksheets, tools, emotions, hub resources, and audio are connected through useful descriptive links. Links support the reader's journey without becoming promotional clutter. Destinations are current, accessible, and appropriately localized.

### Reviewed

Editorial, evidence, educational, accessibility, localization, safeguarding, legal, design, or technical reviews have been completed according to risk. Feedback has been resolved or documented. A named owner, version, publication date, and next review date exist.

### Actionable

The reader knows what to do next. Instructions are specific and realistic. Adaptations, choices, cautions, and routes for further support are included where relevant. The action does not rely on manipulation, hidden requirements, or unrealistic access to resources.

### Aligned with Product Principles

The final content protects child wellbeing and dignity, makes psychology practical, preserves accuracy while reducing complexity, supports relationships, remains calm and accessible, respects global audiences, uses AI responsibly, and can be maintained over time.

Any unresolved issue involving safety, psychological misinformation, accessibility, privacy, discrimination, safeguarding, or manipulative communication blocks publication. Schedule pressure does not reduce the minimum standard. When a complete version is not yet possible, reduce scope rather than publish content that cannot be trusted.

## Governance and Maintenance

The Content Style Guide is a maintained standard. Proposed changes should identify the problem, affected formats and audiences, evidence or user feedback, accessibility and localization implications, and relationship to existing documentation.

New editorial patterns should be tested before becoming requirements. One unusual page should not create a global rule. When a pattern is approved, update examples, templates, AI instructions, checklists, and related handbooks together.

Exceptions must be deliberate, documented, and approved by the appropriate owner. A channel's space limit may justify shorter wording, but it does not justify ambiguity, inaccessibility, or emotional manipulation.

Content should be audited periodically for outdated evidence, broken links, terminology drift, inaccessible files, localization gaps, and inconsistent calls to action. Corrections should propagate across duplicated formats and languages. Retired guidance should be removed or clearly marked so it cannot be mistaken for current advice.

Every sentence published by Healthy Little Minds should make psychological knowledge easier to understand, remember, and use in everyday life.
