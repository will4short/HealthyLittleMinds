# Healthy Little Minds Referencing Policy

## Purpose

This document is the definitive referencing, evidence management, citation, and source governance handbook for Healthy Little Minds. It governs how evidence is discovered, selected, evaluated, recorded, cited, maintained, monitored, updated, corrected, and retired throughout the content lifecycle.

Referencing is not merely a way to avoid plagiarism or make content appear authoritative. It enables readers and reviewers to trace important claims, inspect their basis, understand their limits, and identify when knowledge changes. It supports public trust, scientific integrity, transparency, accountability, correction, and long-term maintainability.

Every important psychological or educational claim should be traceable to an appropriate source. “Important” includes claims that may change how a reader understands a child, interprets behaviour, chooses a parenting or teaching strategy, evaluates risk, seeks professional support, or spends time or money. It also includes definitions, statistics, historical claims, claims of effectiveness, and statements about consensus or professional guidance.

Traceability must exist even when formal citations do not appear in the visible user experience. A child-facing story or interface prompt may not display references, but the underlying psychology and educational decisions still require an evidence record. Short formats, marketing, social media, illustrations, audio, and AI-assisted content receive the same truth standard as long articles.

This policy focuses on evidence management rather than one citation style. Punctuation and ordering conventions may change by publication format, language, or audience. Source identity, claim support, retrievability, accuracy, and stewardship do not.

This policy complements the Psychology Editorial Standard and other governance documents. It does not determine clinical practice, diagnose users, or replace subject review. When evidence is absent, weak, indirect, or conflicting, contributors must say so rather than use references to create an appearance of certainty.

## Referencing Philosophy

### Evidence before popularity

A widely shared claim is not necessarily well supported. Search ranking, media attention, bestseller status, social engagement, an expert's visibility, or frequent repetition cannot substitute for appropriate evidence.

Contributors should trace popular claims to their origin and ask whether later research supports, qualifies, or contradicts them. Familiar frameworks may be useful teaching tools, but their familiarity should not be described as proof. Trend-driven content must meet the same source standard as foundational content.

Popularity can identify what people are asking. It cannot determine the answer.

### Quality before quantity

Ten weak or irrelevant citations do not outweigh one strong, directly relevant synthesis. Reference lists should demonstrate the basis of the content, not the volume of reading performed.

Choose sources that match the population, setting, outcome, and claim. Prefer a high-quality systematic review over a collection of selectively chosen studies when the review is current and applicable. Include multiple sources when they add independent evidence, represent credible disagreement, or cover distinct aspects of the claim.

Quality also includes clarity of methods, transparency, appropriate analysis, ethical conduct, and relevance. Prestige and citation count are not enough.

### Transparency before authority

Readers should be able to understand why a source is relevant and where uncertainty remains. A citation should not be used as an instruction to defer to authority.

State whether a source is research, professional guidance, policy, expert interpretation, or lived experience. Explain important limitations and conflicts. Do not imply that a government or professional organization statement is a universal scientific fact when it also reflects law, policy, feasibility, or values.

Credentials matter when evaluating expertise, but credentials do not make every claim correct. Healthy Little Minds should show its reasoning rather than relying on reputation alone.

### Current knowledge before outdated tradition

Longstanding ideas should remain only when they still reflect credible knowledge or are clearly presented as historical context. Age does not make a theory true, and novelty does not make a new study better.

Contributors should examine current reviews and guidance, then determine whether classic sources remain necessary. A landmark paper may establish origin; a contemporary synthesis should usually establish current understanding.

When stronger evidence overturns or materially qualifies established content, Healthy Little Minds should update the content rather than protect consistency with earlier publication.

### Context before quotation

A quotation can be accurate word for word and still misrepresent its source. Review surrounding argument, population, conditions, definitions, and limitations before quoting or paraphrasing.

Do not use a sentence from an abstract as though it represents the full study. Do not quote an expert's memorable phrase when the surrounding discussion is more cautious. Preserve the distinction between what authors found, what they inferred, and what Healthy Little Minds recommends.

Direct quotation should be rare in public education. Plain-language paraphrase usually supports comprehension better, provided meaning and attribution remain accurate.

### Stewardship before publication

The responsibility for evidence begins before publication and continues afterward. Every evidence-based resource needs an owner, source record, review history, next review date, and route for correction.

Publishing without a maintenance plan creates future misinformation. Before approving content, ask whether the evidence can be monitored, links maintained, translated versions synchronized, and changes propagated across formats.

If a claim cannot be supported and maintained responsibly, reduce its scope or do not publish it.

## Evidence Hierarchy

No hierarchy can replace judgment. The best source depends on the question. Effectiveness, prevalence, lived experience, implementation, safety, development, cultural meaning, and policy may require different evidence.

### Systematic reviews

Systematic reviews use explicit methods to find, evaluate, and synthesize available studies. They are generally preferred for broad questions when current, well conducted, and relevant.

Review the search date, databases, inclusion criteria, risk-of-bias process, population, outcomes, heterogeneity, and certainty assessment. A review can be systematic but still contain weak or inapplicable studies. Publication date alone does not reveal how current the search is.

### Meta-analyses

Meta-analyses statistically combine results. They can clarify average effects and variation, but precision can be misleading when studies differ substantially or share bias.

Check whether pooling was appropriate, how effect sizes were defined, whether publication bias was assessed, and whether a statistically detectable effect is meaningful in daily life. Do not report one pooled number without its uncertainty and context.

### Clinical practice guidelines

High-quality guidelines can support statements about assessment, treatment, referral, safety, and professional practice. Prefer guidelines with transparent evidence review, multidisciplinary authorship, conflict disclosure, grading of recommendations, stakeholder involvement, and update processes.

Guidelines are jurisdictional and may reflect available services, cost, law, and policy. Healthy Little Minds may explain them but must not convert clinical guidance into individualized advice.

### Professional association guidance

Guidance from accountable psychology, health, education, and child-development organizations can support definitions, ethical principles, and practice recommendations. Evaluate how the guidance was produced, when it was updated, and whether it represents evidence synthesis, expert consensus, advocacy, or policy.

### Government publications

Government sources are preferred for current local laws, services, population statistics, school structures, public-health advice, and official procedures. Verify jurisdiction, effective date, version, and whether a document has been superseded.

Government publication does not guarantee scientific quality. Separate administrative requirements from empirical claims.

### Peer-reviewed journals

Peer review provides useful scrutiny but is not certification of truth. Individual studies should be evaluated for design, sample, measures, analysis, registration, transparency, conflicts, and replication.

Use original studies when they add necessary detail, address a specific question not covered by syntheses, or represent important emerging evidence. Avoid building a broad recommendation on one study.

### Educational psychology research

Educational evidence should be evaluated for classroom relevance, implementation, comparison conditions, educator training, outcome measurement, follow-up, and transfer beyond a controlled setting. Studies of test performance may not establish emotional wellbeing, and studies of engagement may not establish learning.

### Developmental psychology textbooks

Established textbooks can support foundational explanations and terminology. Use reputable, recent editions written or edited by qualified specialists. Verify fast-changing, contested, diagnostic, and intervention claims against current reviews or guidance.

Textbooks may describe dominant traditions while underrepresenting cultural variation or newer criticism. They should not be the sole basis for high-risk recommendations.

### Large-scale longitudinal studies

Longitudinal research can illuminate development and associations over time. Examine attrition, measurement changes, confounding, cohort context, and whether findings support prediction or causation.

Large samples reduce some uncertainty but do not automatically remove bias. A small association in a large study may be statistically precise yet practically limited.

### High-quality observational studies

Observational research is valuable when experiments are unethical, impossible, or inappropriate. It can identify patterns and generate plausible explanations. Confounding, selection, reverse causality, and measurement bias must remain visible.

Do not turn an observed association into advice that assumes causation.

### Qualitative research

Qualitative studies can provide essential evidence about experience, meaning, acceptability, culture, barriers, and implementation. Evaluate sampling, researcher reflexivity, analytic transparency, context, and the grounding of conclusions in data.

Qualitative findings should not be dismissed because they do not estimate frequency or causal effect. They should also not be used to make population prevalence claims.

### Books

Books may provide synthesis, history, theory, professional interpretation, or lived experience. Evaluate author expertise, publisher standards, references, review process, currency, and purpose. Popular books can help identify public language or examples but should not serve as primary evidence for important claims when stronger sources exist.

### Expert opinion

Expert opinion may guide interpretation where evidence is limited and can identify practical or ethical concerns. Record the expert's relevant expertise, conflicts, and basis for the view. Present opinion as opinion, not consensus or research finding.

### Conference papers and preprints

These sources may provide early access to developing research. They have not necessarily completed peer review and may change. Use only when timeliness is essential, label their status, avoid strong public recommendations, and monitor for final publication or withdrawal.

### Blogs, news articles, and social media

These may identify topics, experts, sources, public misconceptions, or lived perspectives. They are not primary scientific evidence. Trace claims to original research or official guidance. News summaries and social posts may omit limitations or misstate causality.

Social media can be cited as the object of analysis or as a direct public statement when relevant. It should not establish a psychological claim.

### Lived experience and testimonials

Lived experience is important for understanding impact, language, access, and meaning. It should be represented with consent and context. A personal outcome does not demonstrate general effectiveness. Testimonials must never substitute for evidence or conceal mixed outcomes.

## Acceptable Sources

Acceptability depends on the claim and the source's quality, authority, relevance, and transparency.

### Academic journals

Use legitimate journals with identifiable editorial practices, peer review, ethics expectations, and stable publication records. Be alert to deceptive journals, fabricated metrics, unusually broad scope, hidden fees, and claims of unrealistically rapid review.

Journal reputation should not replace article appraisal. Corrections, expressions of concern, and retractions must be checked.

### Universities

University-hosted research papers, evidence centers, repositories, and expert resources may be appropriate. A university domain does not make student essays, press releases, personal pages, or unreviewed teaching materials authoritative.

Prefer the original publication or transparent institutional report. Press releases can help locate research but should not support the final claim.

### Government agencies

Use responsible agencies for official statistics, services, public guidance, legal requirements, and education systems within their jurisdiction. Record the publication body, date, region, document title, and version.

### Professional organizations

Prefer organizations with relevant disciplinary authority, transparent governance, evidence methods, conflict policies, and revision processes. Distinguish formal guidance from a member blog or general awareness page.

### International organizations

International organizations can provide cross-national data, standards, reports, and implementation guidance. Examine country coverage, data comparability, definitions, publication date, and whether recommendations are designed for a particular resource context.

### Educational institutions

Schools, school systems, training institutions, and evidence organizations may supply curriculum guidance, implementation reports, or practical resources. Use them for context and examples when transparent. Do not treat local practice as proof of effectiveness.

### Evidence summaries

Evidence summaries can make a field easier to navigate. Prefer summaries with explicit search methods, evidence ratings, dates, authors, conflicts, and links to original sources. Verify important claims against the underlying material.

### Reference works

Authoritative dictionaries, diagnostic manuals, encyclopedias, standards, and statistical reference works may support definitions and classifications. Use the appropriate edition and clarify when a definition is clinical, legal, educational, or editorial.

### Sources generally unsuitable as primary evidence

The following should generally not serve as the primary basis for important psychological or educational claims:

- unsourced commercial pages;
- marketing materials and vendor-funded success stories;
- general news coverage;
- personal blogs;
- influencer content;
- social posts;
- anonymous webpages;
- search snippets;
- AI-generated summaries;
- crowd-edited pages without verification;
- student assignments;
- quotation collections;
- sources that cannot be retrieved or authenticated;
- articles that cite no evidence for the relevant claim.

These sources may support discovery, public-language analysis, or direct attribution in limited contexts. They cannot inherit credibility from a link alone.

## Source Evaluation

Every source used for a material claim should receive proportionate evaluation.

### Author expertise

Check whether authors have relevant education, research, professional, methodological, or lived expertise. Expertise should match the claim. A neuroscientist is not automatically an authority on classroom implementation; an experienced teacher is not automatically qualified to make clinical claims.

Look beyond titles. Consider institutional affiliations, publication history, declared roles, and whether expertise is current.

### Publication quality

Assess the publisher or journal's editorial standards, review process, transparency, correction policy, and reputation for integrity. Publication quality raises or lowers confidence but does not decide whether the specific work is sound.

### Peer review

Identify whether and how the source was reviewed. Peer review may detect errors but does not eliminate bias, selective reporting, fraud, or weak methods. Non-peer-reviewed guidance may still be authoritative for current services or law.

### Methodology

Ask whether the design can answer the question. Review recruitment, comparison groups, randomization where relevant, blinding, measures, timing, missing data, analysis, preregistration, and whether conclusions match results.

For qualitative work, review context, sampling, data collection, reflexivity, analytic approach, and supporting evidence. For reviews, examine search breadth and study appraisal.

### Sample size and representation

Sample size affects precision but not necessarily validity. A large biased sample remains biased. Examine who participated, who was excluded, who withdrew, and whether the intended audience resembles the sample.

Avoid treating research from one language, country, school system, age group, socioeconomic setting, or diagnostic population as universal.

### Conflict of interest

Record financial, professional, ideological, and product-related interests. An interest does not automatically invalidate a source, but it may affect design, analysis, publication, and interpretation.

Seek independent evidence for claims about commercial programmes or tools. Do not describe a creator's evaluation as independent.

### Funding

Identify who funded the work and the funder's role in design, data, analysis, publication, and writing. Public or charitable funding does not guarantee neutrality; commercial funding does not automatically invalidate findings. Transparency and independent replication matter.

### Replicability and convergence

Check whether findings have been replicated or align with other methods and sources. One failed replication does not always settle a question; one positive study does not establish a robust effect.

Confidence increases when different high-quality approaches converge and decreases when results depend on one team, measure, analytic choice, or unusually specific setting.

### Publication date

Assess date in relation to the topic's rate of change. Current guidelines and reviews are especially important for diagnosis, treatment-adjacent content, mental health terminology, crisis signposting, technology, and policy.

Do not use publication date as a quality shortcut. New sources may be preliminary; older sources may remain foundational.

### Relevance

A strong study can still be irrelevant. Confirm that source definitions, age, context, outcome, intervention, comparison, and duration match the claim. Indirect evidence should be labelled and should support cautious conclusions.

### Source evaluation record

For significant sources, record:

- full identity and stable locator;
- source type and publication status;
- population and setting;
- research question and method;
- main findings relevant to the claim;
- limitations and conflicts;
- applicability to Healthy Little Minds content;
- reviewer and review date;
- later correction, retraction, or update status.

The record should be concise enough to maintain and detailed enough for another reviewer to reconstruct the decision.

## Citation Principles

### Claim proximity

Place a citation close to the claim it supports. Readers should not have to guess which source applies to a paragraph. A citation at the end of a long paragraph may not support every sentence.

### Direct quotations

Quote when exact wording matters: a formal definition, historical statement, policy requirement, or distinctive first-person account. Use quotation marks, attribution, and a precise locator where available. Preserve context and observe copyright limits.

Do not quote merely because the original sounds authoritative. Paraphrase when it improves accessibility without changing meaning.

### Paraphrasing

Paraphrasing restates a source in original language and structure while preserving meaning. It still requires citation. Changing a few words is neither good paraphrase nor adequate originality.

Check that modality remains accurate. “May support” must not become “improves.” Do not omit the population, condition, or uncertainty that limits the claim.

### Summarizing

A summary represents the overall message of one or more sources. Cite the sources and explain disagreement or variation where material. Do not summarize only favourable findings from a mixed literature.

### Statistics

Statistics require a source, population, location, date or period, denominator, and definition where needed. Prefer original data or an accountable official compilation. Explain absolute numbers alongside relative changes when this affects interpretation.

Do not combine numbers from incompatible sources or repeat a statistic whose origin cannot be verified.

### Definitions

Cite technical, contested, diagnostic, legal, historical, or organization-specific definitions. Common everyday definitions may not need citation. When Healthy Little Minds adopts an editorial working definition, label it as such and show how it relates to established usage.

### Psychological concepts

Cite claims about mechanisms, development, prevalence, associations, risk, protective factors, and effectiveness. A concept's name may not require a citation every time after it is established, but substantial claims about it do.

### Historical information

Cite dates, origins, quotations, and claims about who developed a theory or practice. Avoid “first” and “invented” unless supported by historical scholarship; knowledge often develops across contributors and traditions.

### General knowledge

Stable, widely accepted facts that are not material to the argument may not need citation. When in doubt, ask whether a reasonable reader could question the claim, whether it changes action, and whether citation helps verification.

Do not overcite every sentence. Citation noise can make content harder to read and obscure the evidence that matters. Maintain an internal evidence map where visible references would overwhelm the audience.

### Secondary citation

Read and cite the original source whenever possible. If an original cannot be obtained and a secondary account is necessary, identify that limitation. Never imply direct review of a source not examined.

### Multiple sources

Group sources only when each supports the same claim. Order should follow the chosen house convention consistently. Do not add irrelevant citations to make a statement appear settled.

### Citation across formats

Articles and research summaries should provide visible references. Worksheets, stories, UI, and child-facing content may use an internal evidence record and link to an adult evidence note. Podcasts need show notes and transcripts with sources. Social and marketing claims should link to an accessible evidence page when full references do not fit.

## Currency of Evidence

### Classic theories

Classic works may be cited for historical origin or enduring concepts. Current understanding should be established through contemporary scholarship. Do not present an original theory as unchanged consensus when it has been revised, criticized, or culturally qualified.

### Living science

Psychology and education evolve. Measures improve, samples diversify, effects fail to replicate, and ethical expectations change. Content should reflect the current weight of evidence, not the date it was first drafted.

### Rapidly changing topics

Topics involving diagnostic standards, digital technology, social media, crisis pathways, policy, clinical guidance, AI, and emerging interventions may require frequent monitoring. Use shorter review periods and versioned official sources.

### Older evidence

Older sources remain appropriate when they are foundational, methodologically important, the best available evidence, or necessary for history. Confirm that later evidence has not overturned the relevant conclusion.

### Review schedules

Set review frequency according to risk, volatility, reach, and dependence on external guidance. A stable definition may need a longer interval than a high-traffic page about a changing mental health recommendation.

Every resource should record:

- publication and last evidence-review dates;
- next review date or review trigger;
- accountable owner;
- highest-risk claims;
- guidance or source families to monitor.

### Evidence retirement

Retire a source when it is retracted, superseded, methodologically discredited, no longer relevant, or replaced by stronger evidence. Retirement does not always mean deletion; historical discussion may retain it with clear context.

When a retired source supports a published claim, review the claim immediately. Replacing the reference without reconsidering the wording is insufficient.

## Psychology References

The Psychology Editorial Standard governs interpretation and communication. Referencing adds traceability and maintenance.

### Diagnostic language

Use current authoritative diagnostic or professional sources when explaining diagnostic concepts. State jurisdiction and edition where relevant. Do not convert criteria into self-diagnosis tools or cite a manual as evidence that one individual has a condition.

### Developmental psychology

Prefer contemporary syntheses and studies representing varied populations. Cite milestone claims and describe ranges. Avoid relying solely on theories developed from narrow historical samples.

### Social-emotional learning

Define the framework used, identify intended outcomes, and distinguish programme evaluation from broad claims about social-emotional learning. Examine implementation, cultural assumptions, comparison conditions, and who measured outcomes.

### Parenting

Avoid deriving universal recommendations from correlational studies or one cultural context. Distinguish parenting constructs used in research from moral labels. Include structural and child-level factors where they affect conclusions.

### Teaching

Evidence should match learner age, subject, setting, educator preparation, class conditions, and outcome. A strategy's effect under intensive research support may not transfer unchanged to routine classrooms.

### Neuroscience

Prefer primary research and credible reviews interpreted by qualified experts. Do not use brain images or terminology as proof of educational effectiveness. Neural association does not by itself identify a cause, diagnosis, or teaching method.

### Trauma

Use authoritative trauma research and professional guidance with careful definitions. Avoid sources that label all adversity or distress as trauma. Classroom recommendations must respect safeguarding and the boundary between trauma-informed education and treatment.

### Behaviour

Support claims about behaviour with developmental, educational, contextual, and where relevant behavioural evidence. Avoid sources that infer motives from labels or promote humiliation, coercion, or unsupported universal functions.

### Mental health

Use current systematic reviews, guidelines, epidemiology, and responsible professional sources. Prevalence depends on definition, measure, population, location, and time. Crisis and support information requires authoritative local sources and frequent verification.

## Educational References

Educational recommendations should be evidence-informed and context-aware.

### Teaching strategies

Examine learning outcome, comparison, duration, fidelity, teacher training, subject, age, and transfer. “Evidence-based” should not describe a strategy solely because it resembles a studied one.

### Classroom management

Evaluate effects on learning, safety, belonging, relationships, equity, and child dignity—not compliance alone. Consider whether discipline outcomes differ across groups and whether the strategy depends on resources unavailable to the intended users.

### Learning science

Distinguish robust principles from popular neuromyths and overextended laboratory findings. Cite appropriate syntheses. Avoid fixed learning styles, simplistic left-brain/right-brain claims, and statements that one activity “rewires” learning.

### Assessment

Reference validity, reliability, purpose, population, administration, and interpretation. A tool validated for research or screening may not support individual diagnosis, grading, or high-stakes decisions.

### Child development

Educational expectations should align with broad developmental evidence while allowing variation. Cite claims that define what children “should” do at an age and avoid converting averages into deadlines.

### Teacher wellbeing

Use evidence that considers workload, organizational climate, autonomy, support, discrimination, and system conditions. Do not cite individual coping research as proof that structural problems can be solved through self-care.

### Implementation evidence

Practical guidance should include evidence about feasibility, acceptability, adaptations, and sustained use where available. An effective intervention that cannot be implemented responsibly in the target context is not automatically an appropriate recommendation.

## AI Use

AI may assist evidence organization but is not a source, search authority, or reviewer.

AI may:

- organize supplied reference records;
- identify apparent duplicates;
- suggest search terms and potentially relevant literature for verification;
- help format citations from verified metadata;
- compare a draft against an approved evidence map;
- flag claims without nearby references;
- summarize a supplied source as a preliminary review aid.

AI may never:

- invent a citation, DOI, title, journal, author, date, quotation, or finding;
- complete missing metadata by guessing;
- cite a source that a human contributor has not opened and verified;
- misrepresent a paper's population, methods, result, or certainty;
- replace database searching, critical appraisal, or subject review;
- approve its own summary or reference list.

Plausible references are not verified references. Contributors must confirm metadata through the original publication and an authoritative record where possible. Verify that the document exists, the DOI resolves to it, the authors and date match, and the cited passage supports the claim.

AI-generated search suggestions may overrepresent common, English-language, highly cited, or accessible literature. Human researchers should seek current syntheses, contrary findings, diverse populations, and local evidence.

Record material AI involvement according to governance. Never upload copyrighted full text, confidential review notes, or personal data to an unapproved system.

## Link Management

Links support verification but are not the reference itself. Source metadata should remain sufficient if a URL changes.

### DOIs

Use the canonical DOI link for scholarly works when available. Verify that it resolves to the correct record. Do not invent a DOI or assume every article has one.

### Official publications

Link to the issuing body rather than a copied or third-party version. Record publication title, body, date, version, and jurisdiction. For downloadable reports, link to the landing page when it provides context and update information.

### Stable URLs

Prefer persistent identifiers, institutional repositories, versioned document pages, and stable official paths. Remove tracking parameters and session-specific links.

### Archived sources

Use reputable archives when an important original source is no longer available and archiving is lawful. Label archived links and retain enough citation detail to identify the original. An archive should not conceal that current guidance has been superseded.

### Broken links

Broken links should be monitored and triaged. Search for a moved official version before replacing the source. If the source cannot be recovered, determine whether the claim still has adequate support. Do not silently substitute an unrelated source.

### Versioned guidance

Record the exact version, update date, and jurisdiction used. Link to a version history where available. If a live page changes without stable versions, retain internal notes of the wording reviewed and monitor it more frequently.

### Future updates

Links should have an owner and checking schedule. High-risk support, crisis, safeguarding, policy, and diagnostic links require more frequent checks than stable historical sources.

### Accessible links

Use descriptive link text. Indicate file format and size where useful. Do not make users navigate an inaccessible source to understand the Healthy Little Minds content; provide an accessible explanation while linking to the original.

## Reference Maintenance

Evidence stewardship is an ongoing operational responsibility.

### Evidence records

Maintain a source inventory connected to claims and content assets. Records should show where a source is used, which claims depend on it, and which localized or derivative formats require updates.

### Monitoring

Monitor high-risk source families for new guidelines, systematic reviews, retractions, corrections, safety notices, and material debate. Monitoring intensity should match potential harm and rate of change.

### Scheduled review

At review, do not merely check whether links open. Reassess the question, search for stronger or contrary evidence, inspect current guidance, verify claims, and confirm that practical recommendations still follow.

### Triggered review

Review content early when:

- a source is retracted, corrected, or challenged;
- authoritative guidance changes;
- strong new evidence alters confidence;
- users or experts report possible harm;
- terminology or diagnostic standards change;
- a localization reveals cultural inapplicability;
- a linked service or policy changes;
- content reaches a new audience or use.

### Updating references

Replacing an old citation with a newer one requires checking the claim, not just the date. Update wording, examples, actions, metadata, summaries, marketing, and translations where the evidence change affects them.

### Corrections

Material corrections should be recorded with date, nature, and affected formats. Explain significant changes publicly where readers may have relied on earlier guidance. Minor link or formatting repairs can use internal records.

### Retirement and archiving

Retire content when its evidence base is no longer defensible, risk cannot be mitigated promptly, or maintenance ownership is absent. Archived content should be clearly labelled and removed from ordinary discovery when it could be mistaken for current guidance.

### Ownership

Every evidence-based asset needs a named owner. Ownership includes monitoring, responding to reports, coordinating review, propagating changes, and transferring responsibility explicitly when roles change.

## Review Workflow

Evidence moves through a traceable workflow:

**Research**

↓

**Source evaluation**

↓

**Writing**

↓

**Citation review**

↓

**Psychology review**

↓

**Accessibility review**

↓

**Localization review**

↓

**Publication**

↓

**Evidence monitoring**

↓

**Scheduled review**

### Research

Define the claim question, audience, context, outcome, potential harm, and evidence needed. Search beyond convenient or confirming sources. Record search dates, terms, databases or collections, exclusions, and known gaps proportionately to risk.

### Source evaluation

Assess quality, relevance, methods, population, conflicts, date, and source type. Create an evidence map linking proposed claims to sources and recording uncertainty or disagreement.

### Writing

Draft from the evidence map. Keep citations attached to claims, distinguish evidence from interpretation, and preserve qualifications in headings, summaries, visuals, and derivative copy.

### Citation review

An independent reviewer verifies that each source exists, was reviewed, supports the claim, is represented accurately, and is cited consistently. Check statistics, quotations, identifiers, links, versions, and copyright limits.

### Psychology review

A suitably qualified reviewer assesses interpretation, evidence strength, causal language, diagnostic boundaries, recommendations, and foreseeable harm. Citation accuracy does not by itself establish psychological responsibility.

### Accessibility review

Ensure references, evidence notes, links, tables, and superscripts remain understandable and navigable. Provide plain-language interpretation; do not make readers extract meaning from inaccessible source material.

### Localization review

Verify that evidence applies sufficiently to the target context, qualifications remain, terminology is controlled, and local guidance or services are current. Localized editions should link to accessible local or translated sources when appropriate without replacing stronger original evidence silently.

### Publication

Confirm source inventory, owner, evidence-review date, next review, disclosures, stable links, approvals, and propagation plan. Publish only the approved version.

### Evidence monitoring

Track designated sources, guideline families, and alerts according to risk. Record signals and determine whether they require immediate review or can wait for the scheduled cycle.

### Scheduled review

Reassess the evidence base, not only the document. Record the search, decisions, retained sources, replacements, wording changes, and new review date.

## Examples

### Parents Hub

**Poor referencing:** “Responsive parenting guarantees secure attachment,” followed by a link to a parenting blog.

**Adequate referencing:** “Responsive caregiving is associated with attachment security,” cited to one observational study.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Explain that attachment develops through patterns of relationship over time, cite a current high-quality synthesis, state that associations do not make one interaction determinative, and avoid promising an individual outcome.

### Teachers Hub

**Poor referencing:** “Brain breaks improve learning by 40%,” with no definition or source.

**Adequate referencing:** Cite one classroom study reporting improved on-task behaviour.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Define the activity and outcome, use a relevant review or multiple appropriate studies, distinguish on-task behaviour from learning, describe duration and classroom conditions, and offer a proportionate practical suggestion.

### Emotion pages

**Poor referencing:** “Anger comes from the amygdala,” linked to a popular neuroscience article.

**Adequate referencing:** Cite a neuroscience textbook for threat processing.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Describe anger as involving interacting brain, body, cognitive, relational, and contextual processes; support the explanation with current credible synthesis; and avoid presenting one brain structure as a complete cause.

### Will Talks

**Poor referencing:** A guest says, “Trauma is stored in the body,” and the episode repeats it as fact without review.

**Adequate referencing:** Show notes link to the guest's book.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Clarify the intended meaning during or after the conversation, distinguish metaphor from established mechanism, verify any factual claim using suitable evidence, provide sources and limitations in the show notes, and edit promotional clips so they do not overstate the discussion.

### Stories

**Poor referencing:** A child character uses one breathing exercise and is permanently cured of anxiety.

**Adequate referencing:** The story includes a general note that breathing can help.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** The story shows breathing as one option that helps the character in one moment, includes adult support and future practice, and maintains an internal evidence note for the educational approach. The adult guide explains that anxiety may require professional support.

### Research summaries

**Poor referencing:** List fifteen studies without explaining quality, disagreement, or relevance.

**Adequate referencing:** Summarize a systematic review and provide the citation.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** State the question and search scope, explain source quality and population, present direction and magnitude with uncertainty, describe limitations and conflicting evidence, connect conclusions to proportionate action, and provide a maintained reference list.

### Marketing

**Poor referencing:** “Scientifically proven to transform emotional intelligence.”

**Adequate referencing:** “Based on psychological research,” linked to a generic evidence page.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Describe the exact educational purpose and evidence basis without implying product-level effectiveness unless that product has been appropriately evaluated. Link to a specific accessible evidence note and state important limits.

### Social media

**Poor referencing:** “Children need 12 hugs a day for emotional health.”

**Adequate referencing:** Attribute the number to a named expert.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Do not publish an unsupported numerical rule. Explain that warm, responsive connection can take many forms and that children's preferences and needs differ; link to a reviewed resource.

### Statistics

**Poor referencing:** “Anxiety has increased by 200%.”

**Adequate referencing:** Link to a survey report.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Identify population, region, dates, measure, denominator, absolute rates, and changes in methods. Explain whether the data concern symptoms, diagnosis, service use, or self-report and what the survey cannot establish.

### Historical theory

**Poor referencing:** Cite only an original mid-century theory as current proof.

**Adequate referencing:** Cite the original theory and a modern textbook.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Use the original for historical attribution, current syntheses for present understanding, and relevant criticism or cross-cultural evidence where it materially changes interpretation.

## Referencing Checklist

### Claim identification

- [ ] Material psychological and educational claims are identified.
- [ ] Definitions, statistics, historical claims, and effectiveness statements are included.
- [ ] Claims in headings, captions, illustrations, audio, marketing, and metadata are reviewed.
- [ ] Internal evidence records exist where visible citations are unsuitable.
- [ ] The evidence needed is proportionate to risk and likely action.

### Source selection

- [ ] Current systematic reviews or guidelines were sought where appropriate.
- [ ] Sources match the population, setting, outcome, intervention, and duration.
- [ ] Quality is prioritized over citation quantity.
- [ ] Original sources are used where possible.
- [ ] Discovery sources are not presented as primary evidence.
- [ ] Contrary and null evidence has been considered.
- [ ] Cultural and regional applicability has been assessed.
- [ ] Commercial claims include independent evidence where available.

### Source evaluation

- [ ] Author expertise matches the claim.
- [ ] Publication and review processes are credible.
- [ ] Methodology can answer the research question.
- [ ] Sample size, representation, attrition, and exclusions are considered.
- [ ] Measures and outcomes are appropriate.
- [ ] Conflicts of interest and funding are recorded.
- [ ] Replication and convergence are considered.
- [ ] Publication date is appropriate to the topic.
- [ ] Corrections, concerns, and retractions have been checked.

### Claim support

- [ ] Each citation supports the exact attached claim.
- [ ] Causal language is supported by causal evidence.
- [ ] Group findings are not turned into individual predictions.
- [ ] Statistical and practical importance are distinguished.
- [ ] Recommendations do not exceed the evidence.
- [ ] Evidence, interpretation, policy, opinion, and experience are distinguished.
- [ ] Limitations and disagreement remain visible.

### Quotation and paraphrase

- [ ] Direct quotations are necessary, accurate, attributed, and in context.

- [ ] Summaries represent the full relevant evidence rather than selected findings.
- [ ] Secondary citation is disclosed when unavoidable.
- [ ] Copyright and quotation limits are respected.

- [ ] Every AI-suggested source was opened and verified by a human.

## Definition of Done

Evidence-based content is complete only when all applicable conditions below are met.

### Sources are appropriate

The evidence type, quality, population, setting, outcome, and currency match the claim. Stronger available evidence has not been ignored for convenience or preference.

### Claims are supported

Every material claim is mapped to evidence that directly supports its wording and scope. Recommendations follow proportionately from findings, context, benefits, burdens, and risks.

### Citations are accurate

Authors, title, date, publication, version, DOI, URL, quotations, statistics, and locators are verified. Citations appear close enough to identify the supported claim and are accessible in the relevant format.

### Evidence is current

Current reviews and guidance have been considered. Older sources remain only for a defensible purpose. Corrections, retractions, supersession, and credible new evidence have been checked.

### Links are functional

Links resolve to authentic, stable, appropriate sources. Broken-link repair has not silently altered the evidence basis. Source metadata remains useful if a link later fails.

### Psychology review is complete

A suitably qualified reviewer has assessed interpretation, uncertainty, diagnostic boundaries, developmental fit, recommendations, and foreseeable harm according to risk.

### AI contributions are verified

No AI-generated reference, identifier, quotation, statistic, or summary is trusted without human verification against the original source. AI involvement has not replaced appraisal or approval.

### Future review is defined

The content has an evidence owner, version, last review, next review or trigger, monitoring plan where needed, and a route for correction and retirement.

A long reference list cannot compensate for weak support. A disclaimer cannot repair a misleading claim. If these standards cannot be met, revise, narrow, or remove the claim.

## Future Evolution

Evidence governance should evolve as psychology, education, publishing, and technology develop. New research methods, open-science practices, data standards, living guidelines, automated monitoring, and multilingual evidence discovery may improve stewardship. They do not remove the need for human judgment.

Healthy Little Minds should increasingly support transparent claim-to-source mapping, reusable evidence notes, version-linked content, retraction and correction monitoring, and coordinated updates across products and languages. Systems should reduce administrative burden without turning evidence appraisal into a mechanical score.

Open materials, preregistration, registered reports, data availability, and replication can inform confidence, but none should become a single quality badge. Reviewers must still consider ethics, relevance, methods, interpretation, and inclusion.

Evidence discovery should broaden beyond English-language and highly cited literature. Local research, regional guidance, qualitative evidence, and community knowledge can reveal assumptions and implementation barriers. Inclusion must not weaken appraisal; it should improve the relevance of the question and the completeness of the evidence base.

When stronger evidence becomes available, Healthy Little Minds should be willing to revise previously published content, alter recommendations, withdraw claims, and explain why. Revision is a sign of stewardship, not failure.

The policy itself should receive periodic interdisciplinary review. Psychology, education, librarianship, accessibility, localization, research methods, ethics, and user perspectives all contribute to credible evidence governance.

## Closing Reflection

Every citation represents a promise to readers that Healthy Little Minds values truth over certainty, transparency over authority, and learning over appearance.

Publishing is not the end of responsibility—it is the beginning of stewardship.
