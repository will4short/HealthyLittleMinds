# Healthy Little Minds Readability Guidelines

## Purpose

This document is the definitive readability and comprehension handbook for Healthy Little Minds. It governs how written content is structured so that people of different ages, educational backgrounds, cognitive abilities, languages, and emotional states can understand, remember, and use what they learn.

Readability is not the removal of complexity from important ideas. Psychology, development, relationships, learning, and mental health are often complex. Readability removes unnecessary cognitive effort so readers can spend their attention on the idea itself rather than decoding the writer.

Readable content helps people find the relevant point, understand it accurately, connect it with what they already know, retain what matters, and choose an appropriate next step. It increases confidence without creating false certainty. It makes content more accessible without treating readers as less capable.

Readability is contextual. A familiar technical term may be efficient for a school psychologist but create a barrier for a parent. A short sentence may help a young child but feel abrupt in a reflective podcast script. A detailed explanation may support a teacher preparing a lesson and overwhelm the same teacher during a difficult classroom moment.

This handbook applies to the Emotion Library, Parents Hub, Teachers Hub, stories, interactive stories, worksheets, audiobooks, Will Talks, website pages, dashboards, interface text, marketing, emails, notifications, AI-assisted content, and future mobile applications.

It complements the editorial, voice, psychology, localization, accessibility, design, and typography standards. It does not replace them. Clear content must still be psychologically accurate, culturally appropriate, emotionally responsible, accessible, and useful. Simplification that removes a safety condition or changes the evidence is not readable; it is incorrect.

No single reading level, sentence target, or formula can approve content. Readability is demonstrated when the intended audience can find, understand, remember, and apply the message with reasonable effort.

## Readability Philosophy

### Clarity before complexity

Begin with the simplest accurate explanation. Add complexity when it changes understanding, protects against misunderstanding, or supports action. Do not begin with every exception, theoretical history, or specialist debate.

Clarity does not mean presenting one cause where several are possible. It means organizing complexity. A clear explanation can say, “This behaviour can have many causes,” then group the most relevant possibilities and show what to observe next.

Writers should distinguish necessary complexity from inherited complexity. Necessary complexity comes from the subject. Inherited complexity comes from academic habits, policy language, source phrasing, or organizational terminology. The latter should be rewritten.

### Understanding before sophistication

Sophisticated language is not evidence of sophisticated thinking. A reader should not need to admire the prose to trust the content. Use the most familiar accurate words, explain technical terms, and make relationships between ideas visible.

**Sophisticated but unclear:** Affective dysregulation may precipitate maladaptive behavioural manifestations.

**Clear:** Strong emotions can make it harder for a child to pause, process language, and choose what to do next.

The clear version is not less professional. It is more precise about what may happen.

### Learning before impressing

Healthy Little Minds writes to help readers learn, not to demonstrate the writer's knowledge. Include evidence, terminology, and detail because the reader needs them. Remove facts that are merely interesting if they interrupt the learning path.

A writer who understands a topic deeply can choose what the audience needs now, what can wait, and what belongs in a related resource. Restraint is part of expertise.

### Cognitive ease before density

Dense content asks readers to hold too many ideas in working memory. Use meaningful chunks, visible hierarchy, examples near the relevant principle, and summaries after complex sections. Present choices in groups and steps in order.

Cognitive ease does not mean removing productive effort from learning. Reflection, retrieval, and practice may require thought. The aim is to remove effort caused by poor wording, hidden structure, inconsistent labels, and overloaded presentation.

### Respect before assumption

Do not assume what readers know, how they live, how quickly they read, or why they need help. Define necessary terms without sounding patronizing. Provide context without explaining ordinary experience as though it belongs only to experts.

Respect means trusting readers with uncertainty and meaningful choice. It also means recognizing that stress, disability, language, time, and device constraints affect comprehension. Difficulty reading a page is often a design problem, not a reader failure.

## Reading Audiences

Audience categories guide decisions; they do not describe every individual. Development, language, disability, prior knowledge, culture, and emotional state create variation within each group. Important content should be reviewed with representative users.

### Young Children (4–6)

Young children usually benefit from adult-supported reading, concrete language, short sequences, repetition, rhythm, and visible action. Build from familiar experiences such as waiting, losing a turn, saying goodbye, asking for help, or feeling something in the body.

Use:

- one main idea per sentence;
- familiar verbs and concrete nouns;
- short instructions in the order they happen;
- repeated key words rather than many synonyms;
- illustrations that clarify rather than decorate;
- choices an adult can genuinely honour;
- opportunities to point, move, draw, or listen.

Avoid abstract definitions, long explanations, idioms, sarcasm, rhetorical questions, and demands for private disclosure. Introduce an unfamiliar emotion word through a situation and action.

**Less readable:** Frustration occurs when progress toward a desired outcome is obstructed.

**Suitable:** You want the block tower to stay up. It falls again. You might feel frustrated.

Do not make every sentence extremely short. Read-aloud writing needs flow. Repetition should support memory or anticipation, not fill space.

### Children (7–10)

Children in this range can often follow longer sequences, compare perspectives, learn more precise emotion words, and reflect on causes and choices. They still benefit from concrete examples, visible structure, and adult support for sensitive or unfamiliar topics.

Use direct explanation followed by an example. Limit the number of new terms introduced at once. Ask open questions with more than one acceptable answer. State that a child may be unsure or may choose not to share.

**Suitable:** Anger can tell us that something feels unfair, blocked, or wrong. The feeling is not bad. What matters is choosing an action that keeps people safe.

Avoid turning emotional learning into a vocabulary test. A child can understand an experience without selecting the expected word.

### Pre-teens

Pre-teens can engage with greater nuance, competing perspectives, social context, and self-reflection. They are sensitive to language that feels childish, artificial, or moralizing.

Use respectful directness, authentic scenarios, and meaningful choice. Explain why a skill may help rather than instructing compliance. Include peer relationships, identity, privacy, school pressure, online life, and growing independence where relevant.

Technical terms can be introduced when useful, but connect them to everyday meaning. Do not use simplified brain claims to make content sound scientific. Avoid assuming that all pre-teens want to discuss emotions openly.

### Parents and caregivers

Parents often read while busy, tired, worried, or seeking an immediate answer. Put the central point and practical orientation early. Explain what they may notice, possible meanings, what they can try, and when more support may be useful.

Use plain language without removing developmental or psychological nuance. Separate general information from individualized judgment. Make examples realistic and avoid implying that responsible caregiving requires unlimited time, money, patience, or private space.

Use headings that match likely questions:

- Why might this be happening?
- What can I try today?
- What if this does not help?
- When should I seek support?

Avoid long theoretical openings, blame, perfectionism, and repeated disclaimers that obscure the guidance.

### Teachers

Teachers need content that is easy to scan and detailed enough to implement. Surface the objective, age or phase, context, preparation, time, materials, steps, adaptations, cautions, and reflection route.

Use educational terminology when it supports professional use, but define psychology terms. Connect explanations to classroom observation and language. A teacher should be able to distinguish what can be used tomorrow from background theory and optional extension.

Respect time constraints without reducing guidance to disconnected tips. A concise rationale helps teachers adapt the method without losing its purpose.

### School Leaders

School leaders need summaries that support decisions, implementation, staff communication, and evaluation. Begin with the issue, recommended direction, evidence strength, resource implications, risks, and next decision.

Use clear sections and tables for genuine comparison. Distinguish classroom actions from organizational responsibilities. Avoid burying staffing, accessibility, safeguarding, or maintenance requirements in appendices.

Leaders may need more systemic detail than classroom teachers, but executive summaries must not omit qualifications that could change a decision.

### Mental Health Professionals

Professionals may expect precise terminology, evidence detail, definitions, references, and scope. Professional content can be denser, but clarity remains mandatory.

Use established terms consistently. Explain how Healthy Little Minds operationalizes a concept in education. Distinguish empirical evidence, interpretation, product decisions, and practical examples. Avoid teaching professionals concepts they already know unless the definition establishes a specific project usage.

Layer content so professionals can inspect methods and limits without forcing general readers through every detail. Professional review notes should remain readable to interdisciplinary collaborators.

### General Public

General-audience content should assume interest but not specialist training. Define the topic early, use common examples, and explain why it matters. Avoid assumptions about parenting, teaching, diagnosis, school systems, or one cultural setting.

Offer clear routes to audience-specific guidance. A general overview should not attempt to contain every parent, teacher, child, and professional application on one page.

## Reading Levels

Reading complexity should follow audience, task, risk, format, and context—not a rigid grade level. Numerical readability estimates cannot determine whether a psychology explanation is accurate, respectful, or understandable.

### Emotion Pages

Lead with a plain definition and familiar experience. Use short sections for possible body signals, situations, expression, support, and related emotions. Present signs as possibilities, not universal indicators.

Child-facing summaries should be concrete. Adult sections may add nuance and guidance. Avoid placing clinical distinctions in the first paragraph unless they are essential to prevent harm.

### Stories

Reading complexity should fit the intended independent or shared-reading context. Use narrative action and dialogue to carry meaning rather than explaining the lesson repeatedly. Character motivation can remain partially open for discussion.

Read stories aloud during review. Consider word familiarity, sentence rhythm, page turns, illustration support, repeated phrases, emotional intensity, and whether the listener must remember too many characters or events.

### Parents Hub

Use accessible public language with layered detail. Begin with the immediate concern and a balanced orientation. Use examples and steps, followed by deeper psychology and evidence where helpful.

Parents should not need clinical vocabulary to understand whether a concern may need professional discussion.

### Teachers Hub

Use professional but plain language. Make classroom use scannable without separating actions from rationale. Technical educational terms may be appropriate when common to the intended region; localization review must verify them.

### Research Summaries

Begin with the question, what the evidence suggests, and why it matters. Then explain study type, population, findings, limitations, uncertainty, and practical implications.

Do not simplify by removing denominators, context, or the distinction between association and causation. Use a glossary or evidence note when technical detail is necessary.

### Will Talks

Spoken language should sound natural, with shorter clauses, clear transitions, and periodic summaries. Introduce unfamiliar terms before sustained discussion. Hosts should signpost changes of topic and restate visually presented information for audio-only listeners.

Transcripts should be readable documents, not unedited walls of speech. Preserve meaning and speaker character while adding headings, paragraph breaks, speaker labels, and links.

### User Interface

Interface text should be concise because users are acting, not because every label must be one word. Buttons should name outcomes. Helper text should prevent errors. Error messages should explain recovery.

Do not remove essential context to fit a component. Redesign the component or provide progressive help.

### Marketing

Marketing should remain immediately understandable and factually proportionate. State audience, purpose, format, benefit, requirements, and limits. Avoid cleverness, hype, compressed psychology claims, and calls to action that depend on fear.

### Downloads

Worksheets, posters, cards, and guides should match their actual user. Instructions for adults should not be placed in child-facing language without clear separation. Use visible sequence, enough writing space, and alternatives to reading or writing where appropriate.

## Sentence Construction

Sentence design controls how much information a reader must hold and how clearly relationships appear.

### Sentence length

Prefer short and moderate sentences. Use longer sentences when the relationship between ideas would become less clear if split. Vary rhythm so text does not feel mechanical.

Do not apply a fixed word limit. A short sentence can still be difficult: “Executive dysfunction impairs metacognition.” A longer sentence can be clear when familiar words and logical order guide the reader.

**Poor:** In circumstances where a child is experiencing emotional dysregulation, the adult, having first considered the potential antecedents and environmental variables, should initiate an appropriate co-regulatory response.

**Better:** When a child is overwhelmed, consider what happened and what the environment is demanding. Then offer calm support.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** When a child is overwhelmed, begin with safety and connection. Use fewer words, reduce unnecessary demands, and consider what may have happened. Return to reflection and problem-solving when the child is more settled.

### Active voice

Active voice usually makes responsibility and sequence clear.

**Passive:** The cards should be distributed before the activity is begun.

**Active:** Give each child a card before you begin the activity.

Passive voice is acceptable when the actor is unknown or less important: “The resource was updated in July 2026.” Do not use it to hide accountability.

### One idea per sentence

Give each sentence one main job. Closely related conditions can remain together, but avoid stacking explanation, caution, example, and action in one sentence.

**Dense:** Anxiety, which is common in childhood but may become a concern when persistent, can cause avoidance, so parents should encourage gradual participation while ensuring that they do not force the child or provide too much reassurance.

**Clearer:** Worry is common in childhood. It may need more attention when it persists or limits daily life. Encourage gradual participation without forcing the child. Offer support while helping them take a manageable step.

### Paragraph length

A paragraph should develop one central idea. Most digital paragraphs should be visually manageable, especially on mobile. Short paragraphs can orient or emphasize; longer paragraphs can explain a connected argument.

Do not split every sentence into its own paragraph. Excessive fragmentation hides relationships and increases scrolling. Break when the subject, purpose, time, speaker, or action changes.

### Transition words

Use transitions to make reasoning visible: “because,” “for example,” “however,” “as a result,” “first,” “in contrast,” and “when.” Prefer the simplest natural connector.

**Unclear:** The routine is predictable. Some children still find transitions difficult.

**Clear:** The routine is predictable. However, some children may still find the transition difficult because the activity is ending.

Avoid formal transitions such as “notwithstanding the foregoing” and repetitive signposting that makes prose feel instructional rather than helpful.

### Concrete language

Replace broad abstractions with observable actions, situations, and examples.

**Abstract:** Provide emotional support.

**Concrete:** Sit nearby, use a steady voice, and say, “I am here. We can talk when you are ready.”

Concrete examples illustrate options, not universal scripts. Preserve room for culture, communication preference, and context.

### Avoid ambiguity

Make pronoun references, time, sequence, responsibility, and conditions explicit.

**Ambiguous:** Talk to the teacher after they calm down.

**Clear:** After the child is calmer, speak privately with the teacher.

Avoid words such as “this,” “it,” and “they” when more than one referent is possible. Clarify whether “may” expresses permission or possibility.

### Positive construction

State what to do, not only what to avoid.

**Negative only:** Do not ask too many questions.

**Actionable:** Ask one open question, then allow time for an answer.

Negative wording remains appropriate for clear prohibitions: “Do not promise to keep a safeguarding disclosure secret.”

### Lists inside sentences

Do not hide long lists in prose. Use bullets when items are parallel and numbering when order matters. Keep introductory grammar consistent with each item.

## Vocabulary

### Common vocabulary

Prefer familiar, precise words. “Use” is usually better than “utilize”; “before” is better than “prior to.” Familiarity reduces decoding but should not make language vague.

Repeat an important term when synonyms could suggest different concepts. In educational content, controlled repetition supports learning.

### Technical vocabulary

Use a technical term when it helps readers understand professional communication, search for reliable information, or distinguish concepts. Introduce it after or alongside an everyday explanation.

A useful pattern is:

1. describe the experience;
2. name the term;
3. give an example;
4. explain why it matters;
5. reuse the term consistently.

Avoid defining one unfamiliar term with several others.

### Psychological terminology

Psychology terms require the scope and evidence standards in `PSYCHOLOGY_EDITORIAL_STANDARD.md`. Do not use diagnosis-adjacent words as casual synonyms. Explain that concepts may describe patterns rather than fixed traits.

### Educational terminology

Use terms familiar to the intended educators and region. Explain acronyms at first use. If a role or school phase varies internationally, describe its function. Do not let institutional language enter family or child content without translation into everyday meaning.

### Word choice and emotional load

Words such as “failure,” “warning sign,” “damage,” “normal,” “problem,” and “risk” can carry more force than intended. Use them only when accurate and explain the comparison or threshold.

Prefer observable descriptions over labels. “Leaves their seat during independent work” is more useful than “is disruptive.”

### Consistency

Follow canonical terminology across headings, navigation, articles, worksheets, audio, and localization. Variation for style should not change conceptual meaning. If a child-facing synonym is needed, connect it to the canonical term.

## Explaining Psychology

Psychology explanations should move from experience to concept to application. Begin with what someone may notice. Name and define the concept. Show a realistic example. State important limits. Offer a proportionate next step.

### Emotion regulation

**Technical starting point:** Emotion regulation comprises intrinsic and extrinsic processes that monitor and modify emotional reactions.

**Everyday understanding:** Emotion regulation is how people notice and respond to feelings. It can include changing a situation, asking for help, moving the body, using words, or waiting for a strong feeling to pass.

**Necessary limit:** Regulation is not the same as staying calm or hiding emotion. Children often regulate with adult support.

### Executive function

**Everyday understanding:** Executive functions are mental processes that help us hold information in mind, pause before acting, shift attention, and plan a next step.

**Example:** A child may know a classroom rule but struggle to remember and use it while changing activities.

**Necessary limit:** Executive function is not one skill, and difficulty does not establish a diagnosis or lack of effort.

### Attachment

**Everyday understanding:** Attachment describes how children seek safety and comfort through close relationships and how patterns of care shape expectations over time.

**Necessary limit:** Do not turn attachment into fixed parenting labels or infer it from one interaction. No caregiver responds perfectly, and repair matters.

### Resilience

**Everyday understanding:** Resilience describes adaptation during or after difficulty. It is supported by relationships, resources, skills, opportunities, and wider systems.

**Necessary limit:** Resilience is not a fixed trait or a reason to expect people to manage adversity alone.

### Anxiety

**Everyday understanding:** Anxiety can involve worry, fear, body changes, and urges to avoid. Some anxiety is part of ordinary life.

**Necessary limit:** A disorder cannot be identified from one feeling or behaviour. Persistence, intensity, distress, and daily impact matter, and assessment belongs to qualified professionals.

### Co-regulation

**Everyday understanding:** Co-regulation is the support one person gives another during strong emotion. An adult might slow their voice, reduce demands, stay nearby, or offer a simple choice.

**Necessary limit:** Co-regulation is not control, and the helpful response varies by person and moment.

### Self-awareness

**Everyday understanding:** Self-awareness is noticing aspects of our thoughts, feelings, body signals, actions, strengths, and needs.

**Necessary limit:** People do not always know what they feel immediately. Reflection should allow uncertainty and privacy.

### Layering explanations

Use progressive disclosure:

- a one-sentence definition;
- an everyday example;
- practical meaning;
- caveat or boundary;
- optional evidence detail;
- related resources.

Do not place every technical qualification in the first sentence. Do not hide action-changing qualifications at the end.

## Information Architecture

Information architecture makes the content's logic visible.

### Headings

Use one clear page title and descriptive headings in a logical hierarchy. Headings should help readers predict what follows and navigate with assistive technology.

Prefer “What to do when worry interrupts sleep” to “A cloud at bedtime.” Creative titles may accompany a descriptive subtitle in stories, not replace functional headings in guidance.

### Subheadings

Break long sections by question, task, stage, or concept. Do not create a subheading for every paragraph. Similar sections across a series should use predictable labels.

### Lists

Use bullets for related unordered points, numbered lists for sequence, and checklists for verification. Keep items parallel. Introduce what the list represents and avoid nesting beyond easy comprehension.

### Examples

Place an example near the principle it explains. Include enough context to understand the choice. Use diverse examples without linking identity to difficulty or presenting fictional outcomes as evidence.

### Callout boxes

Use callouts for distinct information such as “Try this,” “Safety note,” “Remember,” or “Evidence note.” Limit their number. Essential content should not depend on colour, position, or optional expansion.

### Reflection prompts

Prompts should focus attention and support future action. Ask one question at a time. Avoid questions that imply blame or require disclosure.

**Poor:** Why did you handle the situation badly?

**Readable and respectful:** What made the situation difficult? What support could help next time?

### Step-by-step guidance

State preparation first, number actions in order, use action verbs, and explain exceptions where they occur. Include a recovery route if the expected result does not happen.

### Tables

Use tables for genuine comparison or repeated field relationships. Keep headings clear and cells concise. Do not use tables for long narrative text or to force layout. Provide an alternative when a complex table is difficult on mobile or for screen readers.

### Summaries

Use a beginning summary for long or urgent content and an ending summary for retention and action. A summary should preserve the main qualification, not turn nuanced content into an absolute claim.

### Page sequence

A substantial resource commonly follows:

1. what this is and who it helps;
2. the immediate answer or orientation;
3. explanation and context;
4. realistic examples;
5. practical steps and adaptations;
6. limits and support routes;
7. summary and related next steps.

Adapt the sequence to the task. Do not delay urgent safety information for narrative effect.

## Learning Design

Readable information is not automatically learned. Content should help readers encode, retrieve, practise, and apply ideas.

### Chunking

Group information into meaningful units. A chunk should have a clear purpose and manageable number of elements. Group emotion strategies by function—connection, movement, sensory support, language—rather than presenting an undifferentiated list.

### Retrieval

Invite readers to recall rather than only reread. Use low-pressure questions such as “Which early sign would you be most likely to notice?” Retrieval should not resemble a high-stakes test.

### Repetition

Repeat core language across formats and over time. Purposeful repetition strengthens memory; superficial duplication increases length. Repeat the concept in a new example or application rather than copying the same paragraph.

### Examples

Use worked examples before independent application. Compare examples and non-examples where distinctions matter. Include imperfect situations and adaptation, not only ideal outcomes.

### Reflection

Reflection connects information to experience. Prompts should be specific, optional where personal, and oriented toward observation and next steps.

### Practice

Provide a small action that fits the context. Explain what successful practice means and normalize repetition. Completion is not mastery.

### Review

Use brief summaries, recurring terminology, printable reminders, and return points. Tell readers when and why to revisit a resource.

### Cross-linking

Link to resources that answer a likely next question, offer practice, or adapt guidance for another audience. Explain why the link is useful. Avoid promotional clusters that divide attention.

### Transfer

Help readers recognize when a skill applies elsewhere. After one worked example, offer a second context and ask what remains the same. State the principle behind the activity so users can adapt it without losing its purpose.

## Emotional Readability

Emotional state affects attention, memory, interpretation, and capacity to choose. Content for distressing moments should reduce demand and avoid emotional escalation.

### Anxious readers

Provide predictable structure, concrete facts, and a small next step. Do not introduce unlikely risks merely to be comprehensive. Avoid false reassurance.

**Instead of:** Do not worry; everything will be fine.

**Use:** Begin with what you know now. If the concern persists or affects daily life, speak with an appropriate professional.

### Stressed readers

Put immediate actions first. Separate “Do now,” “Consider next,” and “Read when you have time.” Avoid long choice lists and unnecessary account or navigation steps.

### Overwhelmed readers

Reduce the number of decisions. Show progress, allow saving and return, and state what can wait.

**Use:** You do not need to complete every step today. Start with the first one and return when you are ready.

### Sad readers

Use gentle direct language. Do not rush toward positivity, lessons, or productivity. Allow space and provide manageable support options.

### Frustrated readers

Name the obstacle and restore agency. In errors, explain whether work is safe and how to recover. Do not blame the reader or use playful failure messages.

### Confused readers

Take responsibility for clarity. Restate the purpose, define the term, show an example, and offer help. Do not refer readers back to the same unclear wording.

### Sensitive topics

Use specific content notes where they help informed choice. Put urgent support information where it is needed, not only in a footer. Avoid repeated alarming warnings. Readers should be able to leave, pause, or choose another format without losing work.

## Accessibility

The Accessibility Standard governs all formats. Readability contributes to cognitive, sensory, linguistic, and educational access.

### Plain language

Use familiar vocabulary, visible logic, direct instructions, and defined terms. Plain language is compatible with accurate complexity and professional audiences.

### Screen readers

Use semantic headings, lists, landmarks, tables, labels, and link text. Avoid directional phrases such as “see the box on the right.” Write alt text according to image purpose and ensure dynamic messages identify the next action.

### Cognitive accessibility

Keep navigation and labels predictable. Limit simultaneous choices. Use progressive disclosure, summaries, memory supports, and consistent terminology. Avoid timed reading and unexpected content changes.

### Dyslexia

Support readable typography, spacing, manageable line length, left alignment for long Latin-script text, clear hierarchy, and uncluttered pages. Do not rely on a special typeface as a complete solution. Avoid italics and capitals for long emphasis.

### ADHD and attention differences

Make the purpose and next step visible. Break long tasks into meaningful stages, preserve progress, reduce competing elements, and use reminders that support rather than shame. Do not fragment content so heavily that the overall relationship disappears.

### Multilingual readers

Define idiomatic and technical language, use explicit references, and avoid unnecessary cultural assumptions. Audio, examples, glossaries, and translated resources can support comprehension. Limited proficiency is not limited intelligence.

### Printable resources

Use clear instructions, enough space, logical reading order, strong contrast, and printer-safe presentation. Separate adult guidance from child activity. Test grayscale, enlargement, ordinary printers, and local paper sizes.

### Multiple modes

Offer text, audio, visual explanation, demonstration, or guided practice where these improve access. Every mode must preserve meaning; an infographic should not become a less complete version of the article.

## Localization

The Localization Guidelines govern adaptation. Readability must be evaluated in the target language, not inferred from the source.

### Translated readability

A clear source can become formal, dense, ambiguous, or unnatural in translation. Target-language editors should rewrite for local comprehension while preserving meaning, qualifications, and voice. Back-translation cannot establish readability.

### Text expansion

Allow natural phrasing and explanation. Do not shorten a translation by removing meaning or shrink text to preserve the source layout. Components and documents should adapt.

### Idioms

Replace idioms with direct meaning or a locally natural equivalent that carries the same tone and no unintended implication. Instructional meaning should never depend on wordplay.

### Reading direction

Future right-to-left editions require mirrored flow where appropriate, correct ordering of mixed-direction text, and native review. Information sequence should remain logical rather than visually copied from a left-to-right design.

### Mixed-language content

Explain unavoidable foreign terms and mark language changes for assistive technology. Review pronunciation, line breaks, punctuation, emphasis, and visual density when scripts mix.

### Cultural comprehension

An unfamiliar school role, family routine, or metaphor increases cognitive load even when translated accurately. Adapt examples and institutional references so readers can apply the guidance. Preserve the psychological principle and document material changes.

## AI Contribution

AI may help identify readability concerns and propose alternatives. It remains an untrusted drafting tool.

AI may:

- suggest simpler accurate wording;
- identify long or overloaded sentences;
- locate dense paragraphs and unclear references;
- propose headings, summaries, and examples;
- compare terminology across a document;
- reorganize supplied content into a clearer sequence;
- flag missing transitions or unexplained terms.

AI may never:

- remove evidence limitations, safety conditions, or diagnostic boundaries;
- replace a technical term with an inaccurate everyday word;
- invent an example that changes the claim;
- assume a reading age from vocabulary alone;
- decide cultural or developmental suitability without human review;
- publish or approve its own revision.

Review AI simplification line by line against the approved source. Common failures include turning “may” into “will,” changing association into causation, removing exceptions, adding generic reassurance, and making professional content sound childish.

Prompts should identify audience, task, emotional context, protected terms, required qualifications, and relevant governance documents. Human audience, psychology, accessibility, and localization review remains mandatory.

## Examples

### Emotion pages

**Poor:** Anger constitutes an affective response to perceived provocation or goal obstruction.

**Better:** Anger may happen when something feels unfair or gets in the way.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Anger may arise when something feels unfair, threatening, painful, or blocked. You might notice heat, tight muscles, faster thoughts, or an urge to act. People experience anger differently, and the feeling does not decide what you must do.

### Parents Hub

**Poor:** Parents should facilitate the development of adaptive coping mechanisms.

**Better:** Help your child learn useful ways to cope.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Help your child practise one way to respond when feelings become strong. You might offer movement, quiet, words, or connection. Notice what helps this child in this moment rather than expecting one strategy to work every time.

### Teachers Hub

**Poor:** Implement a multimodal regulatory intervention prior to behavioural escalation.

**Better:** Use regulation strategies before behaviour becomes difficult.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Teach and practise support options when the class is settled. Before a difficult transition, remind children of available choices, such as movement, a visual cue, or asking for help. Review which option was practical and useful.

### Will Talks

**Poor:** In today's instalment, we shall elucidate the multifactorial determinants of resilience.

**Better:** Today we discuss what helps children become resilient.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Today we explore resilience—not as a trait children either have or lack, but as adaptation supported by relationships, resources, skills, and opportunity. We will consider what adults can influence and what requires wider support.

### Stories

**Poor:** Maya experienced emotional dysregulation after her peer invalidated her perspective.

**Better:** Maya became upset when Ben said her idea was silly.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** “That idea is silly,” Ben said. Maya's face felt hot. She wanted to shout. She pressed her feet into the floor and looked for Ms Chen.

The story shows the experience through action rather than explaining it with adult terminology.

### Worksheets

**Poor:** Identify and document the antecedents and physiological correlates of your emotional episode.

**Better:** Write what happened before the feeling and what you noticed in your body.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** What happened first? What did you notice next? You can write, draw, choose a picture, tell a trusted adult, or skip a question.

### User Interface

**Poor:** Authentication unsuccessful due to invalid credentials.

**Better:** Your login details are incorrect.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** We could not log you in with those details. Check them and try again, or reset your password.

### Emails

**Poor:** We are delighted to announce the commencement of our transformative emotional intelligence resource series.

**Better:** Our new emotional learning resources are now available.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** New emotion activities are available for families and classrooms. Each activity includes a clear purpose, practical steps, and options for different learners.

### Research summary

**Poor:** The statistically significant result proves that the intervention works.

**Better:** The study found an improvement after the intervention.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Children in the programme improved more on the study's attention measure than children in the comparison group. The study was small and short, so we do not yet know whether the effect is meaningful in everyday classrooms or lasts over time.

### Notification

**Poor:** Congratulations! You have successfully mastered emotional regulation!

**Better:** You completed the regulation activity.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Activity complete. Review what you noticed or choose another practice. Completion is a step in learning, not proof of mastery.

### Marketing

**Poor:** Unlock the revolutionary secret to calmer children today.

**Better:** Explore activities that support calm.

**Healthy Little Minds standard:** Explore practical activities that help children notice strong feelings and try safe ways to respond. Each activity includes adult guidance and adaptation options.

## Readability Checklist

### Purpose and audience

- [ ] The content has one clear purpose and a defined primary audience.
- [ ] The reading context and likely emotional state have been considered.
- [ ] Complexity matches what the reader needs to understand and do.
- [ ] The main point appears before optional background.
- [ ] Child-facing content has received developmental review.

### Words and terms

- [ ] Familiar accurate words are preferred.
- [ ] Necessary technical terms are introduced naturally and defined.
- [ ] Psychology and education terms follow canonical usage.
- [ ] Acronyms are removed or explained at first use.
- [ ] Abstract nouns have concrete explanation or examples.
- [ ] Labels, metaphors, idioms, and euphemisms do not obscure meaning.
- [ ] Repeated terms remain consistent across the journey.

### Sentences and paragraphs

- [ ] Most sentences have one main idea.
- [ ] Sentence length and rhythm suit the audience and format.
- [ ] Active voice makes action and responsibility clear.
- [ ] Pronouns, conditions, time, and sequence are unambiguous.
- [ ] Transitions make relationships between ideas visible.
- [ ] Paragraphs develop one central point.
- [ ] The text is neither a wall of prose nor excessively fragmented.
- [ ] Negative instructions state the useful alternative where possible.

### Structure and scanning

- [ ] The title and introduction orient the reader quickly.
- [ ] Headings are descriptive and logically nested.
- [ ] Lists are parallel and used for a real purpose.
- [ ] Ordered actions use numbered steps.
- [ ] Examples appear near the ideas they explain.
- [ ] Callouts are limited, labelled, and accessible.
- [ ] Tables are used only when they improve comparison.
- [ ] Summaries preserve the main qualification.
- [ ] Urgent information is not delayed or hidden.

### Learning and memory

- [ ] Information is grouped into meaningful chunks.
- [ ] New ideas connect with familiar experiences.
- [ ] Key terminology is repeated with purpose.
- [ ] Worked examples precede difficult independent tasks.
- [ ] Reflection prompts are specific and non-judgmental.
- [ ] Practice is manageable and clearly explained.
- [ ] The reader is helped to retrieve and review key ideas.
- [ ] Cross-links support a likely next learning step.
- [ ] The content explains how a skill transfers to another context.

### Psychology and nuance

- [ ] Simplification preserves evidence strength and uncertainty.
- [ ] Association is not changed into causation.
- [ ] Individual and cultural variation remain visible.
- [ ] Diagnostic and educational boundaries remain intact.
- [ ] Examples do not turn possibilities into universal rules.
- [ ] Safety conditions and referral guidance remain clear.
- [ ] Technical accuracy has been reviewed after readability editing.

### Emotional readability

- [ ] The tone reduces rather than amplifies emotional load.
- [ ] Anxious readers receive concrete, proportionate information.
- [ ] Overwhelmed readers can identify one next step.
- [ ] Sadness and frustration are not rushed into positivity.
- [ ] Errors explain recovery without blame.
- [ ] Sensitive content offers informed choice and support.
- [ ] The content avoids fear, shame, guilt, and false reassurance.

### Accessibility

- [ ] Plain language, semantic structure, and descriptive links are used.
- [ ] Screen-reader navigation and reading order are correct.
- [ ] Cognitive load, memory, and attention needs are supported.
- [ ] Typography, spacing, contrast, zoom, and reflow support reading.
- [ ] Instructions do not depend only on visual position or colour.
- [ ] Multiple modes preserve equivalent meaning.
- [ ] Printable resources have been tested in realistic conditions.

### Localization

- [ ] Source language avoids unnecessary idioms and cultural assumptions.
- [ ] Target-language readability has been reviewed independently.
- [ ] Translation preserves nuance, limits, and emotional tone.
- [ ] Text expansion is accommodated without shrinking or truncation.
- [ ] Reading direction, line breaking, and mixed scripts work.
- [ ] Local examples and institutional terms support comprehension.
- [ ] Qualified human localization review is complete.

### Format and journey

- [ ] Interface labels describe actions and destinations.
- [ ] Forms prevent errors with clear labels and helper text.
- [ ] Emails communicate purpose and action early.
- [ ] Spoken content has clear transitions and summaries.
- [ ] Transcripts are structured for reading.
- [ ] Downloads separate adult guidance from child activity.
- [ ] Mobile readers can understand and act without excessive scrolling or context loss.

### AI and review

- [ ] AI suggestions have been checked against the approved meaning.
- [ ] No qualification, safety condition, or scope boundary was removed.
- [ ] AI-created examples are accurate, inclusive, and realistic.
- [ ] Audience, psychology, accessibility, and localization reviewers are recorded as required.
- [ ] Representative users have reviewed high-impact content where feasible.
- [ ] A named person is accountable for the final version.

Any unresolved issue that makes safety, psychological meaning, consent, action, or support pathways difficult to understand blocks publication.

## Definition of Done

Content is complete only when it is all of the following.

### Easy to understand

The intended reader can explain the main idea accurately without specialist help. Terms are defined, relationships are visible, and necessary nuance remains.

### Easy to scan

Readers can locate the relevant section, immediate action, limitation, and next step through descriptive hierarchy and predictable structure. Scanning does not replace coherent reading; it helps readers enter at the right point.

### Emotionally supportive

Language matches the likely emotional context, reduces unnecessary pressure, and preserves dignity and agency. It does not use fear, shame, blame, or false reassurance.

### Accessible

Structure, language, typography, media, interaction, and documents support varied cognitive, sensory, motor, literacy, and attention needs. Appropriate manual and assistive-technology testing is complete.

### Localized

The source is translation-ready, and every localized edition has been reviewed for natural comprehension, culture, script, layout, and local context. Readability is not assumed from linguistic accuracy alone.

### Educationally effective

The resource helps the reader organize, remember, reflect on, and apply learning. It includes realistic examples and a proportionate next step. Completion is not mistaken for mastery.

### Consistent with the Healthy Little Minds voice

The content remains calm, warm, human, respectful, curious, encouraging, practical, hopeful, evidence-informed, honest, and professional. It does not sound academic, childish, clinical, or mechanical merely to meet a reading target.

A page is not done because a formula labels it easy. It is not done because sentences are short. It is done when representative readers can understand and use it without important meaning being lost.

## Future Evolution

Readability standards should evolve through evidence and lived use. Healthy Little Minds should learn from user testing, behaviour patterns, teacher feedback, parent feedback, accessibility reviews, localization review, support requests, and content corrections.

User testing should ask people to find information, explain it in their own words, predict what an action will do, and apply guidance to a realistic scenario. Preference alone is insufficient; a page can feel easy while being misunderstood.

Analytics may identify where readers leave, repeat actions, abandon forms, search again, or fail to progress. These patterns are signals for investigation, not proof of comprehension or failure. Privacy-respecting qualitative research should explain why a pattern occurs.

Teachers can report whether resources are scannable under classroom conditions, whether instructions survive real implementation, and where children misunderstand. Parents can identify language that creates guilt, uncertainty, or impractical expectations. Disabled users and accessibility specialists can reveal barriers that generic testing misses.

Localization teams should feed insights back into source content. A phrase that is difficult to translate may expose hidden ambiguity. A layout that fails with another script may be insufficiently flexible. Readability improvement should flow in every direction, not only from English outward.

Changes should be documented and tested across related formats. A clearer canonical definition should update articles, worksheets, audio scripts, interface help, search terms, and localized editions. Avoid isolated improvements that create terminology drift.

New media and interfaces may change how people read, listen, and interact. The enduring test remains the same: can the intended person understand the meaning, retain what matters, and act with confidence without unnecessary cognitive effort?

## Closing Reflection

People should spend their mental effort understanding psychology—not decoding complicated writing.

Healthy Little Minds succeeds when readers feel more capable, more informed, and more confident after every page.
