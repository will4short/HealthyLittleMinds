# Healthy Little Minds Dashboard

## Purpose

The Healthy Little Minds Dashboard exists to help users continue learning rather than merely manage an account. It is a personalized learning home that organizes the wider Healthy Little Minds ecosystem around meaningful goals, recent context, accessibility needs, and appropriate next steps.

Its primary task is to help people answer:

> What should I do next?

It should not focus only on:

> What have I already done?

The Dashboard should reduce decision fatigue. Healthy Little Minds may contain emotion pages, stories, worksheets, audio, parent guidance, teacher resources, Will Talks, downloads, and future courses. The Dashboard helps a person find a small, relevant route through this material without turning the ecosystem into an overwhelming catalogue.

The Dashboard is not an administrative control panel, productivity tracker, social feed, diagnostic profile, or engagement engine. It should not reward users for spending more time on the platform, pressure them to return every day, or imply that emotional growth can be measured through completion alone.

Every recommendation, status, reminder, and progress representation should have an educational purpose. The interface should support understanding, practice, reflection, application, and return at the user's own pace.

The Dashboard serves children, parents, teachers, school counselors, members, and general learners. Their content, permissions, language, and priorities differ, but the experience should remain recognizably calm, respectful, accessible, transparent, and connected.

This handbook governs information architecture, user roles, personalization, progress, recommendations, search, notifications, privacy, data, AI assistance, accessibility, localization, product integration, editorial operations, evaluation, and future development. All higher-level governance and product-specific handbooks remain applicable.

## Vision

The Dashboard should become a calm, personalized learning companion that brings every Healthy Little Minds product into one coherent experience.

It should help children, parents, teachers, and lifelong learners continue growth without pressure or manipulation. A child should find a story or practice without being ranked. A parent should return to a useful guide without seeing a guilt-inducing streak. A teacher should find a classroom-ready next step without searching through unrelated family content. A general learner should explore psychology without being profiled.

The Dashboard should grow quieter as it becomes more capable. Personalization should reduce clutter rather than add modules. Recommendations should become easier to understand, not more mysterious. Users should know why something appears and be able to change or remove the reason.

The long-term experience should connect learning across formats. A story can lead to a conversation, a worksheet to practice, audio to reflection, a Will Talks episode to evidence, and a parent or teacher guide to real-life application. Progress should represent the user's chosen journey rather than a platform-defined ladder.

International editions should preserve the same philosophy while adapting language, organization, dates, roles, educational systems, examples, and recommendations. Accessibility preferences should travel across the ecosystem and work consistently.

Trust is the foundation. The Dashboard should collect less data than it could, explain what it uses, protect children's privacy, avoid sensitive inference, and give users meaningful control. It should be valuable even when personalization is limited or disabled.

## Dashboard Philosophy

### Learning before productivity

The Dashboard should support learning, not turn emotional education into a list of tasks. Completion can help users return to a resource, but it is not the primary outcome.

A productivity interface emphasizes volume, speed, deadlines, and unfinished work. A learning interface emphasizes purpose, practice, reflection, and application. Healthy Little Minds should not tell a user they are “behind,” display a growing backlog, or reward rapid completion.

The central question for every module is: how does this help the person understand or practise something useful?

### Guidance before complexity

The Dashboard should organize choices and surface a small number of understandable next steps. It should not expose every possible filter, metric, product, and setting at once.

Guidance means explaining why an item may be relevant, what it contains, how long it may take, and what the user can do with it. It does not mean removing choice or hiding the full library.

Progressive disclosure should make advanced controls available when needed. The default home should remain focused.

### Calm before stimulation

The Dashboard should feel visually and emotionally steady. It should avoid crowded cards, competing calls to action, animated counters, celebratory bursts, autoplay, flashing, attention badges, and rapidly changing recommendations.

Calm does not mean empty or dull. Clear hierarchy, warm illustration, purposeful colour, readable typography, stable layout, and gentle motion can support orientation. Automatic carousel rotation should remain controllable, pause on interaction, and respect reduced-motion preferences wherever it is retained.

### Choice before pressure

Users should be able to choose, postpone, dismiss, save, and change direction without penalty. Recommendations are suggestions, not assignments unless an authorized educator or caregiver clearly assigns a resource within an appropriate relationship.

Do not use scarcity, countdowns, streak loss, social comparison, guilt, or loaded choices. A user should never have to select “No, I do not care about emotional learning” to decline a prompt.

### Progress before perfection

Progress should acknowledge practice and reflection without implying a perfect route. Emotional growth is uneven, contextual, relational, and not reducible to platform activity.

The Dashboard may show where a user stopped, resources they chose, goals they set, or practices they wish to revisit. It must not claim that a user has mastered anger, completed resilience, or achieved emotional intelligence.

### Support before surveillance

Information should be collected only when it gives the user clear benefit. The Dashboard must not monitor children or adults merely because technology allows it.

Supportive data helps resume a story, save an accessibility setting, remember a chosen goal, or recommend a related resource. Surveillance data attempts to infer mood, risk, diagnosis, parenting quality, classroom behaviour, or vulnerability without a necessary and transparent purpose.

### Privacy before personalization

Personalization is optional value, not a reason to gather excessive data. When privacy and recommendation precision conflict, privacy takes precedence.

The Dashboard should work well with explicit preferences, broad role, language, selected age group, saved resources, and user-chosen goals. It should not require sensitive profiling, message analysis, emotion recognition, voice inference, or cross-context tracking.

## User Types

Role-based experiences should adapt content and permissions while sharing navigation patterns, terminology, accessibility, privacy, and educational philosophy.

### Children

The child Dashboard should be simple, warm, predictable, and focused on a few safe choices. It may show a story to continue, an adult-approved activity, saved audio, a chosen emotion topic, and recent resources.

It must not show competitive rankings, public profiles, streaks, diagnostic language, behavioural scores, or pressure to complete. Children should understand what information is saved in age-appropriate language.

Adult controls and child agency need balance. Adults may curate suitable content and safety settings, but the child should be able to pause, dismiss, choose another approved resource, and protect private reflection. The platform must clearly distinguish content assignment from surveillance.

### Parents and caregivers

The parent Dashboard should prioritize current family learning goals, saved guidance, child-development context, stories and activities, parent wellbeing, and respectful school collaboration.

It should not profile a child from the parent's searches or imply diagnosis. Recommendations should state their general educational purpose and preserve uncertainty.

Parents may manage family access where lawful and appropriate, but should not automatically receive every private child response or listening choice. Privacy expectations must be clear by age, context, and feature.

### Teachers

The teacher Dashboard should surface classroom-ready resources, saved materials, lesson or routine planning, accessible formats, downloads, professional learning, and relevant Will Talks content.

It should respect time, class size, curriculum, school systems, and professional boundaries. Teachers should not receive psychological profiles, emotional rankings, or unvalidated student analytics.

Where assignment exists, teachers need clear objective, age guidance, duration, accessibility, safeguarding, and privacy information. Completion should not be treated as competence.

### School Counselors

Counselors may use educational resources for consultation, prevention, group learning, and conversation. Their Dashboard may emphasize psychology notes, evidence, facilitation guidance, referrals, and resource organization.

Professional status does not convert Dashboard data into clinical assessment. The product should not create case records, treatment plans, or risk profiles unless a future separately governed clinical product is explicitly developed. That expansion is outside current scope.

### General Learners

General learners need topic-based discovery, Will Talks, emotion education, saved resources, audio, reflection, and chosen goals. The Dashboard should not assume they are parents, teachers, or experiencing a mental health concern.

### Members

Membership may affect access to resources or features but should not alter dignity, safety, or core transparency. Paid options, renewal, cancellation, data use, and limits must be clear.

The free experience should not be deliberately confusing or emotionally incomplete to pressure conversion. Sensitive support content must not use membership barriers inappropriately.

### Future Professionals

Future professional learning may include structured courses, evidence libraries, certificates, or community discussion. The Dashboard must distinguish educational participation from professional qualification, licensure, supervision, and clinical competence.

### Multiple roles

One user may be a parent and teacher, or a counselor and general learner. Role switching should be explicit, reversible, and understandable. Data and recommendations from one context should not silently affect another, particularly when children or professional responsibilities are involved.

## Dashboard Sections

Not every section should appear for every person. The home should show only the modules that support the user's current learning and role.

### Continue Learning

Returns the user to an unfinished or intentionally paused resource. Show title, type, purpose, approximate remaining time, and a clear resume action.

Do not frame paused work as failure. Allow removal from the list and restarting when meaningful. Sensitive resources should not appear unexpectedly on shared screens without user control.

### Recommended Next Step

Offers one primary suggestion with an educational reason. The recommendation should explain its relationship to a saved goal, completed resource, chosen topic, or explicit preference.

Users must be able to dismiss it, ask why, and choose another route. The module should never become an opaque command.

### Today's Emotion

May offer a rotating educational emotion topic, not a request to report the user's mood. It should be labelled clearly as learning content.

If an optional check-in exists, it must allow uncertainty, mixed feelings, another word, no answer, and privacy. It must not infer diagnosis, risk, or recommendation from one response.

### Saved Resources

Provides a user-controlled collection across products. Users should be able to organize, remove, search, export links, and understand offline availability.

Saving should not imply endorsement, completion, or a mental health profile.

### Bookmarked Articles

Returns users to selected articles with audience, topic, reading time, and update status. If the source changes materially, indicate the update rather than silently replacing saved meaning.

### Continue Story

Resumes an Interactive Story at a safe scene boundary. Preserve accessibility, narration, language, and progress settings. Let the user restart or choose another approved story.

Do not display choice history as a psychological interpretation.

### Recommended Worksheet

Suggests a worksheet because it extends a specific resource or selected goal. State audience, objective, duration, materials, adult role, and available formats.

Do not recommend a worksheet as a diagnostic test or generic engagement item.

### Recommended Audio

Suggests audio by objective, context, duration, language, and access need. Clarify whether it is a story, guide, reflection, or optional practice.

Do not imply a listener needs calming based on hidden inference. Autoplay must remain off.

### Latest Will Talks Episode

Provides the latest relevant episode when its topic fits the user's interests or role. Recency alone should not displace a more useful foundational episode.

Show topic, audience, duration, transcript, content note, and why it is relevant.

### Teacher Resources

Organizes classroom guidance, activities, downloads, planning tools, saved resources, and professional learning. Show practical metadata and local school context.

Do not display to children or unrelated users by default merely to fill space.

### Parent Resources

Organizes family guidance, stories, activities, parent wellbeing, and school partnership. Recommendations must avoid guilt and diagnostic suggestion.

### Downloads

Lists files with title, format, size, language, accessibility, version, download date, and update status where available. Users should be able to remove local copies and retrieve updated versions.

### Achievements

Achievements, if used, must recognize meaningful participation without status, competition, scarcity, or pressure. Appropriate examples might acknowledge that a user completed a chosen learning pathway or contributed a reflection privately.

Avoid points, levels, streak badges, leaderboards, “perfect” completion, emotional-skill ranks, and rewards for disclosure. Achievements should be optional and hideable. The preferred default is progress language rather than badges.

### Reflection Journal

Provides private, voluntary reflection linked to user-selected prompts. It should support text, audio where safely governed, drawing, selection, and pass options.

Explain privacy, storage, access, export, deletion, and any adult visibility. Do not analyze entries for diagnosis, risk, sentiment, or recommendations without an explicitly governed and lawful feature. The current default should be no such analysis.

### Learning Goals

Allows users to choose a small, meaningful focus such as “learn language for frustration” or “prepare a calmer classroom transition.” Goals should be editable, pausable, and removable.

Avoid deadlines, red overdue states, and goals framed as fixing a child. Offer process and support goals rather than promises about outcomes.

### Recently Viewed

Helps users return to recent content. Provide clearing controls and shared-device privacy. Do not use the list to infer sensitive interests or automatically surface them in notifications.

### Notifications

Shows necessary account information, chosen reminders, meaningful resource updates, and relevant educational announcements. Notifications should be grouped and easy to clear without creating an unread badge burden.

### Language

Allows users to choose language and region, see content availability, and understand whether switching preserves the current page. Language names should appear naturally and not rely on flags alone.

### Profile

Contains only information needed for identity, role, preferences, and service. Do not encourage public biographies, child profiles, or unnecessary demographic disclosure.

### Settings

Provides understandable control over accessibility, language, privacy, personalization, notifications, downloads, account, consent, and data. Important controls should not be hidden through progressive disclosure designed to discourage use.

### Help

Offers contextual guidance, accessibility support, privacy explanations, account recovery, feedback, corrections, and appropriate service contacts. It must distinguish technical help from psychological or crisis support.

## Personalization

Personalization should make learning easier to navigate while preserving choice, privacy, and transparency.

### Permitted signals

Recommendations may consider:

- explicitly selected age group;
- preferred language and region;
- user role;
- resources the user intentionally completed;
- saved or bookmarked content;
- user-defined learning goals;
- accessibility preferences;
- explicit topic preferences;
- recent activity when the user has not cleared or disabled it.

These signals should be used conservatively. A search or one resource does not establish a stable interest or personal concern.

### Prohibited or high-risk inference

Do not infer or predict:

- diagnosis or mental health condition;
- trauma, abuse, self-harm, addiction, or crisis;
- a child's emotion or behaviour profile;
- parenting quality;
- teacher effectiveness;
- disability not explicitly provided for access;
- family conflict or vulnerability;
- intelligence, character, motivation, or emotional competence;
- protected or sensitive identity from behaviour.

Sensitive support should remain discoverable directly and safely without covert targeting.

### Educational reason

Every recommendation needs a documented reason, such as:

- extends a resource the user completed;
- matches a goal the user selected;
- provides the next step in a chosen pathway;
- offers the same learning in an accessible format;
- supports the user's selected role and age group;
- updates saved guidance that changed.

“Likely to increase engagement” is not an educational reason.

### Explainability

Users should be able to ask “Why am I seeing this?” and receive a plain explanation. Avoid technical model language. Offer controls to remove the signal or recommendation.

### User control

Allow users to turn personalization off, reset history, change role and goals, dismiss recommendations, and browse the full library. The non-personalized experience must remain coherent and useful.

### Diversity and exploration

Personalization should not trap users in one topic. Offer occasional transparent opportunities to explore foundational or adjacent resources without using random stimulation.

### Recommendation frequency

Prefer a few stable recommendations over constant refresh. Do not create urgency by replacing suggestions before a user can consider them. Changes should follow meaningful context, not every click.

### Children

Children's personalization requires heightened privacy, age-appropriate explanation, caregiver governance where appropriate, minimal data, and no behavioral advertising. Recommendations should come from approved content pools and explicit learning context.

### Human oversight

Product, psychology, education, accessibility, privacy, localization, and child-safety reviewers should define recommendation rules and monitor outcomes. Automated systems must not publish unreviewed content or high-risk inference.

## Learning Progress

Progress should help users orient and reflect. It should not pressure them or claim to measure inner growth.

### What progress may show

Appropriate progress includes:

- where a story or course was paused;
- resources a user chose to complete;
- practices they wish to revisit;
- pathway sections explored;
- a user-written reflection on what helped;
- goals and actions the user selected;
- updated resources needing review.

### What progress must not claim

Do not display percentages for emotional regulation, empathy, resilience, parenting, teaching quality, wellbeing, or mental health. Do not infer mastery from completion or choice.

### Avoid streaks

Streaks create pressure, fear of loss, and a false equation between daily platform use and learning. Emotional development does not require opening an application every day.

The Dashboard may gently show that a user returned to a chosen practice over time without creating an unbroken-chain mechanic or penalty.

### Avoid competitive ranking

No leaderboards, class rankings, family comparisons, or percentile scores should represent emotional learning. Competition can discourage honesty and distort the purpose of practice.

### Encourage reflection

Use prompts such as:

- What did you notice?
- Which idea felt useful?
- What would you like to practise again?
- What support could help?
- Would you like to continue, pause, or choose another topic?

### Encourage consistency without pressure

Consistency can be described as returning when useful and practising in real life. Reminders should be user-chosen and easy to stop. Missing a reminder should have no negative state.

### Recognize offline practice

Allow users to record a simple private note that they tried something away from the platform, without requiring proof. Offline action should be more valuable than additional screen time.

### Completion states

Use “Completed,” “Visited,” “Practised,” or “Saved for later” only when they accurately describe platform activity. Avoid “Mastered.” Users should be able to mark an item incomplete or revisit it.

## Product Integration

The Dashboard is the connective layer across the Healthy Little Minds ecosystem.

### Emotion Library

Users may explore emotions by name, relationship, situation, or recent learning. The Dashboard should not interpret a viewed emotion as the user's current state.

### Teachers Hub

Surface classroom-ready guidance, saved downloads, lesson connections, professional learning, age and phase, accessibility, and localized school context. Recommendations should have clear classroom purpose.

### Parents Hub

Surface practical family guidance, conversation coaching, stories, activities, parent wellbeing, and school partnership. Avoid fear-based recommendations and assumptions about a child.

### Interactive Stories

Support safe resume, selected language, narration, accessibility, adult guides, and related practice. Do not analyze branch choices psychologically.

### Worksheets

Show objective, age, context, format, accessibility, materials, adult role, and related source. Manage updates and downloads without treating completion as skill.

### Audio Library

Organize saved listening, downloads, transcripts, duration, content notes, language, and playlists. Autoplay remains off. Listening history should be private and clearable.

### Will Talks

Recommend episodes by explicit topic, role, pathway, or saved interest. Provide transcript, evidence, duration, content note, and companion resources.

### Future Courses

Courses may show modules, chosen pace, learning objectives, accessible formats, and reflective milestones. Avoid countdowns, public ranking, and inflated certificates.

### Membership

Membership status, benefits, billing, renewal, cancellation, and data should be transparent. Recommendations must not privilege paid products when a more appropriate free resource exists without explaining the distinction.

### Cross-product continuity

Terminology, metadata, age guidance, accessibility preferences, language, saved state, and update notices should remain consistent. A user should understand why a resource moved from one product to another.

### Recommendation quality

Every integrated recommendation should answer:

- What will this help me understand or practise?
- Why is it relevant now?
- Who is it for?
- How long or demanding is it?
- What format and access options exist?
- What is a reasonable next step afterward?

## Accessibility

The Accessibility Standard governs every Dashboard state and flow.

### Screen readers

Use semantic landmarks, one clear page title, descriptive headings, lists, buttons, links, forms, dialogs, tables, and live regions. Card collections need understandable grouping and reading order.

Dynamic recommendations should not move focus or announce excessively. Status updates should be concise and purposeful.

### Keyboard navigation

Every action must be operable by keyboard with visible focus, logical order, and no traps. Skip links should bypass repeated navigation. Complex widgets require established accessible patterns.

### Mobile devices

Prioritize the most useful content, preserve hierarchy, use large touch targets, avoid horizontal scrolling, and keep settings reachable. Do not make mobile a reduced-access version.

### Low bandwidth

Use lightweight summaries, progressive loading, clear file sizes, offline indicators, cached saved content where appropriate, and user control over media. Avoid automatic video, audio, and image downloads.

### Offline awareness

State what remains available, what cannot update, whether changes are saved locally, and how synchronization will work. Do not display stale crisis, service, or evidence information as current without an update warning.

### Cognitive accessibility

Use stable layout, predictable labels, few primary choices, grouped content, progressive disclosure, plain language, clear recovery, and visible context. Avoid constantly changing recommendations and notification clutter.

### Clear navigation

Primary destinations should remain stable across roles. Breadcrumbs, search, filters, back behaviour, and role switching should be understandable. Avoid navigation based on playful metaphors.

### Large touch targets

Controls need adequate size and spacing. Do not place destructive actions next to common actions without separation and confirmation.

### Visual access

Support contrast, text resizing, zoom, reflow, colour-independent meaning, high-contrast and forced-colour modes, and reduced motion. Progress must not rely on colour or small rings alone.

### Motor and timing access

Avoid drag-only organization, precision gestures, timeouts, and disappearing messages. Provide alternatives, undo, and enough time to review.

### Media access

Label transcripts, captions, audio, downloads, and accessible variants before opening. Preserve user playback and motion preferences across products.

### Accessibility preferences

Allow users to store useful preferences such as reduced motion, text presentation, preferred media format, captions, narration, and language. Explain storage and make reset easy. Do not infer disability from preferences.

### Error and recovery

Errors should say what happened, whether work is safe, and what to do. Preserve goals, journal text, filters, and forms where possible. Avoid playful language during loss or access failure.

## Localization

The Localization Guidelines govern Dashboard language, organization, recommendations, metadata, and regional context.

### Dashboard organization

The core learning hierarchy should remain consistent, but module order may adapt when local user research identifies a meaningful difference. Changes require documentation and should not fragment the ecosystem.

### Language

Use canonical navigation, action, psychology, education, and account terminology. Keep register consistent across cards, notifications, settings, errors, and help.

### Examples and content

Adapt school roles, family terms, routines, age and grade labels, support pathways, and content examples. Do not recommend region-specific resources to users elsewhere without clear labelling.

### Dates, time, and numbers

Use unambiguous local conventions for dates, calendars, time, numerals, file sizes, and billing. Relative labels such as “today” must respect timezone and locale.

### Notifications

Translate purpose and tone, not word order. Avoid urgency that appears stronger in the target language. Ensure truncation does not remove the action or qualification.

### Recommendations

Recommendation rules must account for localized availability and cultural relevance. Do not send users to untranslated or inaccessible resources without clear language labels and a useful alternative.

### Layout

Support text expansion, CJK line breaking, mixed scripts, future right-to-left direction, local input, and varied typeface metrics. Do not shrink text to preserve English card height.

### Search and discovery

Support local terms, synonyms, script, character variants, spacing, and input. A translated title alone is insufficient if users search with different everyday psychology language.

### Human review

Test complete role journeys in each language: onboarding, home, search, recommendations, downloads, journal, settings, privacy, notifications, errors, and deletion. Raw machine translation is never publication-ready.

## Privacy

Trust takes priority over data collection. Privacy should shape the Dashboard architecture before personalization is designed.

### Minimal data collection

Collect only information necessary for a clear user benefit, legal obligation, security, or service function. Each field and event should have a documented purpose, owner, retention period, access policy, and deletion path.

“May be useful later” is not a sufficient reason.

### Transparent personalization

Explain which signals affect recommendations and provide a plain “Why this?” control. Do not hide sensitive inference behind phrases such as “for you.”

### User control

Users should be able to view and change profile data, goals, roles, language, history, recommendation inputs, notifications, and consent. Controls should be understandable and not use dark patterns.

### Downloadable data

Provide a structured, understandable export of relevant account data where required or appropriate. Explain format, scope, security, and how long preparation may take.

### Account deletion

Deletion should be easy to initiate, explain consequences clearly, offer export where appropriate, and state what must be retained and why. Do not use guilt, repeated obstacles, or hidden cancellation.

### Children's privacy

Children's data requires heightened protection, age-appropriate explanation, appropriate adult consent, child assent where meaningful, restricted access, limited retention, and no behavioral advertising.

Do not collect private reflection, emotion check-ins, voice, biometrics, precise location, contacts, or school identity unless a separately approved essential feature justifies it. The preferred default is not to collect them.

### Family accounts

Clarify what each family member can see. A caregiver account should not automatically expose every child choice, journal entry, search, or listening history. Privacy boundaries should support development, safety, law, and informed expectations.

### School accounts

Schools and educators should access only data necessary for legitimate educational use. Avoid detailed activity logs, emotional profiles, and indefinite records. Role changes and school departure need prompt access removal and data handling.

### Ethical analytics

Analytics should answer product and learning questions with minimal data. Aggregate and de-identify where possible. Do not infer mental health, sell or trade data, create advertising profiles, or combine educational activity with unrelated tracking.

### Sensitive content

Searches and views related to abuse, suicide, self-harm, trauma, diagnosis, or family conflict may be highly sensitive. Do not surface them on shared home screens, notifications, or recommendation explanations without explicit user control.

### Security and incident communication

Security controls, access logging, encryption, least privilege, and incident response should match risk. If data is affected, communicate plainly, promptly, and without minimizing responsibility.

### Retention

Retain data only as long as needed. Provide shorter defaults for children and sensitive content. Deletion must address derived profiles and recommendation features, not only visible account fields.

## AI Contribution

AI may assist learning navigation but must remain constrained, explainable, privacy-preserving, and under human oversight.

AI may assist with:

- selecting candidates from approved resources;
- summarizing a resource accurately for the user's chosen role;
- proposing reflection prompts from approved content;
- suggesting learning steps based on explicit goals and completed resources;
- drafting translations for qualified review;
- identifying duplicate or inaccessible recommendations;
- helping editors audit recommendation coverage.

AI may never:

- diagnose, screen, assess risk, or infer mental health;
- interpret a child's story choice or worksheet response psychologically;
- manipulate behaviour or maximize engagement;
- create urgency, streak pressure, or emotional dependence;
- replace professional judgment, safeguarding, or crisis services;
- recommend unreviewed generated psychology content;
- silently use journal text, private messages, voice, or sensitive searches;
- publish without human-governed rules and review;
- approve its own output.

### Approved-resource grounding

Recommendations and summaries should come from current approved Healthy Little Minds resources and verified metadata. AI should not invent titles, features, evidence, translations, or support services.

### Explainability

Every AI-supported recommendation should have a user-facing reason and an internal audit record sufficient for review. “The algorithm chose this” is not an explanation.

### Human oversight

Educators, psychologists, accessibility specialists, localization reviewers, privacy and security experts, product managers, and affected users should define and test the system. High-risk topics should use fixed governed pathways rather than open-ended generation.

### User choice

Users should know when AI materially shapes an experience and be able to choose a non-AI route. Turning AI off should not make the Dashboard unusable.

### Safety and escalation

The Dashboard should not invite crisis disclosures to an AI assistant. If a user independently enters urgent content in a future conversational feature, a separately approved safety process is required. General recommendations are not crisis response.

### Evaluation

Audit AI outcomes for accuracy, relevance, exclusion, bias, repetitive loops, privacy, age appropriateness, localization, and accessibility. Measure educational value, not click-through alone.

## Notifications

Notifications should support learning, announce meaningful changes, and respect attention.

### Appropriate purposes

Notifications may:

- confirm an account or download action;
- announce an update to a saved resource;
- remind a user at a time they explicitly chose;
- suggest returning to a selected goal after an appropriate interval;
- announce a genuinely relevant new resource;
- communicate security, privacy, service, or access changes;
- provide an educator's appropriate assignment notice.

### Inappropriate purposes

Notifications must not:

- create anxiety or urgency;
- warn that progress will be lost when it will not;
- use guilt about a child, class, or wellbeing;
- reveal sensitive topics on a lock screen;
- send repeated prompts after non-response;
- manufacture scarcity or countdowns;
- celebrate platform use as emotional achievement;
- encourage daily opening for its own sake.

### User control

Allow granular channel, topic, timing, frequency, quiet-hours, and sensitive-preview control. Essential service messages should be clearly distinguished from optional learning messages.

### Tone

Use calm, direct, specific language.

**Avoid:** “You missed another day—do not lose your progress!”

**Use:** “Would you like to return to your saved classroom-routine guide?”

### Frequency

Default to low frequency. Consolidate updates where possible. Learn from explicit settings rather than interpreting silence as a reason to increase prompting.

### Children

Child notifications require heightened restraint, adult governance where appropriate, age-appropriate language, no external pressure, and no sensitive lock-screen content. The preferred default is minimal notification.

### New resources

Announce only when relevant to selected roles, topics, or languages. “New” is not itself educational value. Explain what the resource helps with.

## Search and Discovery

Search and discovery should help users find resources through topics, emotions, roles, age groups, recent activity, recommendations, and practical needs without overwhelming menus.

### Search

Support everyday language, canonical psychology terms, spelling variation, localized synonyms, scripts, and common questions. Results should not reinforce diagnostic assumptions.

A search for “my child is lazy” may provide resources on task difficulty, motivation, executive function, communication, sleep, stress, and learning support while using respectful result titles.

### Filters

Useful filters may include audience, age, topic, emotion, format, duration, language, accessibility, product, cost, and download availability. Begin with a small set and allow clearing all.

Do not require users to know internal product structure before they can find help.

### Topics and emotions

Topic pages can organize learning paths. Emotion discovery should allow related terms, mixed feelings, and cultural language without implying that selecting an emotion reports the user's state.

### Roles and age groups

Role and age can narrow practical context but should remain editable. Avoid rigid developmental assumptions and allow cross-role browsing.

### Recent activity

Recent activity can support return but should be clearable and protected on shared devices. It should not dominate discovery or create a narrow loop.

### Recommendations

Recommendations should be limited, explained, diverse enough to support learning, and subordinate to direct search and browsing.

### Result quality

Rank relevance, educational fit, safety, accessibility, language, currency, and user intent before popularity or novelty. Sponsored or membership content must not receive hidden preference.

### No results

Explain what was searched, suggest a broader or related term, provide role or topic routes, and offer help. Do not invent results or silently change a sensitive query.

### Sensitive discovery

Mental health and safeguarding searches should return accurate general education, scope boundaries, and locally verified support where appropriate. They should not produce personalized diagnosis or marketing.

## Success Metrics

Success is measured by continued learning, resource completion, real-world application, parent conversations, teacher implementation, reflection, and confidence—not daily active users or time spent.

### Continued learning

Do users find a meaningful next step and return when it serves their goal? Healthy continuation may include leaving the platform to practise.

### Resource completion

Completion can reveal usability and pathway continuity. It does not prove understanding or emotional growth. Evaluate why completion occurs or does not.

### Real-world application

Can users identify and try an appropriate action away from the platform? Use voluntary feedback, user research, and product-specific evaluation without demanding proof.

### Parent conversations

Do recommendations lead to safer, more useful family conversation? Measure through respectful feedback rather than recording private conversations.

### Teacher implementation

Do teachers find and use practical resources under real classroom conditions? Consider repeat use, adaptation, accessibility, workload, and observed usefulness.

### Reflection

Do users understand what they learned and what they may try next? Journal volume is not a success metric. Privacy and quality matter more than quantity.

### Confidence

Do users feel more able to choose, discuss, practise, or seek help? Confidence should not be inflated or treated as proof of competence.

### Decision reduction

Can users reach a relevant next step with less confusion and fewer unnecessary choices? This is a core Dashboard metric.

### Trust, privacy, and access

Track understandable settings, opt-out success, deletion, recommendation explanations, accessibility issues, localization parity, complaints, and corrections. Growth that reduces trust is failure.

### Metrics not treated as goals

Daily active users, session length, pages per visit, notification opens, streaks, and endless scroll depth may help diagnose technical behaviour but should not be product objectives.

### Ethical evaluation

Collect minimal data, define questions before analysis, avoid vulnerable profiling, and combine metrics with qualitative research. Do not optimize one metric without reviewing harm and equity.

## Editorial Workflow

Every Dashboard recommendation requires a documented educational reason and lifecycle.

### Resource eligibility

Only approved, current, accessible, correctly localized resources with complete metadata may enter recommendation pools. Record audience, age, objective, topic, format, duration, prerequisites, sensitivity, accessibility, language, region, evidence status, and related next steps.

### Recommendation proposal

Define:

- intended user and learning need;
- eligible resource set;
- explicit signals used;
- educational reason;
- excluded sensitive inferences;
- explanation shown to users;
- dismissal and control;
- accessibility and localization behavior;
- success and harm measures;
- owner and review date.

### Interdisciplinary review

Product, education, psychology, editorial, accessibility, localization, privacy, security, and child-safety reviewers assess the rule according to risk. AI-supported rules require additional model and bias review.

### Content review

Dashboard titles, summaries, reasons, calls to action, notifications, empty states, errors, and help text follow content governance. Summaries must preserve qualifications and not become stronger than source content.

### Design and implementation

Use established components and semantic patterns. Define loading, empty, error, offline, dismissed, unavailable, expired, updated, and permission states. Avoid layout shift and attention manipulation.

### Testing

Test with representative roles, ages, languages, disabilities, devices, privacy settings, and low-bandwidth conditions. Verify:

- users understand why recommendations appear;
- users can dismiss, change, and turn off personalization;
- no sensitive inference is made;
- progress language is not interpreted as emotional scoring;
- child and family privacy boundaries are understood;
- keyboard and screen-reader flows work;
- localized layouts and terminology are natural;
- errors and offline states preserve work;
- recommendations improve decision quality rather than only clicks.

### Publication

Confirm approvals, documentation, logging, monitoring, rollback, support, and user controls. Release gradually where risk warrants it. Do not launch personalization without a useful non-personalized experience.

### Monitoring

Review relevance, exclusion, repeated loops, bias, sensitive exposure, accessibility, complaints, privacy, language parity, dismissal, and real educational use. A high click-through rate cannot override harm.

### Update and retirement

Remove expired or superseded resources promptly. Reevaluate recommendation rules when source content, evidence, roles, products, law, privacy expectations, or localization changes. Retire rules that cannot be explained or maintained.

## Quality Checklist

- [ ] The feature helps users decide what to learn or practise next.
- [ ] It supports learning rather than productivity or account administration alone.
- [ ] Guidance reduces complexity without removing choice.
- [ ] The experience is calm and does not compete for attention.
- [ ] Progress is supported without perfectionism.
- [ ] Personalization does not require surveillance.
- [ ] Privacy takes priority over recommendation precision.

### User types and roles

- [ ] The primary role and its educational needs are defined.
- [ ] Children, parents, teachers, counselors, and general learners receive appropriate experiences.
- [ ] Role switching is explicit and reversible.
- [ ] Data does not silently cross family, school, and personal contexts.
- [ ] Professional use is not confused with clinical functionality.
- [ ] Membership does not alter safety, dignity, or transparency.

## Governance and Lifecycle

Every Dashboard module, recommendation rule, progress representation, and notification pattern needs a named owner. Ownership includes content metadata, logic, user explanation, design states, accessibility, localization, privacy, analytics, monitoring, correction, and retirement.

Material decisions should be documented. Record why a feature exists, what educational need it serves, which data it uses, which signals are prohibited, how users control it, what harms were considered, what testing found, and what would trigger review.

Version changes should be meaningful. New inputs, changed ranking, different visibility, new child access, altered privacy, changed AI behavior, or redesigned progress language require renewed interdisciplinary review. Small visual changes still require accessibility and localization regression checks.

Feedback should be triaged by risk. Sensitive exposure, child privacy, inferred diagnosis, inaccessible controls, manipulative prompting, discriminatory recommendations, account deletion failure, and incorrect educational guidance require prompt response. Preference feedback can inform scheduled improvement.

The Dashboard should receive periodic audits for module growth, notification volume, recommendation loops, stale content, inaccessible states, language parity, data accumulation, hidden defaults, and whether features continue to reduce decision fatigue.

Retire features that cannot show educational value, require disproportionate data, create unhealthy pressure, cannot be explained, or duplicate a simpler pattern. Removal should include stored profiles, notification rules, analytics, documentation, and user communication where needed.

## Future Vision

The Dashboard may evolve through adaptive learning plans, family dashboards, classroom dashboards, teacher groups, parent learning journeys, offline learning, AI-assisted recommendations, and community features. Each expansion must preserve privacy, accessibility, educational integrity, and voluntary participation.

### Adaptive learning plans

Plans could organize user-selected goals, foundational understanding, practice, reflection, and follow-up. Adaptation should rely on explicit preferences and reviewed pathways rather than inferred psychology.

Plans must remain editable, pausable, and free from deadlines, streaks, and emotional scoring. Users should be able to see why each step appears.

### Family dashboards

Family dashboards could coordinate shared stories, activities, language preferences, and schedules while preserving individual privacy. They require clear roles, age-appropriate control, consent, and visibility boundaries.

They must not become a parent surveillance tool or compare siblings. Shared goals should be collaborative and not frame one child as the family problem.

### Classroom dashboards

Classroom dashboards could help teachers assign resources, organize accessible formats, and plan group learning. They should report content access and logistical status, not emotional profiles or psychological scores.

Student-level data should be minimized. Class-level summaries must not conceal inequitable access or invite high-stakes conclusions.

### Teacher groups

Teacher teams may share collections, plans, and implementation notes. Features require school authorization, role controls, privacy, moderation, accessible collaboration, and boundaries around student information.

### Parent learning journeys

Journeys may connect guidance, stories, conversation, worksheets, audio, and reflection around a caregiver-selected need. They should reduce guilt, allow nonlinear progress, and include professional support boundaries.

### Offline learning

Offline mode could provide downloaded stories, audio, worksheets, saved articles, plans, and progress synchronization. Users need clear freshness, storage, privacy, and conflict information. High-risk guidance should not remain silently stale.

### AI-assisted recommendations

AI may improve retrieval across a growing approved library, create plain summaries, and suggest accessible formats. It must remain transparent, optional, constrained to governed content, and free from diagnosis or engagement optimization.

High-risk topics should use fixed, reviewed pathways and local support rather than generative recommendations.

### Community features

Communities could support questions, shared learning, and educator or parent connection. They require moderation, safeguarding, misinformation control, privacy, accessibility, localization, conduct rules, and clear professional boundaries.

Community popularity must not determine evidence or recommendations. Children should not receive open social features without an exceptional, separately governed justification.

### Professional learning

Future courses and certification may use the Dashboard for learning paths and evidence. Participation must not be represented as licensure or clinical competence unless formally warranted.

### Stewardship questions

Every future proposal should answer:

- Does this help the user identify a meaningful next step?
- Does it reduce or increase decision fatigue?
- Does it support offline learning and real-life practice?
- Does it avoid productivity, gamification, and social-media pressure?
- What data does it require, and can less be used?
- Can users understand, control, and disable it?
- Is it accessible and culturally adaptable?
- Can it be audited, corrected, and maintained for ten years?
- Does it strengthen trust across the Healthy Little Minds ecosystem?

The best Dashboard is not the one with the most modules. It is the one that makes the whole platform easier to understand and easier to leave for meaningful practice.

## Closing Reflection

The Healthy Little Minds Dashboard should never feel like a productivity tool.

It should feel like a trusted companion that quietly helps people continue learning, reflecting, and growing at their own pace.

The best Dashboard is one that helps users spend less time deciding what to do next—and more time practising what truly matters.
