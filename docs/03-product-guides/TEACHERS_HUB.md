# Healthy Little Minds Teachers Hub

## Purpose

The Teachers Hub exists to bridge the gap between psychology research and everyday classroom practice. It translates reliable knowledge about child development, learning, relationships, emotion, and behaviour into guidance educators can use within the realities of their work.

Teachers should leave every resource with something practical they can confidently apply in a real classroom. A resource may deepen understanding, invite reflection, or explain evidence, but it must ultimately support action. The goal is not to impress educators with theory. It is to help them make informed daily decisions that improve children's learning, emotional wellbeing, relationships, and behaviour.

The Hub respects teachers as skilled professionals. It does not prescribe a single correct response to every child or classroom. It offers principles, options, examples, and adaptation guidance so educators can exercise informed judgment. It recognises that teaching happens amid limited time, competing needs, cultural expectations, policy requirements, and unequal access to support.

The Hub is educational. It does not diagnose children, replace safeguarding procedures, prescribe treatment, or substitute for qualified psychological, medical, special educational, or social care. When a concern requires specialist assessment or urgent support, resources must say so clearly and direct educators towards appropriate local pathways.

This handbook is the canonical standard for the Teachers Hub. It governs its content, design, production, review, localization, and future development. Where a local preference conflicts with the Project Charter, Product Principles, psychology standards, or Accessibility Standard, those governing documents take precedence.

## Vision

The Teachers Hub should become one of the world's most trusted collections of practical, psychology-informed classroom resources. Trust must be earned through accuracy, usefulness, humility, consistency, accessibility, and responsible stewardship—not asserted through popularity or scale.

The long-term aim is to support educators in every country with resources that are evidence-informed, culturally adaptable, and realistic about classroom conditions. A teacher using a phone between lessons, a student teacher building confidence, a school leader planning professional learning, and a specialist supporting an individual child should all be able to find clear routes into the material.

Growth should deepen usefulness rather than create volume for its own sake. The library should become more coherent over time: related articles, downloads, audio, video, and Will Talks episodes should reinforce one another; foundational ideas should remain stable; outdated evidence should be reviewed; and translated resources should receive the same care as English originals.

The desired future is not a library that educators merely browse. It is a dependable working resource that teachers return to, adapt thoughtfully, discuss with colleagues, and use to make classrooms calmer, safer, more inclusive, and more supportive of learning.

## Primary Audience

The primary audience is educators working directly or indirectly with children. The Hub should never assume that all educators have the same training, authority, time, language, technology, or classroom conditions.

### Classroom teachers

Classroom teachers need guidance that can survive contact with a busy school day. They need concise explanations, examples of what to say and do, realistic preparation requirements, and adaptations for different ages and needs. They also need reassurance that reflective improvement matters more than perfect implementation.

### Preschool educators

Preschool educators support children whose emotional learning is highly relational, embodied, and play-based. Resources should emphasise co-regulation, predictable routines, modelling, sensory needs, simple language, and developmentally appropriate expectations. They should avoid expecting young children to reason or self-regulate beyond their developmental capacity.

### Primary teachers

Primary teachers often teach emotional skills across the whole day and curriculum. They need routines, stories, discussion prompts, visual supports, and short activities that can be repeated. Guidance should account for wide developmental variation within a class and should help teachers connect emotional literacy with relationships, behaviour, and learning.

### Secondary teachers

Secondary teachers may see many groups for shorter periods and have limited opportunities for individual follow-up. Resources should respect adolescents' autonomy and dignity, avoid childish presentation, and offer strategies that work within subject teaching, tutor periods, pastoral systems, and transitions. Content should recognise peer dynamics, identity development, academic pressure, and increasing independence.

### Special educators

Special educators need flexible resources that do not treat one communication, sensory, cognitive, or behavioural profile as universal. Materials should offer multiple means of participation, concrete adaptation notes, accessible formats, and careful distinctions between distress, communication, sensory needs, skill gaps, and deliberate choices. Individual support plans and specialist knowledge must be respected.

### School counselors and school psychologists

Counselors and psychologists may use the Hub to support consultation, staff learning, prevention, and shared language. They need accurate scope, transparent evidence, suitable references, and resources that distinguish universal classroom support from targeted or specialist intervention. The Hub should complement—not dilute—their professional judgment.

### Administrators and school leaders

Leaders need resources that help them create consistent environments, support staff, evaluate initiatives, and avoid isolated interventions. Content for leaders should address implementation conditions, workload, policy, safeguarding, equity, staff development, and sustainable improvement. It should not imply that classroom teachers alone are responsible for systemic problems.

### Teacher trainers and student teachers

Teacher educators need material suitable for reflection, discussion, demonstration, and practice. Student teachers need clear foundations without false certainty. Resources should connect theory to observable classroom decisions and encourage inquiry, supervision, and adaptation rather than rote compliance.

Audience routes may overlap, but labels should help users identify relevance quickly. Every resource must name its intended audience, age or phase where relevant, classroom context, and any important limits.

## Educational Philosophy

Teachers learn most effectively when knowledge is connected to their experience and followed by supported practice. Teachers Hub resources therefore follow a developmental pathway:

**Understanding**

↓

**Reflection**

↓

**Practice**

↓

**Adaptation**

↓

**Improvement**

↓

**Long-term habit**

Understanding establishes a clear mental model. A teacher should know what a concept means, why it matters, what it does not mean, and how it may appear in context. Explanation should be accurate but proportionate to the decision the teacher needs to make.

Reflection connects new knowledge with existing practice. It should invite curiosity rather than guilt: What tends to happen? What might the child be communicating? Which parts of the environment help or hinder? What assumptions need checking?

Practice turns an idea into observable action. Guidance should identify a manageable first step, describe what preparation is needed, and provide examples. Whenever language is central, offer natural wording without presenting scripts as compulsory formulas.

Adaptation acknowledges that no strategy works identically for every learner or setting. Resources should explain what may be changed while preserving the underlying purpose. Adaptations may concern age, language, disability, culture, class size, time, materials, or delivery format.

Improvement depends on noticing outcomes and adjusting responsibly. Resources should suggest what teachers might observe, how to gather proportionate feedback, and when to persist, modify, stop, or seek additional support. A strategy's failure is information, not evidence that a teacher or child has failed.

Long-term habit develops through repetition, shared expectations, and integration into ordinary routines. Emotional learning should not be confined to special events. Whenever possible, resources should help teachers embed useful practices into arrival, transitions, instruction, conflict repair, reflection, and communication with families.

This pathway is not always linear. Teachers may return to understanding after practice reveals a new question. The essential requirement is that learning ends with classroom application and creates a route for reflection and refinement.

## Content Categories

Categories make the library understandable and establish editorial boundaries. A resource may appear in more than one category, but it must have one primary purpose.

### Classroom Management

This category supports predictable, respectful learning environments through routines, expectations, relationships, instruction, and proportionate responses. It should treat classroom management as the creation of conditions for participation—not control for its own sake. Content may cover transitions, attention, boundaries, repair, classroom climate, and group dynamics.

It must not recommend humiliation, collective punishment, public behaviour charts that stigmatise children, coercive compliance, or practices that disregard disability and distress. Consequences should be related, respectful, reasonable, and accompanied by opportunities to learn and reconnect.

### Emotional Literacy

Emotional literacy resources help children notice, name, understand, express, and respond to emotions. They should explain that emotional vocabulary supports communication but does not make emotions simple or universal. Body signals, language, culture, context, and individual differences matter.

Resources must not assign fixed meanings to facial expressions or imply that an adult can know a child's internal state from appearance alone. Activities should permit uncertainty and expressions such as “I am not sure” or “I need time.”

### Behaviour Support

Behaviour support content helps educators interpret behaviour in context, teach missing skills, adjust environments, reinforce helpful patterns, and maintain safe boundaries. It should ask what happened before, what need or function may be involved, what the child can currently do, and what support changes the conditions.

It must avoid labelling children as manipulative, attention-seeking, lazy, defiant, or “bad.” Functional explanations should be hypotheses, not diagnoses. Safety, safeguarding, disability rights, and school policy remain relevant.

### Trauma-Informed Practice

This category explains principles that can make classrooms safer and more predictable for children affected by adversity. It may address trust, choice, routines, triggers, regulation, relationships, and referral. Content must be careful not to assume trauma, encourage teachers to investigate disclosures, or frame all difficult behaviour as trauma.

Teachers are not asked to become therapists. Resources must distinguish trauma-informed universal practice from trauma assessment or treatment and include safeguarding guidance where disclosure or immediate risk is relevant.

### Social-Emotional Learning

Social-emotional learning resources support the intentional development of self-awareness, social awareness, relationship skills, responsible decision-making, and regulation-related capacities. They should integrate instruction, modelling, rehearsal, reflection, and classroom application.

Content should avoid presenting frameworks as culturally neutral or implying that quiet compliance is emotional competence. Skills must be taught without suppressing identity, justified disagreement, or appropriate emotional expression.

### Teacher Wellbeing

Teacher wellbeing content recognises educators as people working within systems. It may support boundaries, recovery, emotional awareness, collegial connection, workload conversations, and help-seeking. It should be practical, compassionate, and honest about structural causes of strain.

It must not turn systemic workload, unsafe conditions, or inadequate staffing into an individual resilience problem. Resources should never imply that self-care alone can resolve burnout or replace organisational responsibility and professional care.

### Parent Partnerships

This category helps teachers build respectful, reciprocal relationships with parents and caregivers. It may cover listening, difficult conversations, shared goals, confidentiality, strengths-based communication, cultural humility, and collaborative problem-solving.

Resources should not assume one family structure, communication style, schedule, literacy level, or relationship with school. They must avoid blame and recognise that families hold essential knowledge about their children.

### Inclusive Education

Inclusive education resources support participation, belonging, access, and meaningful learning across disability, neurodiversity, language, culture, identity, and circumstance. They should focus on removing barriers and expanding options rather than requiring children to appear typical.

Content should distinguish universal design, reasonable adjustments, differentiated teaching, and individual support. It must avoid deficit language, token representation, and unsupported claims about diagnostic groups.

### Classroom Routines

This category provides repeatable structures for moments such as arrival, transitions, check-ins, collaborative work, calming, help-seeking, reflection, and departure. Each routine should state its purpose, duration, preparation, sequence, adaptation options, and indicators of usefulness.

Routines should reduce uncertainty without becoming rigid. They must include dignified alternatives for children who cannot or do not wish to participate publicly.

### Child Development

Child development content explains patterns and variation across cognitive, social, emotional, physical, language, and identity development. It should help educators set realistic expectations while recognising that development is uneven and influenced by context.

Age ranges are guides, not deadlines. Resources must avoid diagnosing delay, treating averages as rules, or presenting one cultural pathway as universal. Concerns requiring assessment should be framed carefully with reference to appropriate local support.

### Psychology Explained

This category translates psychological concepts into accurate, plain-language explanations relevant to education. Each resource should define the concept, explain its classroom relevance, identify limitations and common misunderstandings, and show how it may inform practice.

It must not turn preliminary findings into laws, use neuroscience as decoration, or imply that a named concept explains every behaviour.

### Research Summaries

Research summaries synthesise a defined body of evidence for educators. They should state the question, evidence types, populations, contexts, major findings, limitations, uncertainty, and practical implications. A single study should not be presented as consensus.

Summaries must distinguish correlation from causation and statistical significance from educational importance. Publication date, review date, and references are required.

### Practical Strategies

Practical strategy resources focus on a specific classroom need and provide a usable response. They should state the objective, conditions, steps, examples, adaptations, cautions, and a way to reflect on impact.

No strategy should be called universal, guaranteed, instant, or a “hack.” Strategy lists must not become disconnected tips; each action needs a credible rationale.

### Downloadable Resources

Downloadable resources include worksheets, posters, checklists, cards, routines, and lesson supports intended for print or digital use. They must have a clear instructional purpose, usage guidance, accessible source format where possible, printer-friendly presentation, and localization readiness.

A download is not complete merely because it looks polished. It must work in its intended context and protect children's privacy and dignity.

## Content Standards

Every resource should include the following elements where appropriate. Editors may adapt the order and labels, but omissions should be deliberate.

- **Practical objective:** State what the educator will understand or be able to do.
- **Classroom context:** Identify the setting, age or phase, circumstances, time, group size, and prerequisites that affect use.
- **Psychology explanation:** Explain the relevant mechanism in plain language and within proper scope.
- **Evidence summary:** Describe what supports the guidance, how strong or limited that evidence is, and where it may not generalise.
- **Classroom example:** Show the principle in a realistic situation without presenting an idealised classroom or shaming mistakes.
- **Implementation steps:** Provide a manageable sequence, preparation, suggested language, and likely time demand.
- **Common mistakes:** Identify predictable misunderstandings, harmful shortcuts, and signs that adaptation is needed.
- **Adaptations:** Address age, disability, language, culture, group size, available resources, and delivery format where relevant.
- **Reflection questions:** Help educators notice what happened and decide what to retain or change.
- **Downloadable resources:** Extend application when a separate tool genuinely helps.
- **Related articles:** Connect users to prerequisites, deeper explanations, complementary strategies, and relevant Will Talks episodes.

Every resource must also make its boundaries visible. It should identify who it is for, what it is not designed to do, and when professional, safeguarding, or emergency support may be needed. Content about elevated risk, disclosure, abuse, self-harm, or serious mental health concerns requires specialist review and region-appropriate signposting.

Titles and summaries must accurately represent the content. Estimated reading time, preparation time, age relevance, format, and accessibility information should be supplied when useful. Dates and review status should be visible to editors even if not all metadata appears publicly.

Content should foreground the child's dignity. Examples must not make a child the punchline, expose identifying information, or reduce a child to a behaviour. Composite or fictional scenarios should be labelled when readers could otherwise mistake them for documented cases.

## Evidence Standards

Evidence-informed practice combines the best available research with professional expertise, classroom context, and the needs and perspectives of children and communities. Research informs judgment; it does not remove the need for judgment.

Preferred evidence includes systematic reviews and meta-analyses of suitable quality, professional guidelines, replicated peer-reviewed research, established psychology and education textbooks, and high-quality educational research. Primary studies may be used to explain emerging work but should not carry broad recommendations alone. Relevant qualitative research can illuminate experience and implementation even when it does not estimate causal effects.

Sources should be evaluated for relevance, recency, methods, sample, context, conflicts of interest, and consistency with the wider evidence. Prestige, citation count, or a compelling headline is not enough. Where evidence comes mainly from one country, age group, school system, language, or demographic group, that limit should be stated.

Writers must distinguish:

- evidence from interpretation;
- association from causation;
- group averages from individual prediction;
- short-term outcomes from lasting change;
- absence of evidence from evidence of no effect;
- expert consensus from one expert's opinion;
- educational support from clinical intervention.

Calibrated language is required. Use “may support,” “is associated with,” or “evidence suggests” when findings are conditional. Use stronger language only when the evidence warrants it. Avoid “proves,” “always,” “never,” “rewires the brain,” and precise effect claims without context.

References should enable verification. Citations must support the claim attached to them, not merely discuss the same topic. Quotations require an original source and proper context. AI-generated references, uncross-checked summaries, and citations copied from secondary material are unacceptable.

Evidence review should be proportionate to risk. Foundational explanations require knowledgeable review; content concerning trauma, diagnosis-adjacent language, self-harm, safeguarding, clinical conditions, or major intervention claims requires qualified specialist review. Each published resource needs a review date and a route for correction. Material should be re-examined when important guidance changes, credible concerns arise, or its scheduled review becomes due.

## Writing Standards

Teachers Hub writing follows the Writing Style and Voice & Tone handbooks. It should be clear, calm, respectful, practical, and precise. Teachers should not need specialist vocabulary to access an idea, but simplification must preserve its meaning.

Use plain English, descriptive headings, short purposeful paragraphs, and concrete examples. Define necessary technical terms on first use. Prefer observable descriptions over labels. Write “the child leaves their seat during independent work” rather than “the child is disruptive.” Use “behaviour” rather than moral judgment and “support” rather than “fix.”

Write to educators as capable colleagues. Do not lecture, flatter, shame, or imply that a thoughtful teacher can prevent every difficulty. Acknowledge constraints without becoming fatalistic. Advice should be direct enough to use and flexible enough to adapt.

Instructions need parallel structure, clear sequencing, and explicit conditions. Examples of teacher language should sound natural and allow variation. Avoid acronyms where possible; spell them out at first use when necessary. Avoid idioms, culturally narrow metaphors, and humour that will not localize safely.

Claims about children require particular care. Do not imply that emotions are wrong, that behaviour reveals character, or that regulation means permanent calm. Do not use diagnostic terms casually. Prefer person-centred wording while respecting communities that use identity-first language.

Editorial consistency supports trust, but clarity, accuracy, accessibility, and child dignity take priority over house preferences.

## Classroom Practicality

Every article should answer:

> What can I do tomorrow morning?

If a resource cannot answer this question, it should be revised, repositioned as background material with a clear practical companion, or not published in the Teachers Hub.

Practicality means more than adding a checklist. A recommendation must account for preparation time, lesson demands, classroom size, staff support, available space, materials, school rules, learner diversity, and likely points of difficulty. Where conditions vary, offer a minimum viable version and optional extensions.

Implementation guidance should explain what to do before, during, and after an activity. It should tell educators what success might look like without promising uniform results. It should include ways to respond when children decline, struggle, joke, become distressed, or interpret a prompt differently.

Resources should favour small repeatable practices over elaborate one-off performances. A two-minute transition routine used consistently may be more useful than a visually impressive activity requiring extensive preparation. Materials must never depend on purchases when an ordinary alternative can achieve the same purpose.

Practical advice also respects teacher judgment. Writers should explain the purpose behind each step so teachers can adapt the form without losing the function. When fidelity is important for safety or evidence, identify which elements must remain unchanged and why.

## Downloads

Downloads should extend learning rather than repeat the article in a prettier format. Each item needs a defined user, moment of use, learning purpose, and accompanying instruction.

### Worksheets

Worksheets should prompt meaningful thinking, communication, rehearsal, or planning. They should not function as busywork or require children to disclose private experiences publicly. Provide alternatives for writing, drawing, speaking, selecting, or using assistive communication where appropriate.

### Classroom posters

Posters should provide an enduring cue: a routine, shared language, accessible reminder, or decision pathway. Text must be readable at the intended viewing distance. Decorative detail must not compete with meaning, and colour must not be the only carrier of information.

### Checklists

Checklists should support memory and reliable process, not reduce complex children to scores. Items must be observable, specific, and logically ordered. State whether the checklist is for preparation, reflection, implementation, or discussion; do not imply diagnostic validity.

### Discussion cards

Cards should invite open, dignified conversation with opt-out routes. Prompts should not force disclosure, presume a family structure, or have one emotionally “correct” answer. Include facilitation guidance for sensitive or unexpected responses.

### Reflection sheets

Reflection should encourage curiosity, repair, and future planning rather than compelled confession. Children should not be required to complete reflective writing while highly distressed. Formats must accommodate age, language, disability, and privacy.

### Routines

Routine cards should state the cue, steps, adult role, child choices, approximate duration, and adaptation options. The purpose must remain visible so the routine does not become empty compliance.

### Lesson activities

Activities should include objectives, preparation, sequence, timing, participation alternatives, discussion guidance, differentiation, safeguarding considerations, and closure. They may support a lesson but should not claim to constitute treatment or comprehensive curriculum unless they have been designed and reviewed accordingly.

All downloads require accessible digital source files where feasible, meaningful document titles, logical reading order, tagged headings, suitable contrast, legible type, alt text or text equivalents, keyboard-operable form fields when interactive, and printer-safe versions. Avoid ink-heavy backgrounds. Test monochrome printing and ordinary classroom printers. Include version, review date, licensing or usage terms, and localization identifiers.

## Multimedia

Multimedia should be chosen because it improves comprehension, demonstration, access, or recall. It should not be added merely to increase engagement.

Illustrations must follow the Illustration Guidelines: learning before decoration, emotional honesty, cultural care, consistent characters, and accessible composition. Infographics should reveal relationships or processes that prose alone makes harder to understand. They require a full text equivalent and must remain legible on mobile and in print.

Podcasts can make ideas approachable through explanation, conversation, and reflection. Every episode needs an accurate title, summary, transcript, chapters or timestamps where useful, references, and related practical resources. Audio controls must be accessible and must not autoplay.

Videos are appropriate for modelling routines, demonstrating language, explaining a visual process, or supporting professional learning. They require captions, transcripts, descriptive audio or equivalent description where visual information is essential, accessible controls, and a non-video route to the core learning.

Animations should clarify change, sequence, or interaction. They must respect reduced-motion preferences, avoid unnecessary looping, and provide pause or stop controls where required. Downloadable graphics need contextual instructions, accessible alternatives, and appropriate resolution without excessive file size.

All media should use the Design System and Component Library. Consistent controls, labels, hierarchy, and states allow teachers to focus on learning instead of relearning the interface.

## Will Talks Integration

Will Talks and the Teachers Hub serve complementary roles. Podcast episodes should introduce ideas, invite reflection, and make expert or lived perspectives accessible. Teachers Hub articles should expand, qualify, and operationalize those ideas for classroom use.

Cross-linking is standard. Relevant episode pages should link to related articles, transcripts, evidence notes, downloads, and implementation guidance. Articles should link back to the episode when listening adds explanation or perspective. Links must describe what the teacher will gain rather than using vague text such as “click here.”

An episode is not evidence merely because a guest is experienced or persuasive. Claims introduced in conversation require editorial checking before they are repeated as guidance. Personal stories should remain clearly identified as experience, not general proof. Sponsored or partner involvement must be disclosed.

Integration should help educators move from listening to action. A linked article may provide a concise summary, key cautions, a tomorrow-morning step, reflection prompts, and an adaptable classroom tool. Repetition should be purposeful, not duplicate publication.

## Classroom Diversity

No single classroom model represents the world. Resources must account for culture, disability, language, socioeconomic conditions, class size, staffing, curriculum, physical space, technology, and access to specialist services.

Cultural responsiveness begins with humility. Writers should avoid presenting eye contact, verbal participation, independence, emotional expression, family involvement, or adult-child interaction as culturally uniform. Examples should be varied and reviewed by people familiar with the intended context. Adaptation is not a cosmetic exchange of names or images.

Disability and neurodiversity must be considered from the beginning. Resources should offer multiple ways to receive information, participate, communicate, and demonstrate learning. Behaviour guidance must consider sensory access, communication, executive function, mobility, fatigue, and reasonable adjustments without assigning traits to every person with a diagnosis.

Multilingual classrooms require visual and contextual support, extra processing time, acceptance of home languages, and care not to confuse language acquisition with lack of understanding. Translation support should not isolate children or place inappropriate interpreting responsibilities on peers.

Advice must remain useful where resources are scarce. Offer low-cost and no-print options. Do not assume one-to-one devices, specialist rooms, teaching assistants, small classes, commercial programmes, or reliable internet. Where a recommendation truly depends on resources, state that dependency.

Examples should represent varied families, communities, identities, bodies, abilities, and school environments without turning representation into spectacle. Children should be shown as active participants with strengths, preferences, and agency.

## Localization

Localization preserves purpose and standards while adapting language, examples, educational structures, safeguarding routes, formats, and cultural context. Literal translation alone is insufficient.

For Taiwan, resources should use appropriate Traditional Chinese terminology, local school phases, punctuation, examples, and support routes. For Japan and Korea, language register, classroom norms, school structures, character display, and culturally meaningful scenarios require local review. For Nigeria, content must recognise linguistic and educational diversity, varying resources, class sizes, safeguarding realities, and regional context rather than treating one experience as national. For the Philippines, resources should account for multilingual education, local terminology, family and community contexts, school conditions, and relevant policy. English-language editions must not assume that British, American, or any other national usage is universal.

Each localization should:

- preserve the learning objective and psychology standard;
- adapt examples rather than translating cultural assumptions;
- use locally understood educational terms;
- verify legal, safeguarding, crisis, and referral information locally;
- accommodate text expansion, scripts, punctuation, and line breaking;
- review imagery, names, gestures, colours, and scenarios;
- provide translator context for ambiguous terms;
- receive linguistic and subject review;
- maintain version links to the source and record deviations.

Machine translation and AI may assist a first pass but cannot approve publication. A proficient human reviewer familiar with education and the target context must assess meaning, naturalness, dignity, and practical suitability. High-risk psychological or safeguarding content requires specialist local review.

Localization should not fragment the product. Core navigation, semantic components, accessibility, evidence handling, and editorial governance should remain coherent across regions. Local learning should also improve the wider system rather than flowing in one direction from English.

## Accessibility

The Accessibility Standard governs every Teachers Hub experience. Accessibility is a condition of publication, not a later enhancement.

Printable resources require legible typography, clear hierarchy, sufficient contrast, meaningful reading order, uncluttered layouts, and usable grayscale output. Teachers should be able to enlarge materials without losing meaning. Avoid instructions that depend only on colour, position, fine motor control, or visual interpretation.

Digital pages need semantic headings, landmarks, descriptive links, keyboard access, visible focus, screen-reader-compatible controls, text alternatives, zoom and reflow support, and predictable navigation. Dynamic updates should be announced appropriately without creating noise.

For dyslexia and cognitive accessibility, use plain language, manageable paragraphs, descriptive headings, generous spacing, consistent patterns, and progressive disclosure. Do not rely on a special typeface as a substitute for sound design. Offer summaries and step-by-step versions where complexity warrants them.

Captions and transcripts are required for recorded speech. Important visual information needs description. Audio must have accessible controls and no forced autoplay. Interactive activities need alternatives to drag, gesture, timed response, and precision movement.

Mobile access is essential because teachers may use the Hub on a phone while planning, travelling, or working away from a desk. Core content and downloads must remain understandable and operable at narrow widths, with touch targets and file sizes appropriate to real conditions.

Accessibility testing should include keyboard use, screen readers, zoom, reflow, contrast, monochrome printing, touch, translated layouts, and cognitive walkthroughs. Automated checks are useful but insufficient. Where possible, involve disabled educators and learners in evaluation and respond to feedback with urgency proportionate to impact.

## AI Contribution Guidelines

AI assistants may support research organisation, preliminary summaries, classroom examples, lesson idea structures, readability edits, worksheet drafts, metadata, adaptation prompts, and illustration concepts. They can accelerate careful work; they cannot supply authority.

AI may:

- organise verified source notes without changing their meaning;
- compare drafts with this handbook and related standards;
- suggest multiple classroom examples for human selection;
- identify jargon, ambiguity, repetition, and missing adaptations;
- draft worksheet structures from an approved objective;
- propose accessible descriptions and illustration briefs;
- flag claims requiring evidence or specialist review;
- assist consistency and localization preparation.

AI may not:

- invent, alter, or guess evidence and citations;
- claim certainty unsupported by sources;
- diagnose, profile, or predict individual students;
- replace professional, safeguarding, clinical, legal, or cultural judgment;
- produce final translations without qualified human review;
- infer sensitive personal information;
- reproduce identifiable student data in prompts or outputs;
- approve its own work for publication.

Contributors must verify every factual claim and citation against the original source. Generated examples must be reviewed for stereotypes, unrealistic conditions, hidden coercion, cultural assumptions, and child dignity. AI-created downloads and images require the same educational, accessibility, and design review as human-created work.

No identifiable or confidential child, family, or staff information should be entered into an AI system unless an explicitly approved, lawful, secure process exists. Data minimisation, consent, institutional policy, and applicable law must be respected.

Material AI involvement should be documented internally so reviewers understand provenance and risk. Human review is mandatory. Accountability remains with the named human owner and approvers.

## Editorial Workflow

Publication follows a traceable workflow:

**Research**

↓

**Outline**

↓

**Draft**

↓

**Evidence review**

↓

**Educational review**

↓

**Accessibility review**

↓

**Localization review**

↓

**Illustration**

↓

**Publication**

↓

**Periodic review**

### Research

Define the audience, classroom problem, practical objective, scope, risk, and evidence question. Search beyond convenient sources, record bibliographic details, note limitations, and identify where specialist or local input is required.

### Outline

Map the route from understanding to action. Include classroom context, evidence, implementation, cautions, adaptations, reflection, and related resources. Decide whether multimedia or a download serves a genuine purpose.

### Draft

Write to the approved outline and editorial standards. Keep claims attached to evidence, examples realistic, and actions proportionate. Add metadata, references, alt-text drafts, and review flags as the work develops.

### Evidence review

A reviewer checks source quality, claim accuracy, scope, uncertainty, citations, and clinical or diagnostic boundaries. High-risk material receives appropriately qualified review. Corrections are resolved before progression.

### Educational review

An experienced educator tests relevance, feasibility, developmental fit, wording, workload, and likely classroom responses. Whenever possible, a representative teacher should pilot or walk through the resource in context.

### Accessibility review

Review content, media, interaction, document structure, print behaviour, and alternatives against the Accessibility Standard. Significant issues block publication.

### Localization review

Assess translatability and cultural assumptions before source publication. For localized editions, complete linguistic, educational, cultural, and safeguarding review and record approved adaptations.

### Illustration

Visual production begins from an approved educational brief. Illustrations and graphics receive design, representation, accessibility, and contextual review. Visuals must not introduce psychological claims absent from the text.

### Publication

Confirm approvals, metadata, links, downloads, responsive behaviour, references, analytics proportionality, and rollback readiness. Publish only the approved version and retain source and review records.

### Periodic review

Assign an owner and review date based on risk and evidence stability. Review sooner when guidance changes, links fail, users report harm or confusion, translations diverge, or evidence is challenged. Corrections should be transparent and propagated to related formats and localizations.

Urgency never removes the need for safeguarding, evidence, accessibility, and accountability. It may change the size of the first release, not the minimum standard.

### Ownership and decision records

Every resource needs a named owner who is responsible for its continued accuracy and usefulness. Ownership does not mean that one person completes every review; it means someone can answer what the resource is for, which version is current, when it must be reconsidered, and who can approve a correction. Ownership should transfer explicitly when roles change so that published material never becomes effectively abandoned.

Important editorial decisions should leave a concise record. This includes decisions to use weaker evidence because stronger evidence does not exist, departures from the normal content structure, culturally specific adaptations, unresolved limitations, accessibility exceptions awaiting remediation, and reasons for withdrawing or replacing a resource. Records should be proportionate and readable. Their purpose is institutional memory and accountable maintenance, not paperwork for its own sake.

### Corrections and withdrawal

Anyone should be able to report an error, accessibility barrier, harmful implication, broken resource, or localization concern. Reports should be acknowledged, triaged by potential impact, and routed to a responsible reviewer. Safety, safeguarding, serious misinformation, privacy, and exclusionary access problems take priority over cosmetic defects.

Minor corrections may be published promptly after verification. Material changes to meaning, evidence, classroom guidance, or safeguarding information require renewed review and a version update. Corrections should propagate to downloads, transcripts, translations, related articles, cached assets, and promotional references where applicable. Quietly changing one web page while an inaccurate worksheet remains available is not a complete correction.

Content should be withdrawn when it creates unacceptable risk, no longer reflects credible evidence, cannot be made accessible within a reasonable period, or has lost the context needed for responsible use. Withdrawal notices should be clear and should direct teachers to a suitable replacement when one exists. Archived material must not remain discoverable in a way that suggests it is current guidance.

### Feedback and learning

Teacher feedback is evidence about usability and implementation, though it is not by itself proof of psychological effectiveness. Feedback systems should ask specific, respectful questions: whether the resource was understandable, feasible, adaptable, accessible, and useful; what conditions affected its use; and what unintended results occurred. Avoid collecting personal information that is not needed, especially identifiable information about children.

Patterns in feedback should inform revisions, new resources, localization priorities, and design-system improvements. Positive testimonials must never be used to conceal mixed outcomes or replace formal evaluation. When the Hub learns that an assumption was wrong, the preferred response is transparent correction and improved guidance.

Where a resource is piloted or formally evaluated, the method and intended claims should be decided before results are known. Participation must be voluntary and appropriate permissions obtained. Evaluation should examine not only engagement but also comprehension, practical use, inclusion, burden, unintended harm, and whether benefits persist. The scale of evaluation should match the significance of the claim being made.

## Quality Checklist

### Purpose and audience

- [ ] The resource has one clear practical objective.
- [ ] Its intended educator audience, learner age or phase, and context are explicit.
- [ ] The need is real and is not already met by an existing resource.
- [ ] The scope and limits are visible.
- [ ] The resource supports children's dignity, wellbeing, safety, and development.

### Educational usefulness

- [ ] The resource moves from understanding to classroom application.
- [ ] It answers “What can I do tomorrow morning?”
- [ ] Steps are realistic about time, class size, staffing, space, and materials.
- [ ] The purpose behind each action is explained.
- [ ] Examples are credible and non-idealised.
- [ ] Common difficulties and mistakes are addressed.
- [ ] Reflection and improvement guidance is included.
- [ ] Adaptations preserve the core learning objective.

### Psychology and evidence

- [ ] Psychological terms are defined accurately and used within scope.
- [ ] Claims are supported by appropriate, verified sources.
- [ ] Citations point to the original source where possible.
- [ ] Correlation, causation, averages, and individual variation are distinguished.
- [ ] Uncertainty and evidence limitations are communicated.
- [ ] No diagnosis, treatment promise, neuromyth, or unsupported mental health claim appears.
- [ ] Specialist review has been completed where risk requires it.
- [ ] Safeguarding and referral boundaries are clear.

### Relationships, inclusion, and dignity

- [ ] Guidance encourages connection before correction and co-regulation before discipline where appropriate.
- [ ] Children are not labelled, shamed, manipulated, or reduced to behaviour.
- [ ] Participation includes dignified choice and opt-out routes where needed.
- [ ] Culture, disability, neurodiversity, language, identity, and family diversity are considered.
- [ ] Examples avoid stereotypes and culturally narrow assumptions.
- [ ] System constraints are not reframed solely as teacher or child failures.

### Writing and structure

- [ ] The title and summary describe the content accurately.
- [ ] Headings are descriptive and logically ordered.
- [ ] Language is plain, calm, respectful, and actionable.
- [ ] Necessary jargon and acronyms are explained.
- [ ] Paragraphs, lists, tables, examples, and callouts serve clear purposes.
- [ ] Terminology follows the Writing Style handbook.
- [ ] The content can be translated without dependence on idioms or wordplay.

### Downloads and multimedia

- [ ] Each download or media asset extends learning rather than duplicates it.
- [ ] Usage guidance, audience, and learning purpose are supplied.
- [ ] Printable resources work in grayscale and on ordinary printers.
- [ ] Captions, transcripts, descriptions, and text equivalents are complete.
- [ ] Visuals follow the Design System and Illustration Guidelines.
- [ ] Motion can be paused or reduced and does not distract from learning.
- [ ] Files have clear names, versions, ownership, and review metadata.

### Accessibility

- [ ] Semantic structure and reading order are correct.
- [ ] Keyboard, focus, screen-reader, zoom, and reflow needs are met.
- [ ] Contrast and colour-independent meaning have been verified.
- [ ] Multiple means of understanding, participation, and response are available where appropriate.
- [ ] Mobile use and touch interaction have been tested.
- [ ] Document and download accessibility has been checked, not assumed.
- [ ] Automated and manual tests have been completed.

### Localization

- [ ] Cultural and educational assumptions have been identified.
- [ ] Local terminology, examples, school structures, and support routes are accurate.
- [ ] Text expansion, script, punctuation, and line breaking are supported.
- [ ] Localization has received proficient human review.
- [ ] High-risk localized guidance has received relevant specialist review.
- [ ] Source and localized versions remain traceable.

### Governance and publication

- [ ] Evidence, educational, accessibility, and localization approvals are recorded.
- [ ] AI involvement and generated assets are documented where material.
- [ ] No confidential or identifying information is exposed.
- [ ] Internal and external links work and have descriptive labels.
- [ ] Related articles, downloads, and Will Talks content are connected.
- [ ] An accountable owner and next review date are assigned.
- [ ] Feedback and correction routes are available.
- [ ] The final resource is consistent with all governing handbooks.

A resource is not ready when most boxes are checked. Any unmet item concerning safety, dignity, evidence integrity, accessibility, privacy, or accountability blocks publication. Other omissions require a documented rationale and approval.

## Future Vision

The Teachers Hub may eventually include certification, teacher communities, downloadable lesson packs, professional development, AI classroom assistants, webinars, and research libraries. These possibilities are directions for careful exploration, not commitments to growth at any cost.

Certification would require meaningful learning outcomes, credible assessment, identity and accessibility safeguards, transparent standards, and continuing review. It must not become a superficial badge or imply professional authority beyond its scope.

Teacher communities could support exchange, reflection, and mutual encouragement. They would require active moderation, privacy protection, safeguarding, clear professional boundaries, accessible participation, and systems that prevent anecdote or popularity from outranking evidence.

Downloadable lesson packs could connect sequenced learning, teacher guidance, family communication, adaptations, and assessment for learning. They should remain flexible enough for local curricula and must avoid presenting emotional learning as a scripted programme detached from relationships.

Professional development and webinars may provide modelling, dialogue, guided practice, and implementation support. They should be accessible across time zones, bandwidth conditions, languages, and disabilities, with recordings, transcripts, practical follow-through, and clear facilitator standards.

AI classroom assistants could help teachers find resources, adapt approved materials, prepare options, and reflect on implementation. They must operate within strict evidence, privacy, safety, transparency, and human-judgment boundaries. They must not diagnose children, automate consequential decisions, create covert profiles, or encourage reliance on unverified output.

Research libraries could make evidence easier to navigate through plain-language summaries, structured metadata, limitations, and links to original work. They should not become citation warehouses. Their value lies in helping educators judge relevance and connect evidence with careful practice.

Every future addition must answer the same questions: Does it help teachers support children more responsibly? Does it reduce the distance between psychological knowledge and everyday action? Is it accessible and culturally adaptable? Can it be maintained for ten years? Does it strengthen the connected Healthy Little Minds ecosystem without making it harder to understand?

The original mission remains the constraint and the compass: practical, trustworthy, evidence-informed support. New technology and formats are welcome only when they serve that mission, preserve human relationships and judgment, and meet the standards established in this handbook.

## Closing Reflection

> Every teacher changes lives.

Healthy Little Minds exists to make those daily decisions a little easier, more informed, and more compassionate.

The success of the Teachers Hub is measured not by page views, but by classrooms that become calmer, safer, more inclusive, and more emotionally healthy because teachers found something useful and applied it.
