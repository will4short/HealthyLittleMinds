# Interactive Stories

## Purpose

Interactive Stories are guided emotional learning experiences. They combine storytelling, decision-making, reflection, conversation, activities, and practical application so children can explore emotions safely rather than only receive information.

Stories give children enough distance to consider difficult experiences without being required to disclose their own. A child can wonder what a character may feel, try a choice, observe a consequence, and imagine repair. This creates a protected space for curiosity, empathy, language, and rehearsal.

The purpose is not to test whether a child chooses the “correct” answer. It is to help children notice context, understand that more than one response may be possible, consider how actions affect people, and practise decisions that support safety, relationships, and wellbeing.

Every story should support participation. Participation may include choosing a path, predicting what happens, listening, pointing, speaking, drawing, moving, pausing, or discussing with a trusted adult. No child should be forced to reveal a personal experience, identify an emotion immediately, or perform emotional insight for approval.

Interactive Stories are educational. They are not diagnostic tools, therapy, behaviour assessments, or crisis interventions. Counselors and therapists may use them as educational resources within their own professional judgment, but the stories do not become clinical protocols or substitutes for care.

This handbook governs story concepts, writing, branching, illustration, interaction, narration, activities, worksheets, adult guidance, testing, localization, publication, and maintenance. It applies to writers, illustrators, educators, psychologists, designers, developers, voice artists, translators, reviewers, and AI assistants.

All governing documents remain applicable. This handbook defines the additional standards needed because a story can influence a child through character identification, emotional intensity, choice mechanics, repetition, visual interpretation, and adult facilitation.

## Vision

Interactive Stories should become one of the world's leading evidence-informed emotional literacy libraries for children. Leadership should be understood as trust, educational usefulness, accessibility, cultural reach, and sustained quality—not library size, screen time, or completion volume.

The library should help children build language for inner experience, understand perspectives, practise repair, tolerate uncertainty, and make safer, kinder decisions. It should also help adults talk with children without lecturing, diagnosing, or demanding disclosure.

Stories should remain engaging without sacrificing developmental appropriateness or psychological integrity. Imagination, suspense, humour, choice, and character attachment are welcome when they serve learning and dignity. Engagement must never depend on fear, shame, compulsive loops, competition, reward pressure, or distress used as spectacle.

Over time, stories should form a coherent learning ecosystem. Characters may return across experiences. Emotion pages can explain concepts introduced in narrative. Parent and teacher guides can support application. Worksheets and activities can extend practice. Audiobooks can improve access. The same learning objective should remain recognizable across formats without becoming repetitive.

Every supported language should receive a complete, culturally reviewed experience. Children should encounter names, families, schools, routines, and communication that feel plausible while seeing meaningful human diversity. Localization should preserve character identity and emotional learning while adapting context responsibly.

The long-term measure is transfer. A successful story remains available in memory when a child faces frustration, friendship difficulty, uncertainty, conflict, or a need to ask for help.

## Educational Philosophy

Interactive Stories follow this learning model:

**Story**

↓

**Emotion**

↓

**Choice**

↓

**Reflection**

↓

**Conversation**

↓

**Practice**

↓

**Real-Life Application**

↓

**Growth**

Learning does not end when the story ends.

### Story

Narrative creates context, meaning, anticipation, and emotional connection. The story should work as a story before instructional additions are considered. Characters need motives, relationships, choices, and change—not merely roles in a lesson.

The story provides distance. It allows a child to explore “What might this character need?” before “What do I need?” This distance can make reflection safer.

### Emotion

The story helps children notice possible feelings, body signals, thoughts, needs, and action urges. It should never suggest that one face, body movement, or situation has one guaranteed emotional meaning.

Emotion vocabulary should become more precise over time. Children may remain unsure, use another word, or notice more than one feeling.

### Choice

Choice makes the child an active learner. A meaningful choice should connect to the situation, express a perspective or strategy, and create a consequence worth discussing.

Not every choice must change the plot dramatically. Some choices may change dialogue, support, pacing, or what a character notices. Choice should never be an illusion used merely to increase clicks.

### Reflection

Reflection helps the child notice why a choice mattered. It can occur through a brief question, comparison, character thought, visual cue, or adult conversation. It should not interrupt every scene or turn narrative into a quiz.

### Conversation

Conversation allows adult and child to explore meaning together. Prompts should be open, optional, and suitable for discussion about the character. Adults should be guided to listen before teaching.

### Practice

Practice turns an idea into action. It may involve rehearsing words, arranging a visual sequence, trying movement, choosing a help-seeking option, role-playing repair, or drawing a plan.

### Real-Life Application

The adult guide should connect the story's principle to ordinary moments without suggesting that the child's life matches the character's. Application should begin with a small, safe action and allow adaptation.

### Growth

Growth means increasing understanding and capacity over repeated experience. It is not a permanent transformation caused by one story. Children may need modelling, support, repetition, and repair for a long time.

The learning model is cyclical. A child may revisit a story, choose differently, notice a new feeling, or understand a character differently with age. The platform should support return without framing repetition as failure.

## Child Development Principles

Developmental principles shape story length, language, choices, emotional intensity, adult involvement, interaction, and expected learning.

### Play-based learning

Play allows children to explore possibilities, practise roles, represent experience, and exercise agency. Interactive Stories should invite imaginative participation rather than reproduce school tests on a screen.

Playful elements may include character voices, prediction, movement, pretend dialogue, visual search, or creative extension. They should have a clear relationship to the story and never obscure consent, safety, or instruction.

### Social learning

Children learn by observing how characters and trusted adults respond. Stories should model process, not perfection: noticing a problem, attempting a response, making mistakes, receiving support, repairing, and trying again.

Modelled behaviour should not imply that imitation alone produces learning. Adult discussion and practice help children interpret what they observed.

### Modelling

Characters can model emotion language, help-seeking, boundaries, perspective-taking, coping, apology, and repair. Models should sound natural and remain culturally adaptable.

Avoid an always-wise character who delivers lectures. A model is more credible when the character hesitates, needs support, or improves across attempts.

### Scaffolding

Scaffolding provides enough support for the child to participate now, then adjusts as understanding grows. Stories may offer narration, visual choices, examples, a pause, adult prompts, or fewer options before introducing greater complexity.

Support should not disappear merely because the child has completed an earlier story. Children use skills unevenly across contexts and emotional states.

### Repetition

Repeated phrases, structures, characters, and concepts can strengthen prediction and memory. Repetition should carry a learning purpose or narrative rhythm.

Do not repeat an instruction until it becomes pressure. Allow children to replay, pause, or stop. Repeated exposure to an emotionally intense scene requires particular caution.

### Curiosity

Stories should invite questions rather than provide a moral answer for every ambiguity. Curiosity helps children consider context and alternative perspectives.

Useful prompts include:

- What did you notice?
- What might the character be thinking?
- What else could be happening?
- What could make the next step easier?
- Who could help?

### Self-discovery

Children should be able to form and revise interpretations. The product must not announce what a child's choice reveals about their personality, feelings, diagnosis, or “type.”

Self-discovery is supported through private reflection and optional expression. It is undermined by scoring, profiling, public comparison, or adult pressure.

### Emotion coaching

Stories can model tentative noticing, naming, validation, boundaries, and problem-solving. They should show that feelings are acceptable while actions still have consequences.

Emotion coaching should not become constant adult narration of a child's internal state. Characters can disagree with an adult's guess.

### Executive function

Stories can support holding a goal in mind, pausing, considering options, shifting perspective, sequencing, and planning. These demands must remain developmentally appropriate.

A child may understand a choice while calm and still struggle to use it in real life. Do not present story performance as proof of executive capacity or effort.

### Language development

Stories should introduce useful vocabulary through context, repetition, illustration, and dialogue. New terms need immediate meaning. Audio, visual, and adult-supported routes should complement text.

Multilingual children may know a concept in another language. Adults should be encouraged to use the language that supports the richest connection. Limited vocabulary in the story language is not limited emotional understanding.

### Ages 4–6

Stories for younger children should generally use concrete situations, limited characters, short scenes, repeated structures, visible action, few choices, and adult facilitation. Emotional conflict should be manageable and resolution should restore sufficient safety.

Instructions should be one step at a time. Choices may use text, narration, and clear images. Reflection can focus on noticing, predicting, or choosing support rather than explaining motives.

### Ages 7–10

Stories may include longer arcs, mixed feelings, multiple perspectives, delayed consequences, more nuanced choices, and independent reflection. Language should remain direct and avoid adult clinical terminology.

Children can be invited to compare options and explain reasoning, while uncertainty and privacy remain acceptable. Stories should not become childish in tone merely because they are educational.

### Developmental variation

Age labels are guidance, not judgments. Disability, neurodiversity, language, experience, and context affect participation. Provide adult guidance and multiple access routes rather than defining a story by a rigid reading level alone.

## Story Structure

The canonical story structure is:

**Opening**

↓

**Situation**

↓

**Emotion**

↓

**Choice**

↓

**Consequence**

↓

**Reflection**

↓

**Parent or Teacher Conversation**

↓

**Activity**

↓

**Worksheet**

↓

**Growth Plan**

↓

**Related Resources**

Not every short story requires every extension, but each function should be considered.

### Opening

Introduce the character, setting, and emotional safety of the story. Establish interest without immediate distress or a manipulative cliffhanger. Children should know enough to orient themselves.

### Situation

Present a recognizable challenge that supports the learning objective. The situation should not exist only to provoke a target emotion. It needs believable context and stakes appropriate to the age group.

### Emotion

Show experience through dialogue, action, thought, body signals, illustration, or narration. Use possibilities rather than fixed interpretation. Mixed or changing feelings are welcome.

### Choice

Offer a small number of meaningful, understandable options. The child should know what each choice means before selecting. Include a safe pause, repeat, or adult-help option where appropriate.

### Consequence

Show a natural or logically related result. Consequences should teach, not punish the child for exploring. An unhelpful choice may create difficulty, but it must not humiliate the player or permanently harm the character for one decision.

### Reflection

Invite the child to notice what happened and consider alternatives. Do not label the selected path “wrong.” Reflection can say, “That choice did not give Mina the help she needed. What could she try now?”

### Parent or Teacher Conversation

Provide optional prompts, the learning purpose, listening guidance, and cautions. Adults should discuss the character first and invite personal connection rather than demand it.

### Activity

Offer one practical extension that rehearses the story's skill. State time, materials, adult role, participation alternatives, and how to stop if discomfort increases.

### Worksheet

Use a worksheet only when writing, drawing, sorting, or planning adds value. Provide non-writing alternatives. Worksheets must not require private disclosure, diagnosis, or scoring.

### Growth Plan

A growth plan connects one skill to repeated real-life practice. It should identify a small goal, available support, possible cues, reflection, and adaptation. It is not a treatment plan.

### Related Resources

Link to the most relevant emotion page, story, parent guide, teacher guide, or activity. Explain why each is useful. Avoid an endless carousel designed only to continue use.

## Story Categories

Each story should have one primary category and a clearly stated learning objective.

### Emotions

Supports noticing, vocabulary, body awareness, expression, regulation, and acceptance of emotional variation. Avoid one-face-one-feeling claims and “good” or “bad” emotion categories.

### Friendship

Explores joining, listening, reciprocity, boundaries, exclusion, disagreement, repair, and changing relationships. Do not imply that every peer must become a friend or that popularity is wellbeing.

### School

Addresses arrival, transitions, learning, help-seeking, mistakes, belonging, performance, and school relationships. Adapt school systems and avoid presenting difficulty as laziness or defiance.

### Family

Explores care, routines, change, conflict, connection, and varied family structures. Do not treat one household form as normal or require reconciliation where safety is uncertain.

### Conflict

Helps children understand disagreement, boundaries, listening, accountability, and repair. Avoid teaching appeasement, forced apology, or equal responsibility when power and harm are unequal.

### Kindness

Shows kindness as attention and action, not compliance or self-sacrifice. Include boundaries and receiving care. Do not reward children for suppressing needs to appear kind.

### Courage

Presents courage as acting with fear or uncertainty when a goal matters, including asking for help or setting a boundary. Do not frame risk-taking, silence, or endurance of harm as courage.

### Resilience

Shows adaptation supported by relationships, resources, skills, and systems. Avoid “bounce back” narratives and making children responsible for overcoming preventable adversity alone.

### Mistakes

Normalizes error, learning, accountability, repair, and trying again. A mistake should not reduce a character's worth. Distinguish accidents, skill gaps, and harmful choices without avoiding consequences.

### Problem Solving

Models defining a problem, identifying needs, generating options, considering consequences, choosing, and reviewing. Some problems require adult or systemic help rather than child ingenuity.

### Communication

Explores speaking, listening, non-verbal communication, communication devices, clarification, and help-seeking. Do not require eye contact, speech, or immediate response.

### Self-Regulation

Introduces options for noticing and responding to activation. Regulation is not permanent calm or obedience. Show co-regulation and multiple strategies rather than one universal technique.

### Bullying

Distinguishes repeated power-based harm from ordinary disagreement where developmentally appropriate. The child experiencing bullying is not responsible for solving it alone. Stories must direct children towards trusted adult help and avoid unsafe confrontation advice.

Bullying content requires safeguarding and cultural review. Do not dramatize humiliation or replay harm for entertainment.

### Inclusion

Supports belonging, access, difference, participation, and challenging exclusion. Disabled and marginalized characters should appear throughout the library, not only as subjects of inclusion lessons.

Avoid positioning one child as the lesson for others or implying that inclusion means ignoring individual needs.

### Gratitude

Explores noticing and appreciating support, experience, people, and ordinary moments. Gratitude must not dismiss sadness, injustice, unmet needs, or material hardship. Participation should never require public indebtedness.

### Curiosity

Encourages observation, questions, exploration, uncertainty, and revision of ideas. Curiosity should respect consent, privacy, and safety. A child is not entitled to personal information about another person.

### Growth Mindset

Supports the understanding that skills can develop through effective practice, support, strategy, and opportunity. Avoid slogans suggesting effort always produces success or that structural barriers and disability can be overcome through belief alone.

## Interactive Choices

Choices are educational design elements. They should reveal perspective and support reflection, not sort children into success and failure.

### Encourage reflection

Choices should connect to a meaningful question: ask for help or hide the problem; respond immediately or pause; assume intent or ask; repair or avoid. Options should be developmentally understandable and morally proportionate.

Not every option needs to be equally helpful. The story should allow recovery and explain consequences without shame.

### Avoid punishment mechanics

Do not remove points, lives, access, characters, streaks, or affection because a child explored an option. Do not use sad character reactions to guilt a child into the intended selection.

An unsafe choice may lead to a clear safety boundary and redirection. It should not produce graphic harm or a punitive ending.

### Avoid winning

There should be no “perfect” path, emotional score, leaderboard, or badge for selecting the adult-approved answer. Completion should not imply mastery, kindness, resilience, or emotional intelligence.

If a story uses collection or progress elements, they should support orientation and recall rather than status or compulsion.

### Support emotional understanding

After a choice, show what changed for the character and others. The consequence should connect action, context, and need. Ask what else could help.

### Teach consequences naturally

Natural and related consequences are preferable to arbitrary outcomes. If a character interrupts, another character may not feel heard. If a character asks for clarification, misunderstanding may reduce. Outcomes should remain plausible and not guarantee that an appropriate action always receives a positive social response.

### Safe exploration

Children should be able to replay or revisit paths without judgment. The product should never announce, “You made the wrong choice.” Use language such as:

- “Let us see what happens.”
- “That did not solve the problem yet.”
- “What could the character try next?”
- “Would you like to explore another path?”

### Number and presentation of choices

Offer a manageable number based on age and complexity. Younger children often need two visually distinct options. Older children may handle three or more when differences are clear.

Avoid choices that vary only in wording or hide essential consequences. Provide narration and accessible labels. A timeout must never select on the child's behalf.

### Branching depth

Branching should serve the learning objective. Deep branching increases cognitive and production complexity and may weaken story coherence. Prefer meaningful convergence, where different paths allow repair and return to a shared learning arc.

### Data and personalization

Choice data must not be used to infer diagnosis, emotion, personality, risk, or parenting quality. Collect only what is necessary for functionality, explain it, protect it, and allow deletion. Anonymous aggregate learning research requires appropriate governance and consent.

## Emotional Learning

Every story should intentionally support relevant elements of emotional learning. Not every story needs equal emphasis on every element.

### Emotion identification

Invite children to notice possible emotions without presenting one answer as certain. Characters may feel more than one emotion, change over time, or not know yet.

### Body signals

Show possible sensations such as warmth, tightness, energy, heaviness, or faster breathing. State through design and adult guidance that bodies differ and sensations can have many causes.

Avoid asking children to focus on body sensations when it may be uncomfortable without offering alternatives.

### Feelings vocabulary

Introduce words through context and connect broad terms to more precise ones over time. Support home-language discussion. Do not assess vocabulary as proof of emotional competence.

### Perspective taking

Help children consider what different characters know, want, feel, and assume. Do not imply that empathy requires agreement or mind-reading. A character can ask rather than guess.

### Problem solving

Model pausing, defining the problem, seeking help, considering options, and reviewing outcomes. Show when the problem exceeds the child's responsibility.

### Empathy

Empathy includes noticing, curiosity, and response while preserving boundaries. Avoid teaching children to prioritize another person's emotion over their own safety or consent.

### Repair

Show acknowledgement, listening, apology where appropriate, restitution, boundary restatement, and changed action. Do not force forgiveness or make the harmed character comfort the person who caused harm.

### Coping

Offer multiple context-sensitive supports: connection, movement, sensory change, language, rest, planning, play, or professional help. Avoid universal breathing solutions and claims that coping removes the emotion.

### Reflection

Reflection should connect experience to future choice. It may happen privately, with an adult, or through creative action. “I do not know” and passing are valid.

### Hope

Endings should provide grounded possibility: support is available, repair can begin, a skill can be practised, or a difficult experience can be carried with care. Do not guarantee that every conflict resolves or every feeling disappears.

## Parent Integration

Parents and caregivers are co-learners, not answer judges. Every story intended for family use should provide an adult guide proportionate to the topic.

### Conversation prompts

Begin with the character:

- What did you notice?
- What might the character need?
- Which choice surprised you?
- What else could the character try?
- Who could help?

Personal prompts should be optional: “Does this remind you of anything, or would you rather stay with the story?”

### Reflection questions

Help adults reflect on their own role without guilt:

- Which part of the story was easiest for the child to discuss?
- What support did the character receive?
- How does our family usually handle a similar moment?
- What is one phrase or routine we could practise?

### Activities

Family activities should be brief, low-cost, and adaptable. Explain the learning objective, time, preparation, adult role, child choice, and stopping point. Provide no-print alternatives.

### Follow-up ideas

Suggest ways to revisit the principle during ordinary routines. Avoid instructing parents to monitor the child constantly or turn every moment into a lesson.

### Real-world practice

Practice should happen when the child is sufficiently settled. Adults can model first, use pretend scenarios, and notice attempts. Do not expect the child to reproduce a story choice under high stress immediately.

### Adult guidance

Include common misunderstandings, language to avoid, adaptations, and professional boundaries. For sensitive stories, provide content notes, readiness guidance, and support pathways.

Adults should never use a story as proof that a child understands, agrees, or is ready to discuss a personal experience.

## Teacher Integration

Interactive Stories can support whole-class, small-group, or individual educational use. Teacher guidance should connect naturally to the Teachers Hub.

### Classroom application

State age or phase, learning objective, duration, preparation, group size, materials, participation alternatives, safeguarding considerations, and follow-up. Offer a minimum version for limited time.

### Lesson ideas

Activities may support prediction, vocabulary, role-play, discussion, sorting options, collaborative problem-solving, drawing, or writing. They should not become a complete lesson plan unless designed and reviewed as one.

### Social-emotional learning

Map the story to relevant emotional and relationship skills without treating one framework as culturally universal. Preserve child agency and avoid equating SEL with compliance.

### Discussion

Teachers should discuss characters before inviting personal examples. No child should be required to disclose, act out distress, or identify a classmate. Establish respectful discussion and pass options.

### Classroom routines

Connect story language to a small number of real routines, such as asking for help, pausing during conflict, transition support, or repair. The classroom version may differ from home while preserving the principle.

### Group activities

Activities should support participation without ranking emotional insight. Avoid competitive quizzes, public scores, and rewards for disclosure. Offer non-verbal and assistive-communication routes.

### Downloads

Teacher downloads need clear facilitation notes, accessible print design, answers or discussion guidance where relevant, localization, and alternatives to writing or printing.

### Safeguarding

Stories may prompt disclosures. Teacher guides must remind educators to follow local safeguarding procedures, avoid promising secrecy, listen without investigating, and involve designated support.

### Teachers Hub links

Link to deeper psychology explanations, classroom strategies, inclusive adaptations, and related resources. The story should not carry all professional guidance within the child experience.

## Illustrations

The Illustration Guidelines govern style, character design, diversity, emotional expression, continuity, accessibility, localization, and AI use.

### Consistency

Characters should remain recognizable across scenes, paths, formats, and localizations. Maintain proportions, clothing identity, assistive devices, skin tone, hair, age, and established personality unless change is part of the story.

### Warmth

Warmth comes from composition, relationship, gesture, and visual care—not constant smiling, excessive cuteness, or saturated decoration. Difficult emotions can be shown honestly without frightening exaggeration.

### Clarity

Illustrations should make the action, relationship, choice, or setting easier to understand. Remove visual clutter that competes with the learning objective. Choice images must be distinct and not bias selection through beauty or brightness alone.

### Diversity

The library should represent varied cultures, bodies, skin tones, hair, disabilities, neurodivergence, communication methods, families, homes, and schools across ordinary plots. Marginalized characters should not appear only in stories about difficulty or inclusion.

### Accessibility

Meaning must not depend only on colour, subtle expression, or visual detail. Provide alt text or extended descriptions according to purpose. Test contrast, grayscale, zoom, and cognitive clarity.

### Emotional expression

Use face, body, context, words, and action together. Do not depict one universal “anger face” or “anxiety posture.” Characters can mask, express differently, or correct another character's interpretation.

### Character continuity

Maintain a character reference record covering visual identity, voice, relationships, communication, strengths, preferences, and learning history. New stories should not reset growth or change personality merely to fit a lesson.

### Branch continuity

Every branch must preserve setting, object position, emotional state, and prior action. Visual inconsistencies can make cause and consequence difficult to understand.

### Text in images

Externalize translatable text wherever possible. In-world signs and writing require localization, alternative text, and source assets. Never make essential instruction available only within an image.

## Accessibility

The Accessibility Standard applies from concept through testing. Interactive Stories should provide multiple ways to perceive, navigate, understand, and participate.

### Audio narration

Narration should be optional, controllable, and synchronized with the current scene. Use clear performance, appropriate pacing, pronunciation guidance, and consistent character voices. Do not autoplay unexpectedly.

Provide replay, pause, volume, and narration-off controls. Essential information in sound requires a visual or textual equivalent.

### Captions

Audio and video require accurate human-reviewed captions. Identify speakers and meaningful sounds. Captions should not cover choice controls, faces, or important illustration details.

### Text highlighting

Optional synchronized highlighting may support tracking and language learning. It should follow meaningful phrases, not flash rapidly or force pacing. Users must be able to disable it.

Highlighting is not a substitute for readable typography or narration controls.

### Screen readers

Use semantic landmarks, headings, buttons, lists, progress information, and live-region announcements. Describe scene purpose and choices without overwhelming detail. Reading and focus order must follow the story.

Choice feedback should be announced. Decorative images remain hidden. Complex illustrations receive appropriate extended description.

### Cognitive accessibility

Use predictable controls, one main task per scene, manageable choices, visible progress, consistent character names, simple recovery, and clear pause or exit. Avoid memory-heavy instructions and unexpected timers.

### Language simplicity

Match vocabulary and syntax to the intended audience while preserving meaning. Define new emotion words through context. Adult guidance should remain separate from child-facing language.

### Adjustable pacing

Children must control progression. No essential text, choice, or reflection should disappear automatically. Provide pause, repeat, back, and resume where these do not undermine safety or narrative coherence.

### Motor access

Controls require accessible targets, keyboard operation, switch and assistive-technology compatibility where applicable, and alternatives to drag, swipe, gesture, or precision movement. Do not make timing or rapid response necessary.

### Visual access

Support contrast, text resizing, zoom, reflow, colour-independent meaning, reduced motion, and high-contrast modes. Avoid text embedded in backgrounds that reduces legibility.

### Hearing access

All spoken content needs text. Sound cues need visual equivalents. Activities should remain complete without audio.

### Emotional access

Provide content notes for sensitive stories, an easy exit, adult guidance, and the ability to skip a scene without penalty. Avoid surprise distress, escalating sound, or forced replay.

### Offline and low-bandwidth access

Where feasible, offer lightweight, downloadable, printable, or audio-only alternatives. Core emotional learning should not require a high-performance device or continuous connection.

Accessibility defects involving core meaning or interaction block publication. Automated testing alone is insufficient; children and adults with varied access needs should participate in testing where possible.

## Localization

The Localization Guidelines govern every language and regional edition. Story localization includes narrative voice, dialogue, names, images, school settings, families, choices, activities, narration, captions, worksheets, and support information.

### Names

Names should be natural, varied, pronounceable in audio, and consistent across recurring content. Do not assign marginalised identities repeatedly to conflict or need. A recurring canonical character may retain their name when it works naturally and identity continuity matters.

### School contexts

Adapt school phase, uniforms, classroom roles, daily timetable, lunch, transport, support staff, discipline, and help-seeking. Preserve the learning objective without inventing a local role that does not exist.

### Cultural examples

Review food, games, gestures, holidays, weather, housing, routines, clothing, technology, communication, and social expectations. Surface substitution is not enough if the underlying conflict remains implausible.

### Family structures

Represent and adapt parents, caregivers, grandparents, guardians, foster families, siblings, extended family, and multiple-home arrangements respectfully. Do not assume one family hierarchy or form of address.

### Emotional learning

Emotion terms may not map one-to-one. Preserve the function—notice, communicate, understand, respond—while allowing natural local vocabulary, story-based exploration, or private reflection.

### Dialogue and read-aloud quality

Literary and child-language review is necessary. Dialogue should sound natural when spoken, match character and relationship, and preserve age appropriateness. Translate rhythm and humour by function, not word order.

### Branch integrity

Every localized choice must lead logically to its consequence. Choice labels should remain distinct and not change moral or emotional weight unintentionally. Test all paths in the target language.

### Audio and captions

Localized narration needs performance direction, pronunciation, timing, and accessibility review. Captions and highlighting must synchronize with the localized audio, not the source timing.

### Local professional review

Psychology, education, safeguarding, and support pathways require local review. Cultural adaptation must not weaken child dignity or normalize harmful practice.

Each edition needs sustainable human ownership and update capacity. Raw machine translation or script conversion is never publication-ready localization.

## AI Contribution

AI may assist story development, but it does not provide developmental, literary, psychology, cultural, artistic, or ethical authority.

AI may help:

- generate alternative dialogue from an approved scene purpose;
- suggest possible branches for human evaluation;
- draft reflection prompts from a defined learning objective;
- organize activity and worksheet structures;
- identify continuity questions or repeated terminology;
- compare a draft with governance checklists;
- produce early localization drafts for qualified review.

AI may never:

- replace developmental or child-user review;
- invent psychological mechanisms, evidence, or citations;
- diagnose, profile, or infer traits from a child's choices;
- change canonical characters, relationships, identity, or visual design without approval;
- remove or dilute the educational intent;
- introduce punishment, scoring, coercion, stereotypes, or unsafe advice;
- publish dialogue, images, narration, translations, or activities without human review;
- imitate a real person's voice or likeness without explicit lawful consent;
- approve its own output.

Generated dialogue should be reviewed aloud for naturalness, age, culture, warmth, and character continuity. Branches should be checked for false choice, shame, logical inconsistency, hidden risk, and whether one path is made attractive through visual bias rather than meaning.

AI-generated illustrations follow the Illustration Guidelines. They require consistent character references, anatomy review, representation review, alt text, localization, and documentation. Style imitation that violates rights or project integrity is not acceptable.

Do not enter identifiable child information, private family stories, therapy content, or unpublished sensitive material into an unapproved system. Material AI involvement should be recorded. Human review is mandatory, and named human owners remain accountable.

### Research

Define audience, developmental context, emotional topic, evidence, likely sensitivities, cultural assumptions, and ecosystem need. Review existing stories to avoid duplication and check how the concept is represented elsewhere.

Research should include child-development, psychology, educational, accessibility, and lived-context perspectives. High-risk topics require early safeguarding and specialist consultation.

### Learning Objective

Write one primary objective in observable educational terms. For example: “Children will practise considering more than one explanation for a friend's behaviour.”

Avoid objectives such as “Children will become empathetic” or “Children will stop getting angry.” Identify secondary vocabulary or practice goals separately.

### Story Outline

Define characters, setting, emotional arc, situation, choices, consequences, convergence, reflection, adult role, activity, and related resources. Map each branch and explain how it supports the objective.

Conduct a risk review for distress, shame, forced disclosure, stereotyping, unsafe imitation, and unintended moral messages.

### Psychology Review

A suitably qualified reviewer checks emotional concepts, developmental fit, body-signal language, behavioural interpretation, coping, consequences, diagnostic boundaries, evidence, and foreseeable harm. Sensitive content receives appropriate specialist and safeguarding review.

### Writing

Develop narrative and dialogue according to the approved outline. Maintain character voice, age-appropriate language, read-aloud rhythm, and meaningful choice. Keep adult guidance and child story text distinct.

Read every path aloud. Create a branch script or content map that allows reviewers to inspect the full experience without manually replaying every path.

### Illustration

Create character and scene briefs from the approved story. Produce key visual references before full scene work. Review consistency, expression, context, diversity, accessibility, and localization.

Do not complete the whole visual set before validating whether the scene design communicates action and choice clearly.

### Interaction Design

Prototype navigation, choice presentation, feedback, pacing, audio, progress, pause, replay, exit, and recovery. Reuse established components before inventing new patterns.

Interaction must work without gamification, hidden tracking, or visual pressure. Define all states: loading, selected, unavailable, error, offline, resumed, completed, and reduced-motion.

### Accessibility Review

Review semantic structure, focus, keyboard, switch access, touch, screen readers, narration, captions, highlighting, contrast, zoom, reflow, motion, cognitive load, and emotional access. Include accessible alternatives for activities and downloads.

### Localization Review

Assess source readiness, translatability, cultural assumptions, text expansion, image changes, dialogue, terminology, branch logic, narration, school context, and support information. Complete target-language review in the functioning story.

### Testing

Testing should include:

- child comprehension and emotional interpretation;
- adult facilitation and practical usefulness;
- branch logic and continuity;
- whether choices feel safe and meaningful;
- whether children understand they can pause or explore;
- accessibility with relevant users and assistive technology;
- mobile, tablet, desktop, low bandwidth, and interruption recovery;
- localized language, culture, layout, and audio;
- privacy, analytics, and data minimization;
- error and offline states.

Do not ask children to disclose personal emotional experiences as a condition of testing. Obtain appropriate consent and assent, protect privacy, and make stopping easy.

### Publication

Confirm story, psychology, illustration, interaction, accessibility, localization, privacy, and safeguarding approvals. Verify metadata, age guidance, content notes, adult guides, transcripts, captions, downloads, related resources, ownership, version, and review date.

Marketing must represent the story accurately and avoid promises about emotional outcomes.

### Periodic Review

Review stories according to evidence stability, sensitivity, reach, user feedback, accessibility findings, and localized changes. Trigger earlier review after psychology guidance changes, reports of harm, broken interactions, character inconsistency, support-link changes, or evolving terminology.

Corrections should propagate to branches, audio, captions, worksheets, illustrations, adult guides, localizations, and marketing. Withdraw a story when risk cannot be mitigated promptly.

## Success Metrics

Success is measured by engagement, understanding, conversation, practice, emotional vocabulary, confidence, and real-life transfer—not completion rate alone.

### Engagement

Healthy engagement means attention, curiosity, voluntary return, and meaningful participation. It is not maximum time on screen, repeated clicking, streaks, or inability to stop.

Observe where children pause, replay, ask questions, or seek adult help. A child leaving a story because they have learned enough or need a break is not failure.

### Understanding

Can children identify the central situation, consider possible feelings, explain how a choice affected the characters, and recognize more than one response? Use conversation, retelling, prediction, and observation rather than only quizzes.

### Conversation

Do stories support respectful child-adult discussion? Assess whether prompts feel natural, allow privacy, and help adults listen. Conversation quality matters more than number of responses.

### Practice

Do children and adults use the activity, language, or routine beyond the immediate story? Practice should be voluntary, safe, and adapted.

### Emotional vocabulary

Can children use or recognize more precise emotion words in appropriate contexts? Vocabulary growth should not be treated as complete emotional understanding.

### Confidence

Do children appear more willing to ask questions, explore options, seek help, or try repair? Confidence is not constant certainty or independent performance.

### Real-life transfer

Can the child or adult recognize when the story's principle may help in another situation? Transfer may be supported through adult reports, classroom observation, follow-up activities, and ethical research.

### Completion

Completion can identify technical or narrative barriers but does not prove learning. Do not modify story integrity merely to increase final-page reach.

### Equity and accessibility

Measure who can access, understand, and benefit from stories. Review differences by language, device, disability, reading access, context, and region without profiling children or collecting unnecessary sensitive data.

### Evaluation ethics

Collect the minimum data needed, explain its purpose, protect privacy, obtain appropriate consent, and avoid inferring diagnosis or emotion from choices. Child research requires suitable ethical oversight.

## Quality Checklist

### Purpose and learning objective

- [ ] The story has one clear primary emotional-learning objective.
- [ ] The objective is educational, observable, and developmentally appropriate.
- [ ] Interactive storytelling is the right format for the objective.
- [ ] The story adds value rather than duplicating an existing experience.
- [ ] Scope and non-clinical boundaries are clear.

### Story quality

- [ ] The narrative works as a story, not a disguised lesson.
- [ ] Characters have believable motives, relationships, and agency.
- [ ] The opening orients without manipulating distress.
- [ ] The situation is recognizable and age-appropriate.
- [ ] Emotional stakes remain safe and proportionate.
- [ ] Dialogue sounds natural when read aloud.
- [ ] The ending offers grounded hope without false resolution.
- [ ] The story avoids a final moral lecture.

### Child development

- [ ] Language, length, pacing, characters, and choices match the intended age range.
- [ ] Play, modelling, scaffolding, repetition, curiosity, and discovery serve learning.
- [ ] Expected executive-function and language demands are realistic.
- [ ] Adult support is identified where needed.
- [ ] Age guidance allows developmental variation.
- [ ] Children are not expected to perform insight beyond their capacity.

### Psychology and emotional learning

- [ ] Emotion identification allows uncertainty and multiple possibilities.
- [ ] Body signals are possibilities, not diagnostic signs.
- [ ] Vocabulary is introduced through meaningful context.
- [ ] Perspective-taking avoids mind-reading.
- [ ] Empathy does not require unsafe self-sacrifice.
- [ ] Coping includes multiple options and co-regulation.
- [ ] Repair includes accountability without forced forgiveness.
- [ ] Regulation is not presented as obedience or permanent calm.
- [ ] Resilience includes relationships, resources, and systems.
- [ ] Psychology review and evidence records are complete.

### Interactive choices

- [ ] Each choice is understandable and connected to the learning objective.
- [ ] The number of choices is manageable.
- [ ] Options are not visually biased towards the intended answer.
- [ ] Choices support reflection rather than winning.
- [ ] No points, lives, streaks, or punitive losses undermine learning.
- [ ] Consequences are natural or logically related.
- [ ] Unhelpful choices allow recovery and repair.
- [ ] No path humiliates, frightens, or permanently punishes the child.
- [ ] Replay, pause, and exit are available without penalty.
- [ ] Choice data are not used to diagnose or profile.

### Story structure and extensions

- [ ] Opening, situation, emotion, choice, consequence, and reflection form a coherent arc.
- [ ] Adult conversation prompts are optional and character-first.
- [ ] Activities practise the objective rather than repeat the story.
- [ ] Worksheets add value and provide non-writing alternatives.
- [ ] Growth plans remain small, voluntary, and non-clinical.
- [ ] Related resources answer likely next questions.
- [ ] Learning extends beyond the final page without creating pressure.

### Parent integration

- [ ] Adult guidance explains purpose and boundaries.
- [ ] Prompts support listening rather than testing.
- [ ] Personal disclosure is optional.
- [ ] Follow-up actions are realistic and low-burden.
- [ ] Common misunderstandings and adaptations are included.
- [ ] Parents are co-learners, not answer judges.
- [ ] Professional support routes are included where appropriate.

### Teacher integration

- [ ] Classroom objective, context, time, materials, and group format are clear.
- [ ] Discussion protects privacy and includes pass options.
- [ ] Group activities avoid competition and public emotional scoring.
- [ ] Classroom routines preserve the story's principle.
- [ ] Safeguarding guidance is present where disclosure is possible.
- [ ] Teachers Hub resources deepen professional application.
- [ ] Downloads are accessible and practical in real classrooms.

### Inclusion and dignity

- [ ] Family, culture, language, disability, neurodiversity, gender, and resources are considered.
- [ ] Characters are not reduced to identities or difficulties.
- [ ] Marginalized characters appear in varied ordinary roles.
- [ ] Communication does not require speech or eye contact.
- [ ] Boundaries, consent, and privacy remain visible.
- [ ] No familiar cultural practice is accepted without safety review.
- [ ] Examples avoid tokenism and stereotypes.

### Illustrations

- [ ] Visuals clarify action, emotion, context, and choice.
- [ ] Characters remain consistent across scenes and branches.
- [ ] Expressions are varied and not treated as universal signals.
- [ ] Choice visuals do not manipulate selection.
- [ ] Composition is calm and free from unnecessary clutter.
- [ ] Diversity is integrated naturally across the library.
- [ ] Colour-independent meaning and contrast are verified.
- [ ] Alt text or extended description is complete.
- [ ] In-image text is externalized or localized accessibly.

### Accessibility

- [ ] The story can be completed with keyboard and assistive technology.
- [ ] Focus and screen-reader order follow the narrative.
- [ ] Buttons, choices, states, and feedback have clear accessible names.
- [ ] Narration is optional, controllable, and synchronized.
- [ ] Captions and transcripts are accurate.
- [ ] Text highlighting is optional and supports meaningful phrases.
- [ ] Pacing is child-controlled without forced timers.
- [ ] Drag, gesture, and precision actions have alternatives.
- [ ] Text resizing, zoom, reflow, contrast, and reduced motion work.
- [ ] Cognitive load and emotional access have been tested.
- [ ] Offline or low-bandwidth alternatives exist where feasible.

### Localization

- [ ] Names, families, schools, routines, food, gestures, and dialogue are culturally reviewed.
- [ ] Emotion and psychology terms preserve function and nuance.
- [ ] Every localized branch remains logically coherent.
- [ ] Text expansion and script-specific layout work.
- [ ] Narration, captions, and highlighting synchronize in the target language.
- [ ] Images have been adapted where context requires it.
- [ ] Local education, safeguarding, and support information is accurate.
- [ ] Qualified linguistic, cultural, psychology, and accessibility reviews are recorded.

### Interaction and technology

- [ ] Established design-system components are reused.
- [ ] Loading, selected, error, offline, resumed, and completed states are designed.
- [ ] The story preserves progress appropriately.
- [ ] Navigation is predictable and recovery is easy.
- [ ] No dark pattern, autoplay, compulsion, or engagement pressure appears.
- [ ] Data collection is minimal, transparent, secure, and deletable.
- [ ] Mobile, tablet, desktop, and realistic devices are tested.

### AI

- [ ] AI-generated dialogue has human literary and developmental review.
- [ ] Suggested branches were checked for shame, bias, risk, and false choice.
- [ ] Psychology, citations, and terminology were independently verified.
- [ ] Canonical characters and visual identity remain unchanged unless approved.
- [ ] AI-generated images meet illustration and accessibility standards.
- [ ] No real voice, likeness, or style was used without appropriate rights.
- [ ] No identifiable child data entered an unapproved system.
- [ ] Material AI involvement is documented.

### Testing and governance

- [ ] Child testing used appropriate consent, assent, privacy, and stopping rights.
- [ ] Children were not required to disclose personal experience.
- [ ] Adult facilitation was tested with parents or educators.
- [ ] All paths, links, downloads, audio, and states function correctly.
- [ ] Feedback from disabled and localized users was included where feasible.
- [ ] Story, psychology, illustration, interaction, accessibility, localization, and safeguarding approvals are recorded.
- [ ] The resource has a named owner, version, publication date, and next review.
- [ ] Corrections, reports, and withdrawal routes exist.

Any unresolved issue involving child safety, psychological misinformation, diagnosis, safeguarding, privacy, accessibility, coercion, discrimination, or punitive interaction blocks publication.

## Future Vision

Interactive Stories may evolve through adaptive stories, multiplayer classroom stories, teacher dashboards, family learning journeys, richer voice narration, AI-supported personalization, and new languages. These possibilities must preserve the original educational mission.

### Adaptive stories

Adaptation could change vocabulary support, narration, pacing, number of choices, or recommended practice based on explicit user preference and accessibility need. It must not infer a child's diagnosis, personality, emotion, or capability from choices.

Adaptation should remain transparent and easy to reset. Every path needs review; personalization cannot generate ungoverned psychological advice.

### Multiplayer classroom stories

Classroom experiences could allow groups to discuss and choose collectively. They should support reasoning and perspective rather than majority rule as moral truth.

Provide anonymous or private participation, prevent public ranking, and help teachers manage disagreement. No child's selection should reveal a presumed emotional profile to peers or staff.

### Teacher dashboards

Dashboards could organize stories, guides, accessibility settings, lesson ideas, and class-level progress through assigned content. They must not score emotional intelligence, diagnose students, or display sensitive individual choices unnecessarily.

Data should be minimal, purposeful, role-based, and time-limited. Teachers need clear interpretation and privacy responsibilities.

### Family learning journeys

Journeys could connect stories, conversation, activities, emotion pages, and reflection across a chosen theme. They should remain optional, flexible, and free from streaks, guilt, or comparison.

Families should be able to pause, skip, repeat, or choose another route. Progress should represent content visited, not emotional mastery.

### Voice narration

Future narration may include multiple languages, character voices, adjustable pace, and audio-first experiences. Performance should remain warm and clear without emotional exaggeration.

Synthetic narration requires transparent use, appropriate rights, human quality review, and safeguards against imitating real people. Human narration may remain preferable for emotionally nuanced stories.

### AI-supported personalization

AI could help adults find approved stories, adjust an activity's format, or explain vocabulary using governed content. It must not generate unreviewed branches in real time, diagnose the child, interpret choices psychologically, or provide crisis advice.

Any assistant requires approved-source grounding, privacy protection, human escalation, uncertainty, accessibility, localization, auditability, and ongoing harm evaluation.

### New languages

New-language expansion requires script and layout support, literary translation, child-language review, cultural adaptation, psychology and safeguarding review, narration, captions, branch QA, accessible resources, and sustainable ownership.

The platform architecture should support right-to-left and complex scripts, varied text length, local input, and language-specific audio without redesigning every story.

### New interaction modes

Future voice input, gesture, augmented reality, physical classroom tools, or shared-screen experiences must have accessible alternatives and clear educational value. Novelty alone is not a reason to add an interaction.

### Stewardship questions

Every future proposal should answer:

- Does this deepen emotional understanding or practice?
- Does it preserve story quality and child agency?
- Does it avoid winning, punishment, compulsion, and profiling?
- Is the psychology evidence-informed and non-diagnostic?
- Does it protect privacy, consent, safety, and dignity?
- Is it accessible and culturally adaptable?
- Can every path be reviewed, corrected, localized, and maintained?
- Does it connect responsibly with the wider ecosystem?

The platform should prefer fewer, better stories to rapid expansion. A story is a relationship with the child; it deserves long-term care.

## Closing Reflection

Children rarely remember every lesson they are taught.

They remember stories.

Healthy Little Minds exists to ensure that the stories children remember also teach empathy, resilience, emotional understanding, and hope.

The greatest success of an Interactive Story is not reaching the final page—it is helping a child make a kinder, wiser decision long after the story has ended.
