# Will Talks

## Purpose

Will Talks is the official public psychology education platform and podcast of Healthy Little Minds. It exists to bridge the gap between psychology research and everyday life.

Episodes should help people understand why humans may think, feel, learn, relate, decide, and behave as they do. They should also show how psychological knowledge can inform practical choices without turning general education into individualized advice.

Will Talks is not therapy. It is not self-help entertainment. It is not sensational psychology. It does not diagnose listeners, treat mental health conditions, provide crisis intervention, or replace qualified professional care. The platform educates and directs listeners towards appropriate support when general education is not enough.

The central purpose is psychological literacy: the ability to understand psychological ideas, judge claims with appropriate care, recognize human variation, and apply knowledge responsibly. Knowing a term is not enough. A listener should understand what it means, what evidence supports it, what it does not mean, and how it may shape one realistic decision.

Public psychology communication creates particular responsibilities. Spoken claims can feel more certain than written ones. A compelling story can outweigh statistics in memory. A guest's authority can discourage scrutiny. Short clips can remove qualifications. Every stage—from title to transcript to promotional excerpt—must therefore preserve evidence, context, dignity, and scope.

This handbook governs all Will Talks episodes, videos, articles, show notes, social posts, shorts, audiograms, future courses, and AI-assisted production. It defines how the platform operates within the wider Healthy Little Minds ecosystem. The Project Charter and Product Principles remain the highest authority; all content, psychology, referencing, localization, accessibility, design, and product standards apply in full.

## Vision

Will Talks should become one of the world's most trusted public psychology education platforms. Trust should be earned through clarity, humility, scientific integrity, accessibility, practical usefulness, and responsible correction—not controversy, celebrity, certainty, or reach alone.

The long-term aim is to make careful psychological thinking part of everyday conversation. A parent should be able to understand a child's behaviour with greater curiosity. A teacher should find a classroom implication without being asked to become a clinician. A student should learn how evidence is evaluated. A young adult should gain language for experience without receiving an online diagnosis. A professional should recognize accurate public translation rather than oversimplification.

The platform should become a connected body of learning. Episodes should build on one another, revisit foundational concepts, correct common misconceptions, and connect listeners to articles, stories, worksheets, interactive tools, the Parents Hub, the Teachers Hub, and the Emotion Library. Discovery should lead to deeper understanding and useful practice, not an endless engagement loop.

International growth should come through accessible production, culturally responsive localization, multilingual subtitles and transcripts, locally reviewed examples, and partnerships that preserve the same standards. The platform should not assume that English-language research or one educational and family context represents the world.

Will Talks should remain recognizable over time even as formats, hosts, guests, and technology change. Its reputation should rest on a dependable promise: difficult ideas will be made understandable without being made dishonest, and human difficulty will be discussed without being used as entertainment.

## Editorial Philosophy

### Curiosity before certainty

Will Talks begins with questions. Curiosity distinguishes observation from inference, explores credible alternatives, and resists the urge to explain a person through one concept.

Hosts should ask:

- What do we actually know?
- How was it studied?
- What else could explain this?
- For whom and under what conditions might it apply?
- What does the idea help us notice?
- Where does the concept stop being useful?

Curiosity does not mean avoiding conclusions. Strong evidence can support clear statements, and safety may require direct guidance. The aim is calibrated confidence rather than permanent ambiguity.

Questions should invite learning, not manufacture suspense. Titles such as “The hidden reason you sabotage every relationship” pretend to know the listener and turn uncertainty into a hook. A better question is “Why do people repeat familiar relationship patterns—and what can change?”

### Evidence before opinion

Research, professional guidance, theory, clinical experience, personal experience, and host interpretation should remain distinguishable. An articulate guest, moving story, or familiar idea is not evidence of a general claim.

Every material psychological or educational claim requires an evidence record. The Referencing Policy governs source selection and maintenance. When evidence is weak or contested, the episode should say so. When a discussion is primarily reflective or experiential, it should be introduced as such.

Opinion has a legitimate place when clearly identified. A guest may explain how they approach a problem in their work. The host should ask what informs that approach and whether it reflects evidence, professional judgment, or personal preference.

### Education before engagement

Attention is necessary for learning, but engagement tactics must not distort the subject. Hooks, titles, thumbnails, trailers, chapter names, and clips should accurately represent the episode and its qualifications.

Will Talks does not use fear, diagnostic curiosity, conflict, humiliation, false urgency, outrage, or emotional disclosure to capture attention. It does not withhold the answer unnecessarily or promise a secret that responsible sources have overlooked.

When educational integrity makes a message less clickable, preserve integrity. A smaller audience that understands and trusts the work is more valuable than a larger audience reached through misrepresentation.

### Understanding before judgement

Psychology can help people examine the conditions influencing action. Will Talks should describe behaviour before labelling it and consider development, context, relationships, learning, culture, disability, stress, and systems.

Understanding is not excuse-making. Harm, boundaries, responsibility, and repair can be discussed clearly. The platform should avoid moral categories that divide people into good and bad parents, difficult and easy children, healthy and toxic relationships, or rational and irrational thinkers.

The host should resist diagnosing absent people, public figures, fictional characters treated as real cases, or individuals described by listeners. A concept should illuminate complexity, not end inquiry.

### Compassion before criticism

Critical thinking should be directed at ideas, evidence, systems, and actions without humiliating people. When correcting misinformation, state the accurate understanding first, explain why the misconception is attractive, and show what changes in practice.

Compassion does not require false balance or polite treatment of harmful claims. It requires proportionate language, evidence, and attention to the people affected. Avoid mockery, reaction content, pile-ons, and excerpts designed to make another speaker look foolish.

### Practice before inspiration

An episode should do more than create a temporary feeling of insight. It should help listeners notice, discuss, try, reflect, or seek support.

Practical takeaways must remain proportionate to evidence and format. They should identify one or more manageable actions, explain the purpose, note adaptations, and state when the idea is not enough. A moving conclusion without a usable next step may inspire but does not complete the educational task.

Practice can include better observation, a conversation question, a classroom adjustment, a family routine, a way to evaluate a claim, or preparation for professional discussion. Not every episode requires a behavioural exercise; sometimes the responsible action is to reconsider an assumption or avoid an unsupported method.

## Audience

Will Talks serves a broad public audience while preserving enough structure for each listener to find relevant meaning.

### General public

General-audience episodes should assume interest but not psychology training. Define necessary terms, use familiar examples, and explain why evidence matters. Avoid assuming that listeners are parents, teachers, students, or receiving mental health care.

### Parents and caregivers

Parent-relevant content should translate concepts into relationships, communication, routines, boundaries, observation, and help-seeking. It must avoid guilt, perfectionism, deterministic attachment and brain claims, and advice that assumes unlimited time or resources.

Parent takeaways should connect to the Parents Hub for fuller context and adaptation. A podcast cannot assess an individual child.

### Teachers and educators

Teacher-relevant content should identify classroom implications, practical constraints, developmental variation, inclusion, and professional boundaries. It should not ask teachers to diagnose, treat, or solve systemic problems alone.

Episodes should connect to Teachers Hub resources that operationalize the idea with classroom context, steps, adaptations, and downloads.

### University students

Students may value concepts, methods, history, evidence appraisal, and links to further reading. Episodes should model how claims are evaluated rather than merely simplify conclusions.

Do not present Will Talks as a substitute for coursework, supervised practice, or original sources. Show where introductory metaphors differ from full theory.

### Young adults

Young adults need respectful content about identity, relationships, emotion, learning, work, uncertainty, and mental health literacy. Avoid diagnosis by checklist, generational stereotypes, false intimacy, and self-optimization pressure.

Offer agency and credible support pathways. Do not frame ordinary uncertainty as pathology or every difficulty as a personal mindset problem.

### Professionals

Professionals should find the public translation accurate and ethically bounded. Episodes may include methodological detail and specialist guests, but the main path should remain understandable to non-specialists.

Professional listeners should be directed to original sources and full guidance. The platform should not imply continuing education credit or clinical competence unless a future product is formally designed and governed for that purpose.

### Mental health learners

Learners exploring psychology independently need help distinguishing evidence, theory, popular concepts, diagnosis, and personal experience. Episodes should model skepticism without cynicism and curiosity without self-diagnosis.

### Lifelong learners

Lifelong learners may enter through history, books, relationships, neuroscience, or everyday behaviour. Content should respect intellectual curiosity while returning to practical and ethical meaning.

### Serving multiple audiences

One episode can remain accessible through layered structure:

1. begin with a shared everyday question;
2. define the concept plainly;
3. explain evidence and limits;
4. use examples from more than one context;
5. label audience-specific implications;
6. provide links for deeper application.

Do not force every audience into every episode. Metadata should identify primary and secondary audiences. An episode may be general in explanation and specific in application.

## Episode Categories

Each episode should have one primary category and may have secondary categories. Categories support editorial boundaries, discovery, playlists, and cross-linking.

### Psychology Explained

Defines and contextualizes a psychological concept. Episodes should explain what the concept means, how it is studied, why it matters, common misunderstandings, limits, and practical implications.

Avoid presenting one theory as a complete account of human behaviour.

### Common Misconceptions

Corrects widely repeated inaccurate or overstated claims. Lead with accurate information rather than repeating the myth as the main hook. Explain why the misconception persists and what difference correction makes.

Do not create false myths merely to produce content or ridicule people who believed them.

### Everyday Behaviour

Explores common patterns such as procrastination, habit, avoidance, conflict, attention, cooperation, or decision-making. Consider multiple explanations and contexts. Do not diagnose listeners or reduce behaviour to one hidden motive.

### Relationships

Covers communication, trust, boundaries, repair, conflict, attachment concepts, friendship, family, and partnership. Avoid labeling absent people, prescribing separation or reconciliation, or using popular diagnostic terms as relationship categories.

Content involving abuse, coercion, or safety requires specialist review and must not suggest joint communication strategies where they could increase danger.

### Emotions

Explores emotion concepts, body experience, expression, interpretation, regulation, and culture. Emotions should not be divided into good and bad, and facial expressions should not be presented as reliable mind-reading.

### Mental Health Literacy

Explains terminology, experiences, stigma, support, professional roles, and help-seeking. It must distinguish ordinary experiences from diagnosable conditions and never function as diagnosis or treatment.

Sensitive and high-risk topics require qualified review and verified regional signposting.

### Child Development

Explains broad patterns and variation across childhood and adolescence. Avoid rigid milestones, deterministic brain narratives, and universal claims based on narrow samples.

Applications should distinguish parent, teacher, and professional roles.

### Parenting

Translates psychology into compassionate, realistic family practice. Episodes should provide connection, boundaries, co-regulation, teaching, repair, and caregiver-wellbeing implications without promising perfect outcomes.

The Parents Hub handbook governs companion resources.

### Teaching

Connects psychology and education to classroom practice. Respect teacher expertise, group conditions, inclusion, workload, curriculum, and school systems. Avoid “brain-based” claims that lack educational evidence.

### Learning

Explores memory, attention, practice, feedback, motivation, metacognition, and transfer. Distinguish laboratory findings from classroom outcomes and correct learning-style or brain myths carefully.

### Decision Making

Explores judgment, uncertainty, risk, context, habit, incentives, and cognitive processes. Do not portray biases as personal defects or imply that knowing a bias removes it.

### Communication

Examines listening, questions, language, non-verbal behaviour, disagreement, conflict, and repair. Examples should be culturally responsive and not require eye contact, immediate disclosure, or one communication style.

### Motivation

Explores goals, values, reward, autonomy, competence, belonging, environment, and persistence. Avoid claims that motivation is merely willpower, mindset, or character.

### Thinking Errors

May explain common reasoning patterns and cognitive biases. Use the term carefully; not every shortcut is irrational, and context matters. Do not encourage listeners to diagnose another person's thinking while overlooking their own.

### Resilience

Presents resilience as adaptation supported by relationships, resources, skills, opportunity, and systems. Avoid toughness, “bounce back,” and individual-responsibility narratives.

### Neuroscience

Explains relevant brain and body research without neuro-realism or decorative authority. Neural association does not establish cause, diagnosis, or an educational strategy. Avoid “reptilian brain,” simplistic hemispheres, and single-region explanations unless clearly framed as limited metaphors.

### Research Explained

Examines a study, review, method, controversy, or body of evidence. State the question, design, sample, measures, result, limitations, broader literature, and practical meaning. One study should not become a lifestyle rule.

### Book Discussions

Discusses books as arguments or interpretations, not automatically as evidence. Identify the author's purpose, strengths, limitations, source quality, context, and practical usefulness. Distinguish the book's claims from the host's analysis.

Respect copyright: summarize and quote only within appropriate limits.

### Listener Questions

Addresses general educational questions without diagnosing or advising on an identifiable case. Questions should be anonymized, consented where necessary, and edited to protect privacy.

If a question is too individual, sensitive, or urgent, explain the boundary and provide general education or an appropriate support route rather than answering the case.

### Special Series

Multi-episode series may explore a theme, life stage, research area, or audience pathway. Each series needs a defined learning arc, scope, evidence plan, accessibility plan, and completion point.

Avoid expanding a topic indefinitely for engagement. Each episode should remain useful independently while connecting clearly to the sequence.

## Episode Structure

The canonical structure is:

**Opening Hook**

↓

**Question**

↓

**Psychological Concept**

↓

**Research**

↓

**Real-Life Example**

↓

**Common Mistakes**

↓

**Practical Takeaways**

↓

**Healthy Little Minds Resources**

↓

**Closing Reflection**

The structure may adapt to interviews, stories, panels, and short episodes, but each function should be considered.

### Opening Hook

The hook should establish relevance without fear, diagnosis, or withheld truth. It may use a familiar situation, surprising but accurate contrast, careful question, or brief story.

**Avoid:** “The hidden childhood wound destroying your relationships.”

**Prefer:** “Why can a small disagreement feel much larger than the moment itself?”

The opening should not make a claim stronger than the episode can support.

### Question

State the central question and scope. Explain what the episode will and will not answer. A well-defined question protects against a wandering conversation and helps listeners organize new information.

### Psychological Concept

Begin with everyday meaning, introduce the canonical term, give an example, and identify important boundaries. Do not define one technical term with several others.

### Research

Explain the evidence in spoken language: what was studied, with whom, how, what was found, how confident we should be, and what remains uncertain. Use numbers sparingly and provide full detail in show notes.

### Real-Life Example

Show how the concept may appear in ordinary life. Examples should be plausible, culturally reviewed, non-diagnostic, and explicit when fictional or composite.

### Common Mistakes

Clarify predictable misunderstandings, harmful shortcuts, or overgeneralizations. Critique the idea, not the listener. Lead with what is accurate.

### Practical Takeaways

Offer a small number of realistic actions. Explain why they may help, how to adapt them, and when not to use them. Include professional support boundaries where relevant.

### Healthy Little Minds Resources

Connect to a carefully selected article, guide, story, worksheet, tool, or related episode. Explain its practical value. Do not turn the section into a list of products.

### Closing Reflection

Return to the central question, summarize the most important distinction, and leave one question or action worth carrying forward. Avoid grand promises and motivational speeches disconnected from the evidence.

### Why this structure supports learning

The structure begins with relevance, creates a question for new information to answer, builds a mental model, grounds it in evidence and example, corrects likely misunderstanding, supports application, and provides retrieval and follow-up. Predictability reduces cognitive load while allowing each episode its own narrative shape.

Long episodes should use spoken signposts and periodic summaries. Chapters should reflect meaningful learning sections rather than arbitrary time intervals.

## Evidence Standards

The Psychology Editorial Standard and Referencing Policy govern all Will Talks claims.

### Research translation

Translate research for listening. Introduce the question before methods, explain necessary terms, and use concrete comparisons. Show how study design affects what can be concluded.

Do not read dense statistics without interpretation. Provide exact sources, fuller numbers, and methodological detail in accessible show notes.

### Uncertainty

Spoken confidence should match evidence. Use natural qualifying language:

- “A substantial body of evidence supports…”
- “Research generally suggests…”
- “A few studies have found…, but the evidence remains limited.”
- “We do not yet know whether…”

Qualifications should be placed close to the claim, not added as a disclaimer at the end.

### Limitations

Explain limitations that change meaning: narrow samples, small studies, short follow-up, self-report, weak comparison, inconsistent measures, attrition, conflicts, or indirect evidence. Tell listeners what the limitation prevents us from concluding.

### Conflicting evidence

Represent credible disagreement fairly. Compare methods, populations, definitions, and outcomes. Avoid debate formats that reward certainty or create false balance between strong evidence and unsupported opinion.

### Scientific humility

Hosts and guests should distinguish “I think,” “In my practice,” “One interpretation is,” “The study found,” and “Guidance recommends.” Correct uncertainty is a strength.

When a previous episode is wrong or outdated, correct it visibly in the episode page, show notes, transcript, related clips, and future discussion. If the error changes likely action or safety, consider an inserted audio or video correction and direct notification where feasible.

### Guest claims

Guest expertise does not remove verification. Research material claims before recording where possible and fact-check after recording. Challenge unsupported certainty respectfully. Edit or contextualize claims that cannot be substantiated.

Guests should receive scope and evidence expectations in advance. Editorial approval remains with Healthy Little Minds.

## Storytelling

Stories make psychology memorable and human. They can also create false certainty, expose private experience, or turn suffering into entertainment. Educational purpose and consent must govern their use.

### Personal stories

Hosts and guests may share personal experience when it illuminates the topic and they can give informed consent. Clearly distinguish memory and interpretation from general evidence.

No one should feel pressured to disclose trauma, diagnosis, family conflict, or private details to make an episode compelling. Consent should be revisited during editing for sensitive excerpts and promotional use.

### Case examples

Professional case material requires authorization, ethical review, and robust de-identification. Altering names alone is insufficient. Details can combine to identify a person.

Prefer composite examples when the exact case is not essential. Label them clearly. Never imply that a case demonstrates effectiveness or provides a diagnosis for similar listeners.

### Fictional examples

Fictional examples should be plausible and designed to illuminate a decision, not guarantee an outcome. Avoid assigning stereotypes to names, cultures, disabilities, diagnoses, or family structures.

### Historical examples

Use reliable historical sources, context, and respectful language. Do not diagnose historical figures retrospectively or present unethical studies merely as fascinating stories. Explain harms and whose perspective is absent.

### Listener stories

Obtain explicit consent for use, editing, platforms, promotion, and permanence. Anonymize by default and remove unnecessary detail. Do not air an identifiable child's story based only on an adult's consent when the child's dignity or future privacy may be affected.

Listener submissions must not become a route for diagnosis or crisis response. Establish a clear process for sensitive or urgent messages.

### Ethical storytelling

Ask:

- Whose story is this?
- Who benefits from its use?
- Could someone be identified or harmed?
- Is consent informed and revocable before publication?
- Does editing change the person's meaning?
- Is pain being used as a hook?
- Does the story reinforce a stereotype?
- What evidence is still needed?

Avoid suspense that depends on delayed disclosure of harm, dramatic music under trauma, repeated replay of emotional moments, and thumbnails that display distress without meaningful consent.

## Language

The Content Style Guide and Voice and Tone handbook govern Will Talks language. The spoken voice may be more conversational and reflective while remaining accurate, calm, warm, human, respectful, curious, encouraging, practical, hopeful, emotionally validating, evidence-informed, honest, and professional.

### Conversational education

Write for the ear. Use shorter clauses, natural contractions, explicit transitions, and occasional restatement. Read scripts aloud and remove phrases that are correct on paper but difficult to follow once.

Do not imitate casual speech so heavily that meaning becomes vague. Avoid filler, forced slang, performative intimacy, and constant rhetorical questions.

### Explain terms naturally

Introduce the everyday experience before or alongside the technical term.

**Prefer:** “The mental processes that help us pause, hold information in mind, and shift between tasks are often called executive functions.”

Reuse the term consistently after defining it. Show notes may include a glossary.

### Spoken structure

Signpost the journey:

- “First, let us define what we mean.”
- “Here is where the evidence becomes less certain.”
- “Consider a classroom example.”
- “There are two cautions before we turn to practice.”

Signposting should guide, not announce every paragraph.

### Host authority

The host should not sound like an ultimate authority on a listener's life. Use direct language for evidence and boundaries, and humility about individual application.

Avoid “I know exactly how you feel,” “Here is what you need to do,” and “This explains why you are the way you are.”

## Emotional Responsibility

Episodes about trauma, abuse, grief, suicide, self-harm, violence, addiction, and other high-risk topics require enhanced editorial safeguards.

### Editorial decision

Before commissioning, define the public benefit, audience, risk, necessary expertise, support information, and whether audio or video is the right format. Do not cover a topic merely because it is timely or likely to attract attention.

### Qualified review

Use appropriately qualified psychology, safeguarding, clinical, legal, or lived-experience review according to topic and jurisdiction. Lived experience is valuable but must not carry responsibility for general clinical guidance.

### Content notes

Provide specific, concise content notes before relevant material. A note should help informed choice without sensationalizing or listing unnecessary detail. Include chapters or timestamps so listeners can skip sections.

### Trauma and abuse

Do not ask guests for graphic detail. Avoid implying that one behaviour proves trauma, encouraging untrained investigation, or recommending confrontation that could increase danger. Distinguish trauma-informed support from treatment.

### Grief

Allow variation in grief without prescribing stages or timelines. Avoid “moving on,” silver linings, and statements that loss makes people stronger. Include cultural and spiritual diversity without assuming belief.

### Suicide and self-harm

Follow current responsible-communication guidance and qualified review. Avoid method details, romanticization, simplistic causes, deterministic links, sensational headlines, and prominent imagery that could increase harm. Use direct, non-stigmatizing language and provide verified region-appropriate support.

Will Talks does not manage crisis through comments, messages, or general episodes. Establish moderation and escalation procedures for concerning communications.

### Violence

Do not equate mental illness with violence or use violent events to speculate about diagnosis. Avoid graphic replay and perpetrator-centered spectacle. Prioritize accurate context, prevention, affected people, and support.

### Addiction

Use non-stigmatizing language and explain biological, psychological, social, and environmental factors. Avoid moral failure narratives, recovery guarantees, or one-route treatment claims.

### Emotional production choices

Music, pacing, edits, thumbnails, titles, and promotional clips must not intensify distress for entertainment. Silence and emotion should not be manipulated to manufacture a dramatic reveal.

### Signposting

Support information should be spoken when necessary and included in descriptions, show notes, transcripts, pinned comments, and localized pages. Verify services and regions regularly. State when immediate danger requires local emergency assistance.

## Visual Identity

The Design System, Color System, Typography, Illustration Guidelines, Iconography, Motion Guidelines, and Accessibility Standard govern Will Talks visuals.

### Thumbnails

Thumbnails should identify the topic and episode consistently. Use readable text, high contrast, dignified imagery, and restrained composition. Avoid shocked faces, fear-based wording, diagnostic labels aimed at viewers, red warning symbols, fabricated conflict, and before-and-after transformation.

The thumbnail and title must represent the episode together without misleading omission.

### Graphics

Graphics should clarify definitions, sequences, comparisons, evidence, or practical steps. Cite data and label uncertainty. Do not use decorative brain scans or scientific imagery to create authority.

### Illustrations

Illustrations should support explanation and emotional safety. Represent varied people and contexts without stereotypes. Facial expression should not be presented as a definitive emotion label.

### Captions

On-screen text should complement rather than compete with speech. Captions must remain readable, synchronized, and complete. Do not cover faces, demonstrations, or important graphics.

### Motion

Motion should support orientation and explanation. Avoid constant zooms, rapid cuts, flashing, or kinetic text used only to retain attention. Respect reduced-motion needs and provide enough time to read.

### Episode artwork

Artwork should use a stable template system with room for topic distinction. Series, language, episode number, and format should be identifiable without creating a new visual identity for every release.

### Consistency

Consistent framing, typography, color roles, intros, chapter cards, lower thirds, and end screens reduce cognitive load and build recognition. Consistency should not become visual monotony or prevent accessibility improvement.

## Shorts

YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels, TikTok, Facebook Reels, and audiograms are educational products, not trailers exempt from context.

### Purpose

Each short should have one clear learning objective. Appropriate purposes include defining a term, correcting one misconception, modelling one phrase, explaining one research limitation, or inviting a reflective question.

### Structure

A useful short structure is:

1. state the accurate idea;
2. explain it in one context;
3. name one important limit;
4. offer a practical implication;
5. link to fuller context.

### Avoid oversimplification

Do not remove “may,” population, timeframe, or diagnostic boundary to fit the duration. If the idea cannot be represented responsibly, choose a narrower idea or do not publish it as short-form content.

### Titles and opening lines

Avoid “three signs you have,” “the trauma response nobody discusses,” “psychologists do not want you to know,” and “do this to make your child obey.” Use descriptive language that does not diagnose or create fear.

### Platform adaptation

Adapt dimensions, pacing, caption placement, and description length while preserving the same meaning. Trends, sounds, humour, and visual conventions may be used only when they fit naturally and do not trivialize the subject.

### Clips from long episodes

Review clips as independent claims. Add context in the clip when the original qualification is outside the excerpt. Do not select the most extreme statement merely because it performs well.

### Accessibility

Provide accurate captions, readable on-screen text, audio clarity, descriptive context, and no reliance on sound alone. Avoid rapid text changes and visual overload.

### Comments

Moderation should correct dangerous misinformation, protect privacy, remove abuse, and avoid personalized diagnosis. Do not invite followers to disclose sensitive symptoms publicly.

## Website Integration

Every episode page should function as a learning hub rather than a media embed.

It should include, where relevant:

- accurate title and summary;
- audience and topic metadata;
- embedded audio or video with accessible controls;
- complete transcript;
- chapters or timestamps;
- key ideas and practical takeaways;
- evidence note and references;
- content note and support information;
- guest biography and relevant conflicts;
- related Healthy Little Minds resources;
- publication and review dates;
- correction history.

### Teachers Hub

Link when an episode has classroom implications. Companion articles should translate the discussion into objective, context, steps, adaptations, cautions, and reflection.

### Parents Hub

Link when an episode concerns family relationships, development, behaviour, emotions, or caregiver wellbeing. Parent guides should provide realistic actions and clear professional boundaries.

### Emotion Library

Link to canonical emotion definitions and related guidance when the episode discusses emotional experience. Avoid implying that one emotion label explains the whole situation.

### Stories and audiobooks

Connect to stories when narrative can support child or family exploration without forced personal disclosure. State age guidance and whether shared reading is recommended.

### Worksheets

Use worksheets for reflection or practice when they add value. They must not become diagnostic tests or compulsory disclosure.

### Interactive tools

Link when a tool supports a defined task such as choosing conversation language, planning a routine, or exploring emotion vocabulary. Explain privacy and limitations.

### Related episodes

Choose a small set that answers the likely next question, provides a foundation, offers a contrasting perspective, or deepens application. Do not use endless autoplay as the main discovery strategy.

Cross-linking should feel educational rather than promotional. Describe why a resource is useful. Maintain localized links and clearly label unavailable translations.

## Accessibility

The Accessibility Standard applies to recording, editing, platforms, websites, social derivatives, and downloads.

### Captions

Every video requires accurate, synchronized captions reviewed by a human. Captions should identify speakers and meaningful sound, preserve psychology terminology, and use readable segmentation. Automatic captions are drafts, not publication-ready access.

### Transcripts

Every episode requires a complete, accessible transcript. Structure it with title, speakers, headings, chapters, paragraph breaks, links, and content notes. Edit speech disfluencies only when meaning and speaker character remain intact.

Corrections to spoken claims should be reflected visibly in transcripts, not silently rewritten as though the error never occurred.

### Audio quality

Speech should be intelligible without excessive volume, noise, echo, or music. Balance speakers consistently. Music and effects must not obscure words. Provide a listening experience suitable for hearing differences and varied devices.

### Plain language

Define terms, signpost structure, summarize complex sections, and avoid excessive information density. Audio cannot be scanned like text; restate essential relationships.

### Visual accessibility

Use sufficient contrast, legible type, color-independent meaning, stable framing, accessible charts, and meaningful visual description. Verbally explain information shown only on screen.

### Multilingual subtitles

Translated subtitles require proficient human review, appropriate timing, terminology consistency, and cultural context. Do not publish raw machine subtitles. Reading speed and line length should suit the target language.

### Interaction and platforms

Episode pages, players, chapter navigation, forms, and downloads need keyboard, screen-reader, touch, zoom, and reflow support. Do not autoplay media. Provide platform-independent access where feasible.

### Cognitive and emotional accessibility

Use predictable episode structure, specific content notes, chapter skipping, clear summaries, and manageable calls to action. Avoid rapid editing and constant visual stimulation.

## Localization

The Localization Guidelines govern all international editions. Localization includes spoken language, subtitles, transcripts, titles, examples, graphics, descriptions, support information, and related links.

### Subtitles

Translate meaning and spoken register, not word order. Preserve evidence qualifications, humour only when safe, and speaker distinctions. Adjust timing for reading speed without removing meaning.

### Translated transcripts

Transcripts should read naturally as documents. Localize headings, terminology, references, dates, examples, and support routes. Maintain links to the source version and record material adaptations.

### Culturally appropriate examples

Review family roles, school systems, communication, discipline, relationships, services, names, routines, and metaphors. An example may need replacement while the psychological principle remains stable.

### International evidence

State when evidence comes mainly from one country, language, or population. Seek local research and professional guidance where it changes interpretation or application. Do not imply that one context is universal.

### Dubbing and localized editions

Future dubbing or re-recording requires natural performance, pronunciation, voice direction, and complete editorial review. A localized host may adapt transitions and examples while preserving approved claims and structure.

### International guests

Prepare pronunciation, context, translation, interpretation, and informed-consent processes. Do not ask a guest to represent an entire country or culture. Interpretation should preserve uncertainty and technical meaning.

Every language edition needs sustainable ownership, terminology, review, QA, correction, and update pathways. Launching subtitles without capacity to maintain them weakens trust.

## AI Contribution

AI may support production but is not a researcher, fact-checker, translator of record, editor of record, or authority.

AI may assist with:

- organizing verified research notes;
- drafting episode-outline alternatives;
- suggesting accurate title candidates for review;
- identifying possible chapter markers;
- drafting show-note structure;
- preparing transcript drafts from recordings;
- suggesting social-media copy from approved content;
- drafting translations for human review;
- comparing derivative content with an approved script.

AI may never:

- fabricate evidence, studies, authors, quotations, statistics, or DOIs;
- misquote or merge research findings;
- invent a guest's statement or alter meaning;
- diagnose a listener, host, guest, or person in a story;
- generate an imitation of a real person's voice without explicit lawful consent;
- publish transcripts or translations without human review;
- replace psychology, editorial, accessibility, localization, or legal review;
- approve its own output.

Every source must be opened and verified by a human. Transcript drafts require word-for-word review, especially names, technical terms, numbers, and sensitive statements. AI-generated show notes may omit qualifications or attribute a claim to the wrong speaker.

Do not enter confidential listener submissions, unpublished guest information, personal data, or protected full-text materials into an unapproved system. Record material AI involvement according to governance.

Human review is mandatory at every publication stage. Accountability remains with the named producer, editor, and owner.

### Research

Define the audience, central question, public benefit, scope, risk, evidence needed, possible guests, and ecosystem role. Create an evidence record under the Referencing Policy. Seek current synthesis, original sources where needed, conflicting findings, cultural limits, and practical implications.

For sensitive topics, determine specialist review, support pathways, content notes, moderation needs, and whether the episode should proceed at all.

### Outline

Map the learning path using the canonical structure. Identify the central claim, definitions, evidence, examples, common mistakes, practical takeaways, limits, and cross-links. Mark claims requiring careful language or on-air correction if a guest overstates them.

Interview outlines should include open questions, follow-ups about evidence and limits, and boundaries around private or unsafe disclosure. Guests should receive topic, audience, format, editorial expectations, recording use, and consent information.

### Evidence Review

A suitably qualified reviewer checks source selection, claim strength, uncertainty, developmental and cultural fit, diagnostic boundaries, practical recommendations, and foreseeable harm. Resolve evidence concerns before scripting or recording wherever possible.

### Script

Scripts may range from full narration to structured interview notes. Write for the ear. Include pronunciation, pauses, transitions, visual descriptions, citations for production, content notes, support wording, and alternative lines for complex explanations.

Read the script aloud. Check timing, cognitive density, repetition, and whether each section advances the central question. Fact-check titles and descriptions at the same stage.

### Recording

Create a calm, respectful environment. Confirm guest consent, pronunciation, preferred identity language, boundaries, and the ability to pause or revisit sensitive material. Do not pressure performance or disclosure.

Monitor audio clarity and factual risk. The host should ask for clarification when a term, statistic, causal claim, or recommendation may mislead.

### Editing

Edit for clarity, pace, accuracy, dignity, and learning—not manufactured drama. Preserve a guest's intended meaning. Do not splice statements into stronger claims or hide uncertainty.

Fact-check the recorded episode. Add contextual narration, re-record, remove, or qualify claims that do not meet the standard. Review music, effects, visuals, titles, thumbnails, and clips for emotional responsibility.

### Accessibility

Complete human-reviewed captions, transcript, audio-quality review, visual descriptions, chapter markers, accessible graphics, content notes, and support information. Test the website and player experience.

### Localization

Prepare a source package containing final media, transcript, glossary, references, speaker names, pronunciation, visual text, cultural notes, and locked claims. Complete linguistic, cultural, psychology, accessibility, and functional review for each edition.

### Publication

Confirm approvals, consent, evidence notes, metadata, descriptions, thumbnails, chapters, transcript, captions, support links, conflict disclosures, sponsorship disclosures, related resources, owner, version, and review date.

Publication timing should not override unresolved safety, evidence, consent, accessibility, or localization issues.

### Cross-linking

Connect the episode to a small set of relevant Healthy Little Minds resources and update those resources to point back where useful. Verify links and localized destinations.

### Periodic Review

Review episodes according to risk, evidence stability, reach, and dependence on current guidance. Trigger early review after retractions, guideline changes, credible complaints, expired support information, or identified harm.

Update show notes, transcripts, clips, articles, and related resources together. Material spoken errors may require a correction notice, inserted correction, unlisting, replacement, or withdrawal.

### Guest and rights records

Maintain consent, release, usage, promotional, localization, archive, and withdrawal-request records according to law and policy. Consent to an episode does not automatically include every future course, advertisement, synthetic voice, or derivative use.

### Sponsorship and independence

Any sponsorship or partnership must be disclosed clearly and must not influence conclusions, guest questioning, evidence selection, or resource recommendation. Will Talks should not accept relationships that conflict with child wellbeing, evidence integrity, or public trust.

Advertising should be distinct from education. Products must not be promoted through inflated psychological problems or implied clinical endorsement.

## Success Metrics

Success is measured by understanding, trust, application, retention, and community value—not views alone.

### Understanding

Can listeners explain the central concept accurately, distinguish it from a common misconception, and identify its limits? Use surveys, interviews, comprehension prompts, and user testing rather than completion alone.

### Trust

Do listeners experience the platform as accurate, transparent, respectful, and willing to correct itself? Monitor qualitative feedback, correction response, source use, accessibility reports, and whether trust is consistent across communities.

Trust should not be measured only by agreement. Responsible content may challenge familiar beliefs.

### Application

Can listeners identify and use a proportionate next step? Teacher and parent feedback may show whether companion resources work in real contexts. Application should not be inferred from clicks or downloads alone.

### Retention

Do people remember the important idea and its qualification after time? Repeated listening can indicate value, but learning-focused follow-up is more informative than watch time.

### Community value

Does Will Talks improve public conversation, support local educators and families, reduce stigma, increase appropriate help-seeking, and connect people to accessible resources? Evaluate who benefits and who remains excluded.

### Responsible platform metrics

Views, reach, completion, subscribers, click-through, and shares can inform distribution and production decisions. They must never determine truth, topic worth, emotional intensity, or guest value.

Do not optimize for outrage, compulsive use, autoplay, or endless production. A low-view episode serving an important underserved audience may be successful. A viral episode that creates misunderstanding is not.

### Evaluation ethics

Collect only necessary data, explain its use, protect privacy, and avoid inferring mental health status. Metrics should support learning and stewardship rather than profiling vulnerable listeners.

## Quality Checklist

### Purpose and audience

- [ ] The episode answers one clear public psychology question.
- [ ] Primary and secondary audiences are defined.
- [ ] The educational benefit justifies the topic and format.
- [ ] The episode remains within Will Talks' educational scope.
- [ ] It adds value rather than repeating existing content.
- [ ] The listener can identify what they will understand or do.

### Editorial philosophy

- [ ] Curiosity precedes unsupported certainty.
- [ ] Evidence, opinion, experience, and interpretation are distinguished.
- [ ] Education takes priority over engagement.
- [ ] Behaviour is understood before people are judged.
- [ ] Criticism protects dignity and avoids mockery.
- [ ] Practical learning goes beyond inspiration.

### Evidence

- [ ] Every material claim has an appropriate verified source.
- [ ] Current systematic reviews or guidance were considered.
- [ ] Sources match population, context, outcome, and timeframe.
- [ ] Association is not presented as causation.
- [ ] Group findings are not turned into individual predictions.
- [ ] Statistics include understandable context.
- [ ] Limitations and conflicting evidence are represented fairly.
- [ ] Guest claims have been fact-checked.
- [ ] References and links comply with the Referencing Policy.
- [ ] Evidence review is recorded.

### Psychology and scope

- [ ] Concepts are defined accurately and within scope.
- [ ] The episode does not diagnose, treat, prescribe, or manage crisis.
- [ ] Ordinary experiences are not pathologized.
- [ ] Diagnostic and professional boundaries are clear.
- [ ] Developmental, individual, and cultural variation remain visible.
- [ ] Neuroscience is not used as decorative authority.
- [ ] Practical takeaways are proportionate to evidence.
- [ ] Qualified specialist review is complete where required.

### Episode structure and learning

- [ ] The hook is accurate and non-manipulative.
- [ ] The central question and scope are stated early.
- [ ] The concept is introduced through everyday meaning.
- [ ] Research is understandable in spoken form.
- [ ] Examples are realistic and non-diagnostic.
- [ ] Common mistakes are corrected without shame.
- [ ] Takeaways are specific, adaptable, and bounded.
- [ ] The closing supports recall and reflection.
- [ ] Chapters and signposts reduce cognitive load.

### Storytelling and ethics

- [ ] Every story has a clear educational purpose.
- [ ] Personal experience is not presented as general evidence.
- [ ] Fictional and composite examples are labelled where needed.
- [ ] Consent covers recording, editing, promotion, and platforms.
- [ ] Identifying details and child privacy are protected.
- [ ] No one was pressured to disclose sensitive information.
- [ ] Pain is not used as spectacle or a hook.
- [ ] Historical material is sourced and contextualized.

### Language and voice

- [ ] The episode sounds calm, warm, human, respectful, and curious.
- [ ] Spoken sentences are natural and understandable once.
- [ ] Technical terms are introduced and reused consistently.
- [ ] Uncertainty sounds clear rather than evasive.
- [ ] The host does not claim authority over a listener's life.
- [ ] Language avoids fear, shame, guilt, hype, and false intimacy.
- [ ] The script has been read aloud and revised.

### Emotional responsibility

- [ ] Sensitive-topic coverage has a documented public benefit.
- [ ] Content notes are specific and proportionate.
- [ ] Listeners can skip or leave sensitive sections.
- [ ] Support information is current, regional, and accessible.
- [ ] Suicide, self-harm, abuse, violence, addiction, trauma, and grief follow specialist guidance.
- [ ] Titles, thumbnails, music, and clips do not sensationalize harm.

## Future Vision

Will Talks may grow through live events, courses, guest experts, teacher editions, parent editions, community discussions, certification, and broader public psychology education. Each expansion should deepen the mission rather than turn attention into the product.

### Live events

Live events could support dialogue, demonstration, questions, and community learning. They require accessible venues and streaming, captions or interpretation, moderation, audience privacy, question triage, support pathways, recording consent, and a plan for correcting live misstatements.

Live formats must not pressure attendees to disclose personal experiences or allow individualized diagnosis from the stage.

### Courses

Courses could provide sequenced explanation, reflection, practice, and feedback. They need explicit learning outcomes, evidence maps, accessibility, localization, assessment appropriate to scope, and maintenance.

Courses should not promise transformation or imply therapeutic outcomes. Free and low-cost pathways should be considered so educational value is not limited to those able to pay.

### Guest experts

The guest network may expand across psychology, education, health, neuroscience, social sciences, lived experience, and community knowledge. Selection should prioritize relevant expertise, communication, integrity, diversity, and fit—not celebrity.

Guests should disclose relevant conflicts and receive clear editorial expectations. No guest is beyond fact-checking.

### Teacher editions

Teacher editions could shorten or reorganize episodes around classroom application, professional learning, and team discussion. They should connect directly to Teachers Hub guides and preserve school-system, safeguarding, and professional boundaries.

### Parent editions

Parent editions could focus on everyday family situations, conversation examples, caregiver wellbeing, and development. They should connect to Parents Hub resources and avoid guilt or perfect-parenting narratives.

### Community discussions

Moderated discussions could help listeners ask questions, examine application, and share experience. They require clear scope, safeguarding, misinformation response, privacy, accessibility, and community conduct.

Personal anecdotes should not become universal guidance, and moderators should not provide clinical advice.

### Certification

Certification is appropriate only if future programmes define meaningful competencies, credible assessment, identity and accessibility safeguards, continuing requirements, and recognized scope. Completion of public podcast content alone must never imply professional qualification.

### Public psychology education

Will Talks could support schools, libraries, universities, community organizations, broadcasters, and international partners. Partnerships must preserve editorial independence, evidence standards, accessibility, localization, and correction rights.

### Responsible technology

Future tools may support personalized learning paths, searchable transcripts, translated audio, interactive evidence notes, and approved-resource assistants. They must protect privacy, avoid diagnostic profiling, disclose automation, preserve human review, and remain usable without coercive engagement.

### Stewardship questions

Every future proposal should answer:

- Does this improve psychological literacy?
- Does it preserve evidence and uncertainty?
- Does it help people act responsibly?
- Does it protect dignity, privacy, consent, and emotional safety?
- Is it accessible and internationally adaptable?
- Can it be reviewed, corrected, and maintained for ten years?
- Does it strengthen the Healthy Little Minds ecosystem?

The platform should remain willing to produce fewer, better episodes. Scale is valuable only when the standard scales with it.

## Closing Reflection

Psychology should not remain trapped inside journals, classrooms, or clinics.

Will Talks exists to bring thoughtful, evidence-informed psychology into everyday conversations so people can better understand themselves, support others, and make wiser decisions.

Every episode should leave listeners not only knowing more—but living a little differently because of what they learned.
