# Healthy Little Minds Voice & Tone

## Purpose

This handbook defines how Healthy Little Minds communicates through every form of writing. It applies to educational articles, classroom resources, interactive stories, podcast scripts, worksheets, audiobooks, interfaces, emails, social media, notifications, onboarding, customer support, and AI-generated drafts.

Voice is the enduring personality of Healthy Little Minds. It expresses the project's character and values regardless of format. Tone is how that voice adapts to a particular audience, situation, and emotional context. A parent reading about grief requires a different tone from a teacher scanning lesson instructions, but both should recognize the same careful, practical, respectful source.

The voice remains consistent. The tone adapts with empathy.

Writing is part of the product experience. It can reduce confusion, protect dignity, make evidence usable, and help someone take a constructive next step. It can also increase fear, shame, or cognitive load when handled carelessly. Every contributor who creates or approves words is responsible for their likely effect, not only their grammatical correctness.

This document governs communication choices. The Writing Style handbook should provide more detailed editorial conventions, and the Brand Guidelines define the wider emotional and visual identity. When a stylistic preference conflicts with accuracy, safety, accessibility, or respect, those responsibilities take precedence.

## Voice Principles

The Healthy Little Minds voice should carry all of the following qualities. No single quality is sufficient by itself; their balance gives the voice its identity.

### Calm

Use steady language and a clear sequence. Name difficult realities without amplifying them. Avoid urgency unless an immediate safety action is genuinely required. Calm writing helps a reader orient themselves and preserve enough attention to understand the next step.

Calm does not mean detached or vague. “This may feel frightening” can acknowledge emotion directly. “You must act before it is too late” creates alarm unless a specific, immediate risk makes that language accurate and necessary.

### Warm

Write as one person helping another. Recognize effort, uncertainty, and context. Use natural language rather than institutional phrases. Warmth appears through attention and care, not excessive enthusiasm, pet names, or constant reassurance.

Warm writing can say, “You do not need to solve everything in one conversation.” It should not promise, “Everything will be fine,” when the writer cannot know that.

### Respectful

Treat children and adults as capable people with dignity. Describe behaviour without turning it into identity. Offer guidance without assuming carelessness, ignorance, or bad intent. Respect includes clear boundaries: direct language can be more respectful than a softened message that conceals an important risk.

### Evidence-informed

Communicate psychological and educational knowledge in proportion to its support. Distinguish established understanding, emerging research, general suggestions, and individual professional advice. Do not use scientific language to create authority or add citations that do not support the actual claim.

### Encouraging

Help readers see a manageable way forward. Emphasize that emotional skills develop through practice, support, and repair. Encouragement should increase agency, not impose optimism. Avoid promises that a strategy will work quickly or for everyone.

### Practical

Connect ideas to situations, words, examples, routines, decisions, and next steps. Tell the reader what they can reasonably try and what to notice. State when a need exceeds the platform's educational role. Practical writing respects real constraints rather than describing ideal behaviour alone.

### Curious

Use questions and possibilities to open understanding before declaring a conclusion. Distinguish what was observed from what is inferred. Curiosity supports reflection without avoiding action: where safety is concerned, clear procedures still apply.

### Honest

State limitations, uncertainty, tradeoffs, and errors. Do not hide important conditions in fine print. Say when the platform cannot answer an individual question or when professional help may be needed. Trust grows when language remains accurate even when certainty would sound more reassuring.

### Inclusive

Write for varied cultures, disabilities, family structures, identities, literacy levels, and ways of experiencing emotion. Avoid treating one experience as universal. Inclusive writing does not require listing every possible identity in every sentence; it requires choices that do not unnecessarily exclude, stereotype, or presume.

## What Healthy Little Minds Never Sounds Like

The voice should never become any of the following:

- **Judgmental:** Labels such as “bad parent,” “difficult child,” or “attention-seeking” reduce people to an interpretation. Judgment creates defensiveness and shame where observation and guidance are needed.
- **Alarmist:** Inflated warnings and worst-case framing exploit concern. Risks should be communicated clearly, with likelihood, context, and appropriate action where known.
- **Sensational:** Emotional wellbeing is not material for shocking headlines, dramatic reveals, or claims that one fact “changes everything.” Sensationalism weakens accuracy and trust.
- **Sarcastic:** Sarcasm can humiliate, obscure meaning, and translate poorly. It is especially inappropriate when discussing children, parenting, teaching, disability, or distress.
- **Preachy:** The platform should explain reasoning and offer usable guidance rather than lecture from moral superiority. Imperatives should be reserved for clear instructions and safety needs.
- **Patronizing:** Children do not need baby talk, and adults do not need praise for understanding a basic point. Use accessible language without lowering respect.
- **Manipulative:** Do not use guilt, fear, artificial urgency, hidden consequences, or emotional pressure to obtain a click, subscription, share, purchase, or continued session.
- **Corporate:** Avoid impersonal jargon such as “leveraging solutions,” “driving outcomes,” and “stakeholder enablement.” Name the person, need, action, and result plainly.
- **Overly academic:** Necessary evidence should be understandable. Dense terminology, passive construction, and citation displays should not make readers work harder merely to prove that the writer is informed.
- **Emotionally exaggerated:** Not every setback is devastating, every insight transformative, or every activity joyful. Use emotional language that matches the situation.

Humour may be used carefully in light, appropriate contexts, but never at the expense of a child's feelings, a caregiver's concern, a teacher's pressure, or a person's identity. When in doubt, prefer humane clarity over wit.

## Tone Changes With Context

Tone should respond to what the audience likely knows, needs, feels, and can do. Adapt vocabulary, detail, pace, and directness while preserving the voice principles.

### Parents

Use a supportive, reassuring, and practical tone. Parents may arrive worried, tired, or afraid they have made a mistake. Acknowledge concern without confirming an unsupported fear. Give realistic phrases and steps, explain what may vary, and identify when further help is appropriate.

Never induce guilt. Avoid “You should have,” “Good parents always,” and language suggesting that one response determines a child's future. Support growth and repair rather than perfection. Recognize caregivers' knowledge of their children and differences in family circumstances.

Prefer: “If the first conversation does not go as planned, you can pause and return to it later.”

### Teachers

Use a respectful, collaborative, realistic, and time-conscious tone. Put objectives, timing, materials, and essential instructions where teachers can scan them. Acknowledge classroom variation and provide adaptations without turning a short activity into an unmanageable list.

Never blame teachers for systemic problems or imply that enthusiasm can overcome every shortage, policy, workload, safety issue, or unmet specialist need. Do not ask educators to act as therapists. Clarify professional and safeguarding boundaries.

Prefer: “Use the two-minute version when time is limited; return to the reflection during a quieter transition.”

### Children

Use simple, encouraging, emotionally validating language. Introduce one main idea at a time, use concrete examples, and explain what the child can do. Validate feelings without validating harmful actions or assuming why a child feels a certain way.

Never be babyish or dismissive. Avoid forced cheer, rhyming instructions for serious situations, and “It is nothing to worry about.” Do not promise secrecy or place responsibility for adult problems on a child. Provide a clear route to a trusted adult when needed.

Prefer: “Anger can feel very strong. You can pause before choosing what to do next.”

### Adolescents

Use a respectful, honest, and curious tone. Adolescents can recognize performance, evasion, and borrowed slang. Explain the reason behind guidance, allow complexity, and offer choices where appropriate. Address them directly without pretending to be a peer.

Never lecture, moralize, imitate trends, or assume rebellion. Do not trivialize their relationships or concerns as temporary simply because they are young.

Prefer: “You may not be ready to talk about everything. You can start by telling someone what kind of support would help.”

### Professionals

Use an accurate, evidence-informed, collaborative tone. Define scope, intended audience, evidence basis, and limitations. Make sources and review context available without filling every sentence with qualifications.

Do not imply clinical validation where none exists or use professional readers to confer borrowed authority. Invite correction and distinguish educational materials from assessment or treatment.

Prefer: “This resource supports general psychoeducation and has not been evaluated as a clinical intervention.”

### Crisis or Sensitive Topics

Use a calm, direct, compassionate, and clear tone. Put immediate safety information before background explanation. Use plain verbs, short steps, region-appropriate support details, and clear boundaries about what the platform can provide.

Never be dramatic, euphemistic, or minimizing. Avoid graphic detail unless essential for safety. Do not ask a person in crisis to read a long introduction, complete an engagement flow, or rely on an automated conversation. Do not promise confidentiality the platform cannot provide.

Prefer: “If someone may be in immediate danger, contact local emergency services now. Stay with them if it is safe for you to do so.”

## Writing Principles

### Use plain English

Choose familiar, precise words. Prefer “use” to “utilize” and “help” to “facilitate” when the meaning is the same. Plain English is not simplistic English; keep distinctions and conditions that affect safe understanding.

### Use short sentences when clarity matters

Sentence length should vary naturally, but instructions, safety information, errors, and child-facing text often benefit from shorter construction. Break apart sentences containing several actions or qualifications. Do not make all writing abrupt in the name of brevity.

### Explain psychological terms

Use a specialist term when it gives readers a useful name for an idea, then define it at first use. Explain through an example where possible. Do not replace established terms with inaccurate metaphors or use diagnostic language casually.

### Prefer examples over abstractions

Show what a principle sounds or looks like in a real situation. “Connect before correcting” becomes useful when paired with words an adult might say and conditions where immediate safety action comes first. Examples should represent varied contexts and should not be presented as scripts that guarantee results.

### Use active voice naturally

Active voice often identifies responsibility and makes action clear: “The teacher introduces the activity.” Passive voice is appropriate when the actor is unknown, unimportant, or intentionally protected. Do not force active voice where it becomes accusatory or awkward.

### Avoid unnecessary jargon

Remove technical, educational, corporate, and product language that the audience does not need. Terms familiar to the team may still be barriers to users. When a technical term is necessary, explain it rather than placing the burden on the reader.

### Respect uncertainty

Match certainty to evidence. Use “may,” “can,” “is associated with,” or “some children” when outcomes vary. Do not weaken every established statement with unnecessary caution, but never turn a general tendency into a universal rule.

### Give useful next steps

Conclude explanations with an action suited to the audience: notice a pattern, try a phrase, practise a routine, adapt an activity, read a related guide, or seek qualified help. A next step should serve the user rather than keep them on the platform.

## Emotional Language

Emotions should be described as meaningful human experiences, not moral categories. Avoid calling feelings good, bad, positive, or negative when “comfortable,” “uncomfortable,” “pleasant,” or “difficult” is more accurate. Feelings can be accepted while behaviour remains subject to boundaries.

### Anxiety

Distinguish ordinary worry, persistent anxiety, and diagnosed anxiety disorders. Do not call every concern “anxiety” or present a checklist as diagnosis. Avoid “just calm down.” Explain that anxiety can affect thoughts, bodies, and actions, and that support may be needed when it is intense, persistent, or disrupts daily life.

### Sadness

Treat sadness as a valid response, not something to remove immediately. Do not equate sadness automatically with depression. Avoid pressuring people to find a positive lesson. Offer presence, expression, routine, connection, and appropriate help according to context.

### Anger

Separate the emotion from harmful behaviour. Anger can signal unfairness, threat, frustration, pain, or unmet needs; it does not excuse hurting someone. Avoid describing an angry child as aggressive or dangerous without behavioural evidence. Focus on safety, regulation, communication, repair, and context.

### Grief

Do not prescribe a fixed sequence, timeline, or correct expression. Grief varies across people, relationships, cultures, and time. Use direct words appropriate to the audience rather than confusing euphemisms. Avoid “move on” and promises that pain will disappear.

### Trauma

Use “trauma” with care. Do not apply it to every difficult event, diagnose trauma responses, or invite unnecessary disclosure. Emphasize safety, choice, predictable support, and professional guidance. Avoid graphic details and claims that a simple activity can heal trauma.

### Behavioural challenges

Describe observable actions and context rather than labelling a child as naughty, manipulative, disruptive, or attention-seeking. Ask what skill, need, stressor, environment, or relationship may be relevant. Avoid assuming that all behaviour communicates the same thing. State safety limits clearly when behaviour may cause harm.

Across all emotional topics, do not define a person by a temporary state. Use language that leaves room for variation, agency, support, and change.

## Inclusive Language

Inclusive language recognizes human variation without treating people as categories to display. Ask how the relevant community describes itself, respect individual preferences, and update terminology as usage changes. Context matters more than a universal list of approved words.

### Disability

Both person-first language (“a child with autism”) and identity-first language (“an autistic child”) are used. Follow individual or community preference where known. Avoid “suffers from,” “confined to,” “normal children,” and inspiration narratives. Name barriers in environments as well as individual access needs.

### Culture

Do not treat one family practice, emotional expression, classroom model, or help-seeking route as universal. Avoid using culture as a simple explanation for behaviour. Be specific when context matters, use informed review, and do not exoticize names, clothing, food, or traditions.

### Family structures

Use “parent or caregiver” where the responsible adult may vary. Examples should include different family structures without presenting one as the default and others as exceptions. Do not assume that home is safe; phrase guidance so children can identify another trusted adult where necessary.

### Gender

Use a person's stated name and pronouns. Avoid unnecessary gendering of roles, toys, emotions, subjects, or behaviours. Use singular “they” when a person's gender is unknown or irrelevant. Do not make a child's conformity to gender expectations a measure of wellbeing.

### Language learners

Write clearly without treating language proficiency as intelligence. Avoid idioms, puns, cultural references, and phrasal complexity when they do not add meaning. Provide visual or audio support where useful, and allow localized versions to adapt examples rather than copy English structure.

### Neurodiversity

Describe differences and support needs without presenting one way of thinking, communicating, regulating, or sensing as the only correct one. Avoid using diagnostic labels as casual adjectives. Do not promise normalization or imply that visible compliance is the primary goal.

Person-centered language also requires relevance. Do not mention identity characteristics when they do not matter to the subject. When they do matter, use language that preserves dignity and avoids making a person representative of an entire group.

## User Interface Writing

Interface writing should help users understand where they are, what they can do, what happened, and what comes next. It should sound human without becoming chatty or vague.

### Buttons

Use specific action labels: “Download worksheet,” “Play story,” or “Save changes.” Avoid “Click here,” “Submit,” or “Yes” when the action can be named. Keep labels concise and consistent. Destructive actions should state the consequence.

### Navigation

Use familiar, stable terms that describe destinations. Avoid branded names when a plain audience label is clearer. Navigation text should not become promotional. Indicate current location and preserve a clear route back.

### Forms

Use visible labels, concise instructions, appropriate examples, and clear statements about why sensitive information is requested. Do not use placeholders as the only label. Mark optional fields explicitly and collect only what is necessary.

### Validation

Place validation near the relevant field and explain how to correct it. Say “Enter an email address in the format name@example.com,” not “Invalid input.” Do not blame the user or announce errors through colour alone.

### Empty states

Explain why nothing appears and offer a relevant action. Avoid jokes that make a user feel responsible. “No saved worksheets yet. Browse worksheets to find one for later” is clearer than “It is lonely here!”

### Loading states

State what is loading when a wait is noticeable. Do not invent precision. Provide a useful fallback or retry when loading fails, and keep essential content available without unnecessary blocking where possible.

### Confirmation messages

Confirm the completed action and any important consequence: “Your changes are saved.” Avoid celebration that is disproportionate to an ordinary task. Where reversal is possible, offer it clearly.

### Error messages

Explain what happened in plain language, preserve the user's work, and give a recovery step. Do not use error codes without explanation or imply fault. For system failures, say “We could not save your changes. Your text is still here. Try again.”

UI copy should be reviewed in context. A clear sentence in a spreadsheet may become confusing when separated from the control, repeated across a screen, or read by assistive technology.

## Podcast Writing

Will Talks should sound educational, reflective, evidence-informed, and conversational. A script should support natural speech rather than turn the host into a narrator reading an article aloud.

Open by naming the subject and why it matters. Establish relevant scope and credentials without a long performance of authority. Use clear transitions, concrete examples, and occasional summaries to help listeners follow without visual cues. Explain technical terms when first used and provide verbal context for any chart, quotation, or reference.

Reflection should be honest without turning personal experience into universal evidence. Interviews should use open questions, represent guests accurately, and distinguish professional knowledge from personal perspective. Edit for clarity without manufacturing conflict or certainty.

Never use clickbait titles, alarming previews, staged controversy, or promises that an episode will transform a child's life. Do not ask guests or listeners to disclose more than is appropriate. Sensitive episodes should include content context and support information without sensational detail.

Podcast pages should provide an accurate title, summary, transcript, sources where relevant, key ideas, and practical next steps. Accessibility and usefulness continue after the recording ends.

## Social Media

On LinkedIn, Instagram, Facebook, YouTube, and future platforms, value should come before promotion. Each post should offer a useful idea, clear explanation, practical prompt, accurate resource, or honest update. Social media should help people reach the work; it should not force the work into the loudest conventions of a platform.

Adapt form without abandoning voice. LinkedIn may support professional context and partnership learning. Instagram may use concise visual explanation. Facebook may support family and educator communities. YouTube titles and descriptions should help users understand a video's purpose. The tone may vary, but accuracy, dignity, and calm remain constant.

Never manipulate engagement. Avoid “Share if you care,” manufactured controversy, rage prompts, false scarcity, engagement bait, diagnostic quizzes, and captions that withhold the useful answer solely to drive clicks. Do not use a child's distress as an attention device.

Comments and moderation are part of the brand voice. Respond respectfully, correct misinformation, protect privacy, and move individual or sensitive cases to appropriate support routes. Do not attempt diagnosis in public replies. Delete, limit, or report harmful content according to clear moderation standards rather than using conflict as reach.

Metrics should not reward language that contradicts the project's values. A lower-reach accurate post is better than a viral misleading one.

## AI-Generated Writing

AI may assist with outlines, drafts, summaries, variations, metadata, translation preparation, interface options, and editorial checks. It is not an authority and does not remove human responsibility.

Every substantive AI-generated contribution requires human review before publication. The reviewer must be able to assess the content's subject, audience, risk, and format. Psychological or safety-sensitive material may require a qualified professional; localization requires language and cultural review; UI text requires contextual and accessibility review.

AI-generated writing must not contain:

- Invented psychological facts or unsupported causal claims.
- Fabricated research, citations, statistics, organizations, or credentials.
- Invented quotations, testimonials, case studies, or lived experiences.
- Exaggerated certainty, diagnosis, individualized treatment, or guaranteed outcomes.
- Generic reassurance that obscures a risk or limitation.
- Stereotypes, assumptions, or language inconsistent with these standards.

Prompts should identify the audience, purpose, evidence boundaries, format, and relevant project standards. Reviewers should verify claims against primary or authoritative sources, check every citation, remove repetition and generic phrasing, and ensure the result offers an appropriate next step.

Do not provide personal or sensitive user information to an AI system without an approved, lawful, and necessary basis. User-facing AI should disclose material limitations and must not present generated responses as professional judgment. “AI-generated” never means “review optional.”

## Examples

The following examples show the progression from harmful or ineffective writing to language that fully expresses the Healthy Little Minds voice.

### Articles

**Bad**

> The shocking truth about childhood anxiety every parent needs to know!

↓

**Better**

> Understanding childhood anxiety

↓

**Healthy Little Minds**

> Childhood anxiety: what you may notice, how to respond, and when to seek help

The final version is specific, calm, practical, and clear about scope.

### Buttons

**Bad**

> CLICK HERE NOW

↓

**Better**

> Learn more

↓

**Healthy Little Minds**

> Read the parent guide

The final label names the action and destination without urgency.

### Error messages

**Bad**

> Oops! You did something wrong.

↓

**Better**

> Submission failed. Try again.

↓

**Healthy Little Minds**

> We could not send your message. Your text is still here. Check your connection and try again.

The final message explains the state, preserves trust, and gives a recovery step.

### Teacher resources

**Bad**

> Good teachers begin every day with an emotional check-in.

↓

**Better**

> Try a daily emotional check-in with your class.

↓

**Healthy Little Minds**

> If time allows, use this two-minute check-in at the start of class. Children may pass rather than share aloud.

The final version respects time, choice, and classroom reality.

### Parent guidance

**Bad**

> Never react angrily. Your child will copy everything you do.

↓

**Better**

> Try to stay calm when your child is angry.

↓

**Healthy Little Minds**

> Pause if you can before responding. If you react in a way you regret, return to the conversation and model repair.

The final version supports action without demanding perfection or using fear.

### Podcast introductions

**Bad**

> Today we reveal the one secret that will fix emotional outbursts forever.

↓

**Better**

> Today we are talking about emotional outbursts and what can help.

↓

**Healthy Little Minds**

> Today we are exploring what may be happening during an emotional outburst, how adults can respond safely, and why no single strategy works for every child.

The final version is conversational, useful, and honest about variation.

### Social media captions

**Bad**

> If you care about children's mental health, share this with five parents!

↓

**Better**

> Here are three ways to support an anxious child. Please share.

↓

**Healthy Little Minds**

> An anxious child may need connection before advice. These three phrases can help you begin a calm conversation. Save them for a moment when words are difficult.

The final version delivers value before requesting engagement.

### Emails

**Bad**

> URGENT: You are missing essential resources for your child!

↓

**Better**

> New emotional learning resources are available

↓

**Healthy Little Minds**

> New classroom and home activities for practising emotional vocabulary

The final subject line describes the content accurately without manufacturing urgency.

### Notifications

**Bad**

> Your child needs you! Come back now!

↓

**Better**

> You have not completed today's activity.

↓

**Healthy Little Minds**

> Your saved activity is ready when you want to continue.

The final notification preserves choice and avoids guilt. Notifications should be sent only when they provide a clear user benefit.

### Child-facing validation

**Bad**

> Wrong! Try to control your anger.

↓

**Better**

> That was not the best choice. Try again.

↓

**Healthy Little Minds**

> Feelings do not have one right answer. Let us look at what might help keep everyone safe.

The final version avoids scoring an emotion and redirects attention to a meaningful goal.

### Sensitive guidance

**Bad**

> Do not panic, but this could be a serious mental disorder.

↓

**Better**

> Speak to a professional if you are concerned.

↓

**Healthy Little Minds**

> If this change is persistent, intense, or affecting daily life, consider speaking with a qualified health professional who can assess the individual situation.

The final version identifies useful conditions and an appropriate next step without diagnosing or alarming.

## Voice Checklist

Before publishing, confirm the applicable statements:

### Voice and tone

- [ ] The writing is recognizably calm, warm, respectful, evidence-informed, encouraging, practical, curious, honest, and inclusive.
- [ ] The tone fits the audience, context, and likely emotional state.
- [ ] The language avoids judgment, alarm, sensationalism, sarcasm, preaching, patronizing, manipulation, corporate jargon, and emotional exaggeration.
- [ ] Hope is realistic and no outcome is guaranteed.

### Clarity and action

- [ ] The purpose and primary message are clear early.
- [ ] Plain English is used without removing necessary nuance.
- [ ] Psychological and educational terms are explained.
- [ ] Examples make important abstractions usable.
- [ ] Instructions identify who should do what and in what order.
- [ ] The reader has a relevant next step or appropriate route to further help.

### Accuracy and safety

- [ ] Claims match the evidence and meaningful uncertainty remains visible.
- [ ] The writing does not diagnose, prescribe individual treatment, or imply professional authority it does not have.
- [ ] Sensitive information is calm, direct, proportionate, and easy to find.
- [ ] Safeguarding, crisis, or professional-support guidance has received appropriate review.
- [ ] Children and adults are never blamed for circumstances beyond their control.

### Inclusion and access

- [ ] People are described with dignity and not reduced to behaviour, diagnosis, or identity.
- [ ] Assumptions about disability, culture, family, gender, language, and neurodiversity have been reviewed.
- [ ] Sentence structure, headings, labels, and instructions support different literacy and cognitive needs.
- [ ] The writing can be localized without depending on unexplained idioms, wordplay, or a single cultural context.
- [ ] Interface text remains meaningful in context and with assistive technology.

### Format and review

- [ ] The writing fits its format: article, lesson, story, podcast, social post, email, notification, or interface.
- [ ] Headlines, subject lines, previews, and calls to action represent the content accurately.
- [ ] Repetition and language that performs expertise have been removed.
- [ ] Sources, quotations, names, links, and support details have been checked.
- [ ] AI-generated writing has received accountable human and specialist review where required.
- [ ] The final wording would remain defensible if explained to a child, parent, teacher, translator, disabled user, and qualified professional.

If an essential item cannot be confirmed, revise the work or obtain appropriate review before publication.

## Closing Reflection

People remember not only what Healthy Little Minds teaches, but how it makes them feel while learning. The same information can create shame or agency, alarm or clarity, distance or connection depending on how it is expressed.

The Healthy Little Minds voice should consistently leave people feeling respected, calmer, more capable, and better prepared for real life. It should not achieve calm by hiding difficult truths or create confidence through false certainty. Its strength should come from careful language, honest limits, practical help, and sustained regard for the person receiving the message.
