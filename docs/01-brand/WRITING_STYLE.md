# Healthy Little Minds Writing Style

## Purpose

This handbook establishes editorial consistency across the Healthy Little Minds ecosystem. It governs the structure, mechanics, terminology, formatting, sourcing, and review of written work. It applies whether the writing appears in a long article, a single button, a lesson plan, an interactive story, a podcast script, technical documentation, a translated resource, or an AI-generated draft.

The Voice & Tone handbook defines how Healthy Little Minds should sound. This document defines how writing should be constructed and presented. Contributors should use both. A sentence can sound warm and still be ambiguous; it can be grammatically correct and still be inaccessible. Editorial quality requires voice, structure, accuracy, and mechanics to work together.

Good writing should reduce cognitive load, improve accessibility, strengthen learning, and increase trust. Readers should be able to identify the purpose, follow the reasoning, understand the terms, and act on the information without unnecessary effort.

These standards create defaults, not excuses for mechanical editing. Audience, format, language, and safety may justify an exception. Editorial preferences must never override clarity, accuracy, accessibility, psychological responsibility, or child dignity.

Use British English as the default editorial variety unless a localized edition specifies another standard. This includes forms such as “behaviour,” “practise” as a verb, “practice” as a noun, and day–month–year dates in prose. Do not mix language varieties within the same edition.

---

## Writing Objectives

Every written asset should achieve six objectives.

### Be understood quickly

Place the main point where readers expect it. Use recognizable words, visible structure, and direct instructions. Quick understanding does not require every subject to be short.

### Be psychologically accurate

Use terms within their accepted scope, match certainty to evidence, and distinguish general education from individual assessment or treatment. Never improve readability by introducing a false claim.

### Be practical

Connect information to a relevant action, example, question, routine, decision, or next step. State who should do what and where the limits lie.

### Be inclusive

Avoid unnecessary assumptions about culture, family, gender, disability, language, school, resources, or emotional expression. Use terminology preferred by the person or relevant community where known.

### Be accessible

Write for scanning, screen readers, zoomed and mobile layouts, varied literacy, cognitive differences, language learners, and translation.

### Be memorable without exaggeration

Use concrete examples, purposeful repetition, meaningful labels, and clear summaries. Do not use shock, fear, inflated claims, or invented certainty to make content memorable.

When objectives compete, accuracy, safety, and dignity take precedence. Then seek the clearest structure that preserves them.

## Structure Before Style

Organise information before refining individual sentences. A polished paragraph cannot repair a page with no clear purpose or sequence.

Begin by defining:

1. The primary audience.
2. The question or need the asset addresses.
3. The outcome the reader should reach.
4. The minimum information required for that outcome.
5. The appropriate next step and any scope or safety boundary.

### One main idea per section

Each section should answer one recognizable question or advance one part of the explanation. If a section needs several unrelated headings to describe it, divide it. If two short sections repeat the same point, combine them.

### Logical progression

Use an order that matches the reader's task. Common patterns include:

- Situation → explanation → action → further help.
- Objective → materials → preparation → steps → reflection.
- Concept → example → practice → application.
- Problem → cause or context → options → decision.

Do not imitate a standard pattern if another sequence better fits the task. Safety-critical action should appear before background detail.

### Clear introductions

An introduction should identify the subject, intended audience where needed, and practical value. Avoid opening with a long history, broad statement, dictionary definition, or suspense.

### Useful conclusions

A conclusion should consolidate the central point and indicate an appropriate next step. It should not merely repeat the introduction or introduce a major new claim.

### Predictable heading hierarchy

Headings should reveal the page outline and follow a sequential hierarchy. Structure content semantically rather than choosing heading levels for visual size. Styling belongs to the design system.

### Progressive disclosure

Give readers essential information first, then allow access to examples, technical detail, sources, adaptations, or advanced guidance. Do not hide safety information, key limitations, or necessary instructions behind optional controls. Progressive disclosure reduces initial load; it must not conceal meaning.

Before line editing, review the outline alone. If the headings do not tell a coherent story, revise the structure.

## Headings

Headings help readers predict content, scan efficiently, return to a section, and navigate with assistive technology.

### Page titles

Use one H1 for the primary page or document title. It should name the subject accurately and distinguish the resource where needed. Prefer “Helping a Child Manage Worry Before School” to “When Mornings Feel Hard.” The second may be emotionally appealing, but it is less discoverable and precise.

Keep titles concise enough to display clearly, but do not remove necessary meaning to meet an arbitrary character count. Search titles and social titles may be adapted without becoming sensational or misleading.

### Heading hierarchy

- Use H1 once for the title.
- Use H2 for major sections.
- Use H3 for subsections within an H2.
- Use H4–H6 only when genuine nesting requires them.
- Do not skip levels, such as moving from H2 to H4.
- Do not use bold paragraphs as substitutes for headings.

Deep hierarchy often signals that a resource needs restructuring. Most public pages should rarely need more than three heading levels.

### Descriptive headings

State what the section contains. Headings may be phrased as topics, questions, or actions, but remain consistent within a sequence.

**Avoid:** “A Few Thoughts”

**Prefer:** “Why Children May Avoid Difficult Tasks”

**Avoid:** “Ready?”

**Prefer:** “Prepare the Classroom Activity”

### Scannability

Front-load meaningful words and distinguish adjacent headings. “What You May Notice” and “What You Can Try” are more useful than several headings beginning “Understanding…”. Headings should remain meaningful when listed without surrounding paragraphs.

### Capitalisation

Use title case for H1 document titles when established by the resource name. Use sentence case for most page and section headings: “What to try at home,” not “What To Try At Home.” Named programmes and formal document titles retain their approved capitalisation.

Avoid clever headings that rely on wordplay, ambiguity, or cultural references. They translate poorly and force readers to infer the subject.

## Paragraphs

Use one main idea per paragraph. Begin with a sentence that establishes the idea, then explain, qualify, or illustrate it. Move to a new paragraph when the subject, time, actor, or function changes.

For public digital content, aim for roughly two to five sentences in most paragraphs. This is a guide, not a rule. A single sentence may deserve its own paragraph for clarity. A longer paragraph may be appropriate when dividing it would break a tightly connected explanation. Review blocks that exceed about 100 words, especially on mobile.

Use transitions when a relationship is not obvious, but do not add them mechanically. Do not create several one-sentence paragraphs merely to look concise or compress distinct ideas into a dense block.

Spacing is part of readability. Separate paragraphs in digital content with consistent margins rather than indented first lines. Do not insert repeated blank lines, manual spaces, or line breaks to control visual layout. Use semantic components and style rules.

In worksheets and print resources, keep instructions close to the task they govern. Test the rendered format, not only the source text.

## Sentences

Aim for an average sentence length of approximately 15–20 words in general public content. This is a diagnostic range, not a target for every sentence. Vary length naturally to maintain rhythm and express relationships accurately.

Use shorter sentences when:

- Giving child-facing instructions.
- Explaining an error or recovery step.
- Communicating immediate safety information.
- Introducing an unfamiliar concept.
- Separating actions that must occur in order.
- Writing for constrained interface space.

Longer sentences may express conditions, contrast, or careful qualification. Keep the main clause easy to identify.

Prefer active voice when it clarifies responsibility:

**Passive:** “The activity should be completed after the story has been read.”

**Active:** “Read the story, then complete the activity.”

Passive voice is useful when the actor is unknown, unimportant, or intentionally protected: “The resource was updated in July 2026.” Do not convert every passive construction mechanically.

Reduce ambiguity by naming actors, objects, and references. Avoid unclear pronouns:

**Ambiguous:** “Tell the parent when they are calm.”

**Clear:** “When the child is calm, tell the parent what happened.”

Place modifiers beside the words they modify. Avoid long noun strings. Use positive construction where it remains accurate, while retaining negative instructions for prohibited or unsafe actions.

## Word Choice

Choose words for precision, familiarity, and usefulness.

### Use concrete language

Name observable actions and specific outcomes.

**Abstract:** “Facilitate emotional expression.”

**Concrete:** “Invite children to name or draw what they are feeling.”

Concrete language does not mean claiming to observe an internal state. Write “The child looked away and stopped speaking,” not “The child did not care.”

### Prefer plain English

Use “before” rather than “prior to,” “about” rather than “with regard to,” and “help” rather than “facilitate” where meaning is unchanged. Plain language is compatible with serious subjects and professional readers.

### Remove filler

Delete phrases that delay meaning: “It is important to note that,” “in order to,” “at this point in time,” and “the fact that.” Retain an introductory phrase when it genuinely frames uncertainty or scope.

**Wordy:** “It is important to remember that children may sometimes require additional support.”

**Direct:** “Some children may need additional support.”

### Avoid unnecessary jargon

Do not assume users understand internal names, acronyms, clinical terminology, or technical language. Define necessary terms at first use and apply them consistently.

### Explain technical terms

Give a concise definition and, where useful, an example:

> Co-regulation is the support one person provides to help another person become calmer and more organised. For example, an adult may lower their voice, reduce demands, and guide slower breathing.

### Maintain terminology

Choose one term for each concept within a resource. Do not alternate among “pupil,” “student,” “learner,” and “child” merely for variety if they refer to the same group. Use the terminology table in Appendix A and record project-specific terms in an editorial glossary.

Avoid anthropomorphising the platform. Prefer “This guide explains…” to “Healthy Little Minds believes…” unless describing an actual organisational commitment.

## Lists

Use a list when items are easier to scan separately than in a sentence. Avoid turning every set of two or three related ideas into bullets.

### Bulleted lists

Use bullets for items without a required order, such as signs to notice, materials, examples, or options. Do not use bullets for a sequence where order affects the outcome.

### Numbered lists

Use numbers for steps, rankings, or referenced items. Begin steps with an action and place warnings before the action they affect.

### Checklists

Use checklists for verification or repeatable preparation. Each item should describe a condition the user can confirm. Do not use checkboxes as decoration or for tasks the user cannot control.

### Comparison tables

Use tables to compare the same attributes across options. Keep headings and cells concise, avoid wide mobile tables, and provide an accessible alternative when a complex table cannot reflow.

### Parallel structure

Items in a list should use the same grammatical form.

**Inconsistent:**

- Naming the feeling.
- Take a slow breath.
- A trusted adult can help.

**Parallel:**

- Name the feeling.
- Take a slow breath.
- Ask a trusted adult for help.

Introduce lists with a complete sentence. Use full stops when items are complete sentences or contain multiple clauses. Short fragments may omit terminal punctuation. Do not mix styles within one list.

## Emphasis

Emphasis should reveal structure or meaning. Too much emphasis removes hierarchy and makes pages harder to scan.

### Bold

Use bold for key terms, short lead-ins, or critical phrases. Do not bold full paragraphs or every action verb.

### Italics

Use italics sparingly for titles of complete works, linguistic examples, or occasional emphasis. Avoid italics for long passages.

### Quotation marks

Use double quotation marks for direct quotations and spoken examples, and single marks for a quotation within one. Do not use quotation marks for informal emphasis.

### Capital letters

Do not use all capitals for emphasis, buttons, warnings, or headings. Capitals reduce word shape and may be read awkwardly by assistive technology. Use sentence case and visual styling. Retain capitals for established abbreviations and proper names.

### Exclamation marks

Use exclamation marks rarely. One may be appropriate in a genuinely celebratory, low-risk message. Never use repeated exclamation marks. Do not add excitement to safety, errors, emotional distress, or ordinary interface confirmations.

Links should be visually distinct without relying only on bold or colour. Link text should describe the destination or action rather than “click here.”

## Numbers

Consistency in numbers improves comprehension and translation.

- Spell out zero to nine in general prose; use numerals for 10 and above.
- Use numerals for ages, measurements, percentages, dates, times, steps, and data, even below 10.
- Do not begin a sentence with a numeral; rewrite or spell it out.
- Use commas in numbers of 1,000 or more unless a localization standard specifies otherwise.
- Avoid unnecessary decimal places and false precision.

### Percentages

Use the % symbol with numerals: 25%. State the denominator, timeframe, or comparison when it affects interpretation.

### Measurements

Use metric units by default and provide a local equivalent when required. Place a space between number and unit: 5 cm.

### Dates

Use “3 July 2026” in prose and ISO 8601 (`2026-07-03`) in technical data. Avoid ambiguous numeric dates.

### Time

Use “9:30 a.m.” and “2 p.m.” in prose unless localization specifies otherwise. State the time zone for multi-region events.

### Age ranges

Write “ages 6–8” or “children aged 6 to 8.” Use an en dash for a compact range and “to” in running prose. Age guidance is approximate unless a validated instrument or legal rule requires exact limits; state developmental context where relevant.

### Statistics and uncertainty

Provide source, population, timeframe, and relevant limitations. Prefer “In this study of 240 pupils…” to “Studies show…”. Do not convert association into causation.

Use “about,” “approximately,” or a range when exactness is not justified. Never invent a number to make guidance appear authoritative.

## Psychological Terminology

Psychological terms carry clinical, cultural, and personal meaning. Use them accurately, define them for the audience, and avoid turning educational content into diagnosis.

- **Trauma:** Do not use as a synonym for every painful or stressful event. Distinguish an event from a person's response, avoid diagnosing trauma, and use trauma-informed principles without claiming treatment.
- **Anxiety:** Distinguish a feeling of worry, persistent anxiety, and an anxiety disorder. Do not label ordinary nervousness as a disorder or diagnose through a checklist.
- **Depression:** Use for the clinical condition only when context supports it. Sadness, disappointment, and low mood are not automatically depression. Include appropriate professional guidance when discussing symptoms.
- **Resilience:** Describe the capacity and supports involved in adapting to difficulty. Do not present resilience as toughness, emotional silence, or an individual duty to withstand harmful environments.
- **Emotional regulation:** Explain as processes that influence how emotions are noticed, understood, expressed, and managed. It does not mean suppressing feelings or remaining calm at all times.
- **Attachment:** Use within established developmental and clinical meaning. Do not assign attachment styles casually, blame caregivers through simplified labels, or claim that a brief behaviour reveals an attachment pattern.

Apply the following rules to all psychological terminology:

1. Check the current accepted definition and intended scope.
2. Explain the term in plain language at first meaningful use.
3. Separate observable behaviour from interpretation.
4. Distinguish education, screening, assessment, diagnosis, and treatment.
5. Match claims and recommendations to evidence.
6. Avoid fixed labels that reduce a person to a condition or behaviour.
7. Obtain qualified review for clinical, developmental, safeguarding, or high-risk material.
8. Include limitations and routes to further help where relevant.

Do not create branded psychological terms that imply a scientific status they do not possess.

## Citations and References

Prefer primary research for specific findings and authoritative evidence reviews or professional guidance for broad recommendations. Assess source quality, population, method, date, limitations, conflicts of interest, and relevance. A recent source is not automatically better, and an old source is not automatically obsolete.

### Citing research

Place citations close to the supported claim. Ensure the source supports its exact wording. Use readable linked references, endnotes, or a consistent reference list according to content type.

### Quoting experts

Quote only when the original wording adds value. Verify wording, speaker, source, context, permission, and translation. Keep quotations brief.

### Linking sources

Link to the specific source, not a search page or general homepage. Use descriptive link text. Prefer stable sources and persistent identifiers where available. Check that links are accessible, lawful, and not misleading about endorsement.

### Paraphrasing

Paraphrase the meaning in original language and cite the source. Changing a few words is not adequate paraphrasing. Do not remove qualifications, population details, or uncertainty during simplification.

### Maintaining evidence

Record source access or review dates where appropriate. Review time-sensitive and safety-critical evidence on a defined schedule. Replace or contextualise outdated sources; do not update a citation without checking whether the surrounding claim also needs revision. Preserve a correction history when changes materially affect guidance.

AI-generated citations must be treated as unverified until each source and claim has been checked directly.

## Accessibility

Accessible writing allows more people to find, understand, and act on information.

### Screen readers

Use semantic headings, real lists, descriptive links, table headers, and meaningful alternative text. Avoid instructions based only on position, shape, or colour, such as “click the green button on the right.” Expand an abbreviation at first use. Write punctuation naturally; decorative symbols can produce confusing announcements.

### Cognitive accessibility

Keep tasks focused, sequence instructions, use consistent terms, and make consequences explicit. Avoid double negatives, ambiguous choices, unnecessary time limits, and memory-dependent directions. Summarise long processes and place help beside the relevant step.

### Dyslexia

Use clear structure, manageable paragraphs, left-aligned text, familiar words, and sufficient spacing. Avoid long italics, full capitals, and dense justified text. Do not assume that a specialist font solves broader readability problems.

### Language learners

Use direct syntax, explicit references, and concrete examples. Avoid unexplained idioms, phrasal verbs with obscure meanings, culturally dependent humour, and complex noun strings. Language proficiency does not indicate intelligence; do not remove substantive meaning unnecessarily.

### Translation

Write complete, self-contained strings with context for translators. Avoid assembling sentences from fragments in code. Preserve placeholders clearly and explain variable meaning. Keep terminology consistent and identify content requiring cultural or regional adaptation.

### Mobile reading

Front-load useful information, keep headings descriptive, break up dense content, and avoid tables wider than the viewport. Place instructions before the control or activity they govern. Test actual line wrapping, link text, and error messages at narrow widths and high zoom.

## Localization

Write source content so meaning can travel across languages without treating English structure as universal.

- Avoid idioms such as “keep your chin up,” “on the same page,” or “a piece of cake” unless the expression itself is being taught.
- Avoid puns and wordplay in headings, calls to action, and essential instructions.
- Do not assume specific family roles, school structures, holidays, seasons, foods, services, or emotional customs.
- Use simple sentence structures while preserving adult-level meaning.
- Keep pronoun references clear and avoid strings that depend on English word order.
- Choose examples that can be replaced without changing the learning objective.
- Provide context for screenshots, images, symbols, quotations, and proper names.

Localization is adaptation, not mechanical translation. Emotion words and formality may differ, while safeguarding routes, school terms, dates, and measurements may require regional change.

Allow for text expansion and different scripts. Avoid text in images. Store user-facing strings separately and give translators context, audience, and intended action.

Localized content requires language, cultural, subject, and layout review in proportion to risk. Machine translation can assist a workflow but cannot approve a final psychological or safety-sensitive resource.

## User Interface Text

Interface text follows the Voice & Tone handbook and the same accuracy standards as long-form content. Its limited space increases the need for precise editing.

- **Buttons:** Begin with a clear verb and name the result: “Download worksheet,” not “Continue” when the next action can be stated.
- **Labels:** Use stable nouns and visible labels. Do not rely on placeholder text. Match labels to the terms users see elsewhere.
- **Forms:** Explain why sensitive information is needed, identify optional fields, and place instructions before input.
- **Helper text:** Add information needed to complete the task, not repeated labels or promotional copy.
- **Notifications:** State the relevant event or available action. Do not create guilt, urgency, or dependence.
- **Confirmations:** Confirm what changed and whether another step is needed. Keep celebration proportional.
- **Errors:** Explain the problem, preserve user work, and provide a recovery step. Avoid blame and unexplained codes.

Use sentence case throughout interface text unless a proper name requires capitals. Omit terminal punctuation on short button and navigation labels. Use punctuation for complete helper, confirmation, and error sentences.

Review strings in the actual interface, including loading, empty, error, disabled, offline, and long translated states. The surrounding layout and action determine whether wording is clear.

## Podcast Scripts

Podcast scripts are written for the ear. They should sound natural when spoken and remain understandable without visual reference.

Format scripts with clear speaker names, section headings, pronunciation notes, and distinct production cues. Use short paragraphs and mark pauses sparingly.

Open by naming the topic, relevance, and scope. Use signposting such as “First, let us look at…” and “The important distinction is…” to replace the visual structure a reader would otherwise see. Recap after complex sections and before changing direction.

Use natural spoken forms. Read numbers, abbreviations, URLs, and citations aloud; rewrite anything aurally ambiguous.

Use examples and questions without manufacturing suspense or delaying the main value to increase listening time.

Scripts should identify sources conversationally when relevant, with full references on the episode page. Include content context and support information for sensitive subjects. Prepare an edited transcript that preserves meaning and speaker identity without retaining every false start.

Perform a read-aloud edit and time the script at a realistic pace.

## AI-Assisted Writing

AI-generated writing is an unverified draft. The human contributor remains accountable for the final asset.

Before generation, provide the AI assistant with the intended audience, purpose, format, source material, evidence boundaries, applicable project documents, and required output. Do not ask it to fill factual gaps through inference. Protect personal, confidential, and sensitive information.

Review AI-assisted work in this order:

1. **Purpose:** Confirm that it answers the actual need and does not add unsupported scope.
2. **Facts:** Verify every substantive claim, name, date, number, definition, quotation, and link.
3. **Psychology:** Check terminology, evidence strength, uncertainty, developmental fit, safety, and the boundary between education and care.
4. **Citations:** Open every cited source and confirm existence, authorship, relevance, and support for the claim. Replace fabricated or secondary citations.
5. **Structure:** Remove generic introductions, repetition, artificial symmetry, and sections that do not help the reader.
6. **Language:** Edit for plain English, consistent terminology, natural rhythm, inclusion, and the Healthy Little Minds voice.
7. **Accessibility and localization:** Check headings, links, instructions, alternatives, string context, idioms, and assumptions.
8. **Format:** Test the writing in its final page, resource, script, interface, or document.
9. **Specialist review:** Obtain qualified review where subject or risk requires it.

Never permit AI to invent psychology, research, citations, quotes, testimonials, case studies, partnerships, or user experiences. Do not retain a claim because it sounds plausible. Do not ask an AI assistant to imitate a living writer's distinctive style.

Use automated consistency checks to support, not replace, editorial judgment. Record AI involvement when provenance matters for accountability, rights, or future revision.

## Editorial Checklist

Before publication, confirm the applicable items.

### Purpose and structure

- [ ] The audience, need, and intended outcome are clear.
- [ ] The title and introduction identify the subject accurately.
- [ ] Sections follow a logical order and contain one main idea.
- [ ] Heading hierarchy is sequential, descriptive, and scannable.
- [ ] Essential information appears before optional detail.
- [ ] The conclusion provides closure and a useful next step.

### Clarity and mechanics

- [ ] Paragraphs are focused and manageable at mobile width.
- [ ] Sentence length and structure suit the audience and task.
- [ ] Actors, pronouns, conditions, and sequences are unambiguous.
- [ ] Active voice is used where it improves responsibility and clarity.
- [ ] Words are concrete, plain, precise, and consistent.
- [ ] Filler, unnecessary jargon, repetition, and decorative emphasis are removed.
- [ ] Lists use the correct format and parallel grammatical structure.
- [ ] Numbers, dates, times, ages, measurements, and percentages follow the standard.
- [ ] British English spelling and punctuation are consistent unless localized otherwise.

### Accuracy and responsibility

- [ ] Psychological terms are defined and used within scope.
- [ ] Observation is distinguished from interpretation and diagnosis.
- [ ] Claims match the strength and relevance of evidence.
- [ ] Statistics include necessary population, timeframe, and uncertainty.
- [ ] Quotations, paraphrases, citations, and links have been verified.
- [ ] Professional, safeguarding, or emergency guidance is present where needed.
- [ ] Qualified review has occurred for sensitive or specialist material.

### Inclusion and accessibility

- [ ] People are described with dignity and not reduced to labels.
- [ ] Assumptions about disability, culture, family, gender, language, resources, and neurodiversity are reviewed.
- [ ] Headings, lists, links, tables, and alternatives use semantic structure.
- [ ] Instructions do not rely only on colour, shape, sound, or position.
- [ ] Content supports cognitive access, screen readers, zoom, dyslexia, language learners, and mobile reading as applicable.
- [ ] Link text and alternative text communicate purpose in context.

### Localization and format

- [ ] Idioms, wordplay, fragments, and culturally fixed examples are removed or intentional.
- [ ] User-facing strings include translator context and are not built from fragments.
- [ ] Layout allows text expansion and different scripts.
- [ ] Regional services, terms, measurements, and dates are identified for adaptation.
- [ ] The final asset has been reviewed in its rendered format.

### Final review

- [ ] Names, titles, metadata, links, downloads, captions, and references are correct.
- [ ] Voice & Tone requirements are met without weakening editorial clarity.
- [ ] AI-assisted content has completed factual, citation, consistency, accessibility, and human review.
- [ ] The asset can be maintained, corrected, translated, and retired.
- [ ] A reader can understand the key message and know what to do next.

## Appendix B — Formatting Examples

These examples show source Markdown and preferred editorial structure.

### Headings

```markdown
# Helping Children Understand Anger

## What anger may feel like

### Changes in the body
```

Use one H1, then descend one level at a time. Do not choose heading levels for appearance.

### Paragraphs

```markdown
Anger can affect the body, thoughts, and actions. A child may notice a faster heartbeat, tight muscles, or an urge to move.

Help the child notice these changes while they are calm. Recognition can make it easier to pause before acting.
```

Each paragraph advances one idea. The second turns explanation into action.

### Bulleted lists

```markdown
You will need:

- Paper.
- Coloured pencils.
- A quiet place to work.
```

Use bullets because the materials have no required order.

### Numbered lists

```markdown
1. Read the story together.
2. Ask what the character may be feeling.
3. Invite the child to choose one response to discuss.
```

Use numbers because the activity follows a sequence. Begin each item with a parallel action verb.

### Tables

```markdown
| If you notice | You might try |
|---------------|---------------|
| The child becomes quiet | Offer time before asking another question. |
| The child wants to move | Continue the conversation during a short walk. |
```

Use a table only when row-by-row comparison improves understanding. Keep headers and cells concise.

### Quotations

```markdown
> “I can see that this is difficult. We can take one step at a time.”
```

Use block quotations for words intended to be read or spoken as a complete example. Identify and cite external speakers.

### Callout boxes

```markdown
> **Remember:** A child may choose not to share personal feelings with the group. Offer a private or non-verbal alternative.
```

Use a short, descriptive label. Callouts should contain information readers need to notice, not decorative summaries of every section.

### References

```markdown
## References

1. Author, A. A. (Year). Title of article. *Journal Name, volume*(issue), pages. https://doi.org/example
2. Organisation Name. (Year). *Title of guidance*. https://example.org/guidance
```

Use one approved reference style consistently within an asset. Link to the specific source and verify every detail.

## Closing Reflection

Excellent writing should feel invisible. Readers should remember what they learned, what they can try, and where they can find support—not the effort required to understand the sentence.

That apparent ease is created through deliberate structure, precise language, responsible terminology, accessible formatting, careful sourcing, and rigorous review. When those practices are consistent, writing becomes a dependable part of how Healthy Little Minds teaches and earns trust.
