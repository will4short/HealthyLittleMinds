# Healthy Little Minds Illustration Guidelines

## Purpose

Illustrations are educational tools, not decoration. They can make an emotion recognizable, clarify a relationship, show a sequence, create safe distance from a difficult subject, support memory, and help people feel represented. They can also confuse, stereotype, frighten, or distract when created without a clear purpose.

This handbook defines the illustration philosophy, artistic direction, educational role, consistency rules, accessibility expectations, localization responsibilities, governance, and AI illustration standards for Healthy Little Minds. It applies to product experiences, stories, resources, audio artwork, dashboards, documentation, marketing, social media, and future formats.

Every illustration should have an identifiable job. It may:

- Explain a concept or action.
- Support emotional recognition.
- Establish setting or narrative continuity.
- Model a healthy interaction.
- Guide attention through a sequence.
- Represent the people who use the platform.
- Create warmth or reassurance appropriate to the context.

Illustrations should improve understanding, emotional safety, inclusion, learning, memory, and appropriate engagement. They should simplify ideas without removing necessary nuance and should never compete with the information or task they support.

The system must remain coherent across many contributors and years of work. A recurring character should remain recognizable; a safety illustration should not resemble entertainment; a worksheet image should survive printing; a localized scene should feel culturally credible without becoming a separate visual brand.

This document does not teach drawing or prescribe software workflows. It defines the principles and standards against which briefs, sketches, generated assets, final artwork, placement, adaptation, and maintenance should be reviewed.

When artistic preference conflicts with child dignity, psychological responsibility, accessibility, cultural respect, or educational clarity, those responsibilities take precedence.

## Illustration Philosophy

### Learning before decoration

Illustration should begin with a learning or communication need. The brief should state what a person should notice, understand, remember, or feel able to do after seeing the image. An image that merely fills empty space should be removed or given a genuine purpose.

Decorative illustration can contribute warmth and identity, but it must remain secondary to the page's task. It should not resemble an interactive control, interrupt reading, or increase download cost without meaningful benefit.

### Warmth before realism

Healthy Little Minds illustrations should feel human, approachable, and emotionally safe. Warmth can come from posture, relationships, composition, shape, and attentive detail. It does not require constant smiling or idealized situations.

The system favours a stylized visual language over photographic realism. Stylization allows clear focus, broad representation, and gentle treatment of difficult subjects. Realism should not introduce visual intensity that makes distress feel immediate or frightening.

### Clarity before detail

The important action, expression, or relationship should be identifiable at a glance. Detail should establish context or character, not compete for attention. Small decorative objects, complex patterns, and crowded backgrounds can make a scene harder to understand, especially for children and people with cognitive or visual access needs.

Simplification must preserve meaning. Removing context that explains why a character feels or acts a certain way can make the illustration ambiguous or misleading.

### Diversity before stereotypes

The illustrated world should reflect varied children, adults, families, cultures, bodies, disabilities, languages, homes, and schools. Representation should be woven naturally through ordinary scenes rather than reserved for special diversity content.

Avoid visual shortcuts that assign personality, ability, wealth, profession, family role, or emotional state based on appearance. Diversity requires research and consistency, not random combinations of visible traits.

### Emotional honesty before exaggeration

Emotions should be recognizable without becoming theatrical. Sadness does not always involve tears, anger does not make a child monstrous, and happiness does not require a broad smile. Body posture, gaze, distance, hands, context, and facial expression should work together.

Honesty includes mixed and subtle feelings. Images should leave room for individual variation and should not imply that emotional skill means looking calm or happy at all times.

### Consistency before novelty

Shared artistic language helps users recognize Healthy Little Minds and understand recurring visual functions. Character construction, shape, line, colour relationships, expression, perspective, and complexity should remain compatible across assets.

New styles may be explored for a demonstrated format or audience need, but novelty alone is not justification. Uncoordinated styles make the platform feel assembled from unrelated sources and weaken recurring-character trust.

These principles should be applied together. An image can be clear but stereotyped, warm but educationally empty, or diverse but visually inconsistent. Approval requires the complete standard.

## Educational Role

### Emotional learning

Illustrations can help users connect emotion words to visible and contextual cues. They should show that people express the same emotion differently and that one expression can have several possible explanations. Pair images with language and context rather than using facial recognition as a diagnostic exercise.

Images can also model the difference between feeling and action. A character may feel angry while choosing to pause, seek help, set a boundary, or repair harm. Do not depict emotional regulation as erasing the emotion.

### Classroom teaching

Classroom illustration can introduce a routine, demonstrate activity steps, support discussion, and reduce reading demands. Visuals should work at the intended viewing distance and preserve meaning in print or projection. A teacher should know what the image is intended to teach and how it relates to the lesson.

### Parent education

Illustrations can make abstract guidance practical by showing listening posture, shared regulation, conversation, routines, and repair. They should avoid presenting one ideal family or a perfect adult response. Include realistic contexts without surrounding families with visual disorder or judgment.

### Storytelling

Story illustration builds setting, character, sequence, emotional continuity, and inference. It can let children explore difficult experiences through a fictional character rather than direct disclosure. Images should add narrative meaning rather than repeat every sentence literally.

### Reflection

Reflective images can provide a neutral starting point for questions. They should allow more than one plausible observation and avoid forcing users to interpret a character through stereotypes. Personal disclosure must remain optional.

### Psychology education

Diagrams and scenes can explain body signals, thought–feeling–action relationships, co-regulation, perspective, stress responses, and help-seeking. These images must be reviewed for psychological accuracy. Metaphors should be identified as metaphors and should not become false biological claims.

### Social-emotional learning

Illustration can model turn-taking, empathy, disagreement, repair, boundaries, collaboration, and decision-making. Show relationships and environments, not only isolated children managing themselves. Emotional learning is social as well as individual.

Educational impact should be assessed in context. Ask what users understand from the image before reading the caption, what they understand after, and whether the combination supports the intended outcome without introducing a misconception.

## Illustration Style

The canonical Healthy Little Minds style is a calm, flat, shape-led visual language designed for educational clarity and emotional warmth.

### Flat illustration

Use primarily flat areas of colour and restrained depth. Limited shading may clarify form or focus, but the image should not depend on realistic lighting, texture, or dimensional rendering. Flat construction supports consistency, scalability, clean reproduction, and adaptation.

### Soft geometry

Build scenes from clear, gentle shapes rather than sharp, aggressive, or highly technical geometry. Shape language should feel stable and approachable. Soft does not mean every object must be rounded; real objects and important directional cues should remain recognizable.

### Rounded forms

Rounded contours can support warmth in characters, furniture, callouts, and environmental forms. Use them consistently and with restraint. Excessive roundness can make older audiences feel infantilized or reduce the clarity of objects.

### Gentle expressions

Faces should use controlled, readable changes in eyes, eyebrows, mouth, cheeks, and head angle. Expressions should be subtle enough to feel human and strong enough to read at the intended size. Context and posture should support the face.

### Friendly proportions

Characters may use simplified, slightly stylized proportions that support clear gesture and recognition. Avoid extreme head-to-body ratios, tiny bodies, or caricatured features that make children look like mascots. Adults and adolescents should remain distinguishable without becoming rigid stereotypes.

### Minimal visual clutter

Each scene should have a clear focal point. Include enough environmental detail to explain place, action, and relationship, then remove competing objects and patterns. Background characters should support context rather than create a search task.

### Calm compositions

Use stable balance, purposeful negative space, readable grouping, and clear direction. Avoid dramatic angles, extreme close-ups, chaotic diagonals, and crowded edges unless a carefully reviewed narrative moment requires tension.

### Clean backgrounds

Backgrounds should provide context without lowering figure contrast or interfering with text. Large areas can remain simple. When artwork sits near interface content, preserve safe visual space and avoid shapes that resemble controls.

### Educational clarity

The style must reproduce clearly across screen sizes, worksheets, thumbnails, social assets, and presentations. Important gestures and objects should survive reduction. Essential text should remain outside the illustration whenever possible.

Style consistency should be documented through approved reference sheets, character turnarounds, expression studies, proportions, palettes, scene examples, and asset metadata. References guide judgment; they should not encourage mechanical reuse that makes every scene identical.

## Character Design

Recurring and supporting characters should be designed as people with context and agency, not as symbols of lessons.

### Children

Child characters should reflect varied ages, bodies, skin tones, hair, disabilities, cultures, interests, and ways of expressing emotion. Their clothing and environments should be age-appropriate and practical. Avoid making child characters uniformly small, passive, cute, or cheerful.

Children should be shown making choices, asking questions, practising, helping, disagreeing, repairing, and seeking support. Do not depict them as responsible for adult conflict or for managing another person's unsafe behaviour.

### Parents

Parent and caregiver characters should represent different genders, ages, abilities, family relationships, and cultural contexts. They should not be visually divided into competent and incompetent adults. Show adults learning, pausing, making mistakes, setting boundaries, and repairing.

Use “parent” as a content role only when accurate; visual representation should also include grandparents, guardians, foster carers, older siblings where appropriate, and other trusted caregivers.

### Teachers

Teacher characters should look like real educators rather than authority caricatures. Include varied ages, genders, cultures, disabilities, clothing, and classroom styles. Show teachers facilitating, observing, listening, adapting, and seeking support—not only standing at the front and instructing.

Avoid implying that an ideal teacher is endlessly calm or personally responsible for solving systemic and clinical needs.

### Psychologists

Psychologist and other professional characters should be represented accurately and without clinical stereotypes. Avoid white coats unless genuinely appropriate, imposing desks, diagnostic props, or imagery that makes support feel frightening. Show collaborative conversation, boundaries, and varied settings.

Professional role should be clear through context rather than costume alone. Do not imply that every supportive adult is a psychologist.

### Narrators

Narrator figures may guide attention, explain a sequence, or connect parts of a resource. They should not compete with the learner or claim authority beyond the content. A narrator's visual presence, gestures, and frequency should remain consistent and should never pressure children to disclose or continue.

Every named recurring character needs a character record covering identity, role, age range, pronouns, appearance, personality, relationships, clothing logic, accessibility considerations, approved expressions, and narrative boundaries. Character information should support continuity without turning personality into a fixed emotional stereotype.

## Character Consistency

Recurring characters such as Amaka, Bella, Goshen, and Destiny must remain immediately recognizable across illustrations, stories, resources, languages, formats, and contributors.

### Facial structure

Preserve face shape, feature placement, eye construction, nose and mouth treatment, eyebrow shape, ear treatment, and distinguishing details. Expression may change these relationships temporarily but should not create a different person.

### Hairstyle

Maintain established hair texture, length, silhouette, parting, colour, and recurring accessories. Narrative changes such as a new style require approval and should remain consistent after introduction. Hair should be represented with cultural and physical credibility.

### Clothing identity

Characters may have signature colours, silhouettes, accessories, or garment types that aid recognition. They should not wear one exact outfit in every context unless the story requires it. Variations should follow the character's age, culture, setting, weather, activity, and established taste.

### Personality

Gesture, posture, interests, and response patterns should reflect the character record without making the person one-dimensional. A curious character can be uncertain; a confident character can need help. Do not assign one recurring emotion to a character.

### Body proportions

Preserve relative height, build, limb proportion, head proportion, and age cues. Camera perspective and pose may change apparent relationships, but a character should not become older, smaller, or differently built through style drift.

### Recognizable appearance

Key recognition cues should survive thumbnails, grayscale, different clothing, and translated contexts. Recognition must not depend on skin tone or colour alone. Silhouette, face, hair, posture, and recurring details should work together.

Maintain an authoritative character sheet for each recurring person, including front, side, and three-quarter views; proportions; expressions; gestures; clothing examples; colour relationships; and prohibited changes. Each approved asset should record character version and relevant references.

Do not redesign an established character during routine production. Proposed changes require a reason, continuity assessment, representative review, approval, version update, and plan for existing assets. AI-generated approximations do not become new references merely because they look plausible.

## Emotional Expression

Emotional expression should be authentic, proportionate, readable, and supported by context. Avoid creating a rigid visual dictionary in which each feeling has one face.

### Happiness

Show varied forms such as a soft smile, relaxed posture, shared attention, laughter, energy, or quiet enjoyment. Avoid making happiness the default “correct” ending to every lesson.

### Sadness

Sadness may appear through lowered energy, gaze, posture, distance, or tears. Tears should not be mandatory. Avoid making the character look broken, helpless, or isolated unless the narrative responsibly addresses that condition.

### Fear

Use tension, protective posture, widened attention, proximity to support, or environmental context. Avoid horror imagery, looming threats, extreme eyes, and dark visual effects that frighten beyond the educational purpose.

### Anger

Show bodily tension, eyebrows, stance, hands, distance, or action context without turning the character red, monstrous, explosive, or violent by default. Distinguish anger from harmful behaviour and show choices, boundaries, or support.

### Embarrassment

Use subtle gaze, posture, facial warmth, hands, or social context. Avoid public humiliation, ridicule, or exposing a character for comedy. Embarrassment should not be represented as guilt unless the story establishes it.

### Pride

Pride can appear through posture, expression, showing work, connection, or quiet satisfaction. Avoid triumph over another person or exaggerated reward imagery. Healthy pride need not be loud.

### Frustration

Show effort meeting difficulty through posture, repeated attempts, a pause, or facial tension. Avoid portraying frustration as misbehaviour. Images may model help-seeking, breaking a task down, or resting.

### Curiosity

Use attentive gaze, leaning, questions, exploration, comparison, or open posture. Avoid reducing curiosity to a single raised eyebrow or making curious children appear intrusive.

### Calm

Calm may involve steady breathing, grounded posture, focused activity, safe connection, or rest. Do not equate calm with perfect stillness, compliance, closed eyes, or one meditation pose.

### Gratitude

Show appreciation through words, gestures, shared attention, care, or reflection. Avoid implying obligation, debt, forced affection, or that children must feel grateful for basic care.

Mixed emotions may require combined cues and narrative explanation. Review expressions at actual display size and with users where possible. Alternative text should describe relevant action or context rather than claiming an internal state as certain when it is ambiguous.

## Diversity & Representation

Representation should make the illustrated world feel naturally broad and credible. It should not operate as a checklist in which one character carries an entire category.

### Ethnicity and culture

Represent people from varied ethnic and cultural backgrounds through researched combinations of features, hair, clothing, names, settings, objects, and relationships. Avoid mixing cultural markers randomly or using traditional clothing as the only sign of culture.

### Disability

Include disabled people in ordinary learning, family, play, and professional scenes—not only content about disability. Represent visible and non-visible disability responsibly. Do not use assistive devices as inspiration symbols or remove them when inconvenient to composition.

### Language

Visuals may show multilingual environments through appropriate signs, books, communication, or context. Essential text should not be embedded in artwork. Language learners should not be portrayed as less intelligent or socially peripheral.

### Family structures

Show single-parent, two-parent, blended, extended, adoptive, foster, guardian-led, and other families where appropriate. Do not present one structure as the neutral standard and others as special cases. A trusted adult need not be a parent.

### Skin tones

Use a considered range of skin tones with stable character values and sufficient contrast against clothing and backgrounds. Avoid unnatural shifts across assets, ashy or overly saturated rendering, and one token dark-skinned character in an otherwise uniform world.

### Hair textures

Represent straight, wavy, curly, coily, braided, loc'd, covered, short, long, and protective styles with informed construction and continuity. Hair should not become a joke, exotic detail, or visual shorthand for personality.

### Assistive devices

Wheelchairs, canes, hearing devices, glasses, communication tools, prostheses, sensory aids, and other devices should be functional, consistent, and used plausibly. Research posture, scale, grip, seating, and interaction. Do not place a device randomly to signal inclusion.

### Classroom diversity

Classrooms should include varied participation, communication, seating, movement, devices, and adult support without segregating one child visually. Avoid showing inclusion as everyone completing the same action in the same way.

Representation requires review by people with relevant cultural or lived knowledge. No individual reviewer represents an entire community. Record decisions and correct errors openly.

## Classroom Illustrations

Classroom scenes should reflect real educational conditions while maintaining visual clarity.

Teachers should be shown facilitating, modelling, listening, observing, adapting, and collaborating. Students should have varied posture, attention, expression, participation, and access needs. Avoid a room where every child sits identically, smiles, and looks at the teacher.

Classroom environments may vary in size, resources, furniture, technology, decoration, climate, and cultural context. Do not assume every school has individual desks, digital displays, specialist materials, or abundant space. Keep backgrounds simple enough for the learning action to remain clear.

Activities should be physically and educationally plausible. Hands, tools, books, worksheets, seating, and group relationships need correct scale and use. If an image demonstrates a process, show steps or cues in a sequence users can follow.

Emotional interactions should preserve privacy and dignity. Do not depict a child disclosing personal distress to an entire class unless the content specifically and responsibly examines that situation. Show options such as private conversation, passing, non-verbal response, or adult support.

Avoid using punitive classroom imagery to create contrast with an approved method. Humiliation, isolation, and anger should not become dramatic “before” scenes. When boundaries or conflict are shown, focus on safety, co-regulation, repair, and realistic adult responsibility.

Teacher and accessibility review should confirm that the scene supports the resource and does not model an unsafe or unworkable activity.

## Parent & Family Illustrations

Family illustration should communicate connection, respect, practical support, and varied home life without idealizing or judging families.

Represent multiple family structures and caregivers across ordinary scenes. Show warmth through attention, proximity, gesture, shared activity, and repair—not only physical affection. Some children and adults do not prefer touch, and affection must never appear forced.

Home environments should vary in architecture, size, objects, income cues, sleeping arrangements, and cultural context without turning difference into poverty spectacle or aspirational consumption. Include enough setting to explain the moment, but avoid clutter that suggests a family's worth or competence.

Caregiving scenes can show listening, routines, boundaries, play, frustration, pause, help-seeking, and reconnection. Adults may make mistakes; images should not divide them into perfect and harmful caricatures. Do not imply that one calm conversation solves a complex issue.

Avoid gender stereotypes in care, work, emotion, discipline, and household tasks. Do not assume a mother is the emotional caregiver or a father is the authority figure. Clothing, roles, and activities should follow the character and context.

Sensitive family subjects require careful privacy, safeguarding, and cultural review. Images must never make a child responsible for restoring adult relationships or staying in an unsafe environment.

## Storybook Illustrations

Storybook illustration should carry narrative information, emotional continuity, and pacing while leaving room for the reader's imagination.

### Scene composition

Each scene should have a clear focal action and readable relationship among characters. Composition can direct attention through gaze, gesture, placement, scale, light, and negative space. Avoid staging every scene frontally or filling all available space.

### Pacing

Visual density, distance, and composition can slow or quicken reading. Quiet moments may use more space and fewer elements; active moments may contain stronger direction while remaining calm enough to follow. Do not use constant visual intensity.

### Page turns

Page or screen transitions can reveal a decision, consequence, change in setting, or emotional shift. Build anticipation without withholding safety information or using frightening surprise. Important visual cues should not be lost at a fold, crop, or responsive boundary.

### Visual storytelling

Images should add something the text does not already state: setting, relationship, subtext, action, or perspective. Avoid contradictions between text and image. When a story invites inference, ensure the cues are fair and do not depend on a stereotype.

### Emotional continuity

Expression, posture, clothing, objects, environment, and character position should evolve coherently across scenes. Emotional change should feel gradual or narratively explained. A character should not jump from severe distress to broad happiness merely to provide a tidy ending.

Interactive stories need layouts and assets that adapt across screens while preserving sequence, controls, captions, and alternative descriptions. Branches should maintain character and world continuity. Track scene, character, prop, and setting versions to prevent contradictions.

## Educational Infographics

Educational infographics combine illustration, diagrams, symbols, text, and sequence to explain a concept. Their value is clarity, not visual density.

### Diagrams

Diagrams should identify the relationship they show—sequence, cycle, comparison, hierarchy, cause, or association. Direction, labels, and reading order must be clear. Use more than colour to distinguish parts and provide a textual equivalent.

### Psychology concepts

Visual metaphors for thoughts, feelings, bodies, stress, attachment, or regulation must be accurate within their stated scope. Avoid depicting the brain as a collection of simplistic switches or implying that one emotion has one biological location. Specialist review is required.

### Teacher frameworks

Framework visuals should help educators remember and apply steps without turning flexible practice into a rigid formula. State conditions, boundaries, and optionality where they affect safe use.

### Parent guides

Parent infographics should reduce complexity into an understandable sequence, comparison, or reminder. They should not diagnose from a checklist or imply that a short visual replaces full guidance.

### Classroom routines

Routine visuals should use consistent cues and a sequence children can follow. Offer text, icons, and other supports so understanding does not depend on reading or images alone. Routine cards should remain usable when printed and rearranged.

Limit the number of ideas in one infographic. If a visual needs several legends, colours, arrows, and long paragraphs, divide the content. Test whether users interpret the intended relationship without explanation from the creator.

## Backgrounds

Background complexity should follow the educational and narrative need.

### Detailed backgrounds

Use detail when environment is part of the meaning: a classroom arrangement, a home routine, a story setting, a safety context, or an object the learner must notice. Detail should be researched and compositionally subordinate to the focal action.

### Simplified backgrounds

Simplify when the image teaches an expression, gesture, object, sequence, or concept. A restrained background improves recognition, accessibility, reuse, and small-size performance. Simplification should not erase context necessary for interpretation.

### Transparency

Transparent assets allow placement across products, but their edges, contrast, and internal colour must work on approved surfaces and themes. Do not assume a transparent illustration remains readable on every background. Define permitted contexts.

### Isolated assets

Characters, objects, gestures, and symbols may be maintained as isolated assets for reuse. Each needs clear purpose, scale, cropping guidance, metadata, and version. Avoid assembling isolated parts into scenes whose perspective, light, or style conflicts.

### Reusable elements

Recurring furniture, props, environments, and decorative motifs can support continuity and efficient production. Reuse should not make every home or classroom identical or repeat culturally specific objects in unrelated contexts.

Backgrounds should avoid embedded essential text, interface-like shapes, and visual patterns that reduce contrast. Test crops and responsive placements so focal content is not removed.

## Illustration Accessibility

### Colour independence

Meaning must not depend on colour alone. Emotions, choices, sequences, and categories need labels, shape, position, pattern, or context. Check colour-vision differences and grayscale.

### Contrast

Important figures, objects, lines, and symbols should remain distinguishable from their background. When text appears near or within a visual, it must meet relevant contrast requirements and remain readable across approved placement.

### Simplified scenes

Limit irrelevant detail and competing focal points. Cognitive accessibility improves when users can identify the central action and relationship. Provide closer or isolated views when a complex scene is necessary.

### Visual hierarchy

Composition, scale, placement, contrast, and negative space should guide attention in the intended order. Do not rely on a caption to repair an image whose focus is unclear.

### Cognitive accessibility

Use consistent symbols, character appearance, perspective, and sequence. Avoid ambiguous metaphors, hidden visual jokes, and unnecessary symbolism. Show one primary educational idea at a time where possible.

### Printable resources

Illustrations should survive grayscale, low-quality printing, photocopying, and ordinary paper. Avoid large ink-heavy backgrounds, faint outlines, tiny details, and colour-dependent instructions. Leave suitable writing and cutting space where activities require it.

Every image needs an alternative-text decision: concise alternative text, adjacent explanation, extended description, or decorative treatment. The choice depends on context and purpose, not the image alone. Do not duplicate a caption or surrounding text without need.

Animated illustration must respect reduced-motion preferences, avoid harmful flashing, and provide static alternatives. Accessibility review includes the placed asset at actual sizes, not only the source artwork.

## Localization

Illustration localization should preserve character, style, educational purpose, and emotional identity while adapting details that affect meaning.

### English

English-language assets should avoid assuming one national, school, or family context. Text should remain outside artwork where possible. Idioms and culturally specific visual metaphors require review before use as global source material.

### Traditional Chinese

Review signs, writing, books, classroom conventions, clothing, homes, gestures, food, festivals, and region-specific context with relevant local knowledge. Use correct Traditional characters where text is unavoidable and do not substitute Simplified forms mechanically.

### Simplified Chinese

Use appropriate Simplified characters and regional context. Review symbols, school settings, public signs, family scenes, and gestures. A translated label does not make an otherwise culturally mismatched scene localized.

### Japanese

Review written language, school items, indoor conventions, gestures, clothing, homes, and social context. Avoid visual clichés and random traditional motifs. Mixed scripts and vertical text require accurate handling when essential.

### Korean

Review Hangul, educational settings, family context, gestures, clothing, food, and home details. Do not treat Japanese, Chinese, and Korean settings as interchangeable because of geographic proximity.

Localization should not replace every scene with a cultural stereotype. Many ordinary experiences can remain shared when representation is credible. Adapt only where context changes recognition, safety, respect, or educational meaning.

Maintain recurring-character identity across localized settings. Amaka, Bella, Goshen, and Destiny should not change ethnicity, facial structure, body, or personality to fit a locale. Contextual clothing or environment changes must follow character and narrative logic.

Future right-to-left languages may require mirrored composition and directional review. Do not mirror text, handed cultural actions, asymmetrical assistive devices, or narrative details automatically.

Local reviewers should assess final images in context. Record variants, locale scope, source relationship, and approval so adaptations remain maintainable.

## AI Illustration Guidelines

AI may assist with concept exploration, composition alternatives, background studies, isolated-object drafts, and production variations. AI output is an unreviewed proposal, not an approved illustration or character reference.

AI-generated illustrations must follow the same educational, artistic, accessibility, cultural, legal, and governance standards as human-created work.

### Preserve recurring characters

Use approved character sheets and reference assets. Check facial structure, hair, clothing logic, age, body proportions, assistive devices, personality, and relationships in every generation. A name in a prompt is not sufficient character control.

### Preserve proportions

Compare head, torso, limbs, hands, feet, height, and relative scale with canonical references. Reject distorted anatomy, inconsistent age cues, impossible posture, duplicated features, and perspective errors.

### Preserve colour relationships

Use approved semantic and character colour roles. Check skin tone, hair, clothing identity, contrast, background, and theme placement. Do not accept unplanned colour shifts across scenes.

### Avoid style drift

Prompt and review against the canonical flat, soft, calm, shape-led style. Reject changes in rendering depth, line treatment, texture, realism, geometry, facial construction, and visual density that make assets feel unrelated.

### Avoid inconsistent anatomy

Inspect hands, fingers, limbs, eyes, teeth, ears, joints, assistive devices, object contact, reflections, and repeated characters. Technical polish does not compensate for anatomical or functional errors.

### Document prompt changes

Record model or service, date, approved references, prompt, negative constraints, settings where available, source asset, editing, reviewer, rights status, and version. Significant prompt changes should state what problem they address and their effect on continuity.

### Never redesign without approval

AI must not reinterpret an existing character, palette, style, device, or cultural identity during routine production. Unintended variation is a defect, not creative evolution. Redesign follows formal governance and migration.

AI must not fabricate documentary events, testimonials, partnerships, classrooms, or people. Photorealistic generation is generally inconsistent with the canonical system and raises additional trust and consent concerns.

Do not imitate a living artist's distinctive style or use unapproved personal, child, copyrighted, or confidential references. Confirm licensing and rights for generation, editing, publication, and long-term reuse.

Human review is mandatory. Reviewers should assess educational meaning, psychology, character continuity, anatomy, representation, culture, accessibility, rights, and placed context. AI cannot approve its own output or claim that a visual has passed review.

## Illustration Review Checklist

### Purpose and education

- [ ] The illustration has a defined audience, context, and educational or communication purpose.
- [ ] It helps users understand, remember, reflect, practise, or orient.
- [ ] The central idea is clear without creator explanation.
- [ ] The image adds meaning rather than repeating or decorating text.
- [ ] Psychological and educational content has appropriate review.
- [ ] Metaphors are accurate, proportionate, and identified as metaphors where needed.

### Style and composition

- [ ] The asset follows the canonical flat, soft, shape-led style.
- [ ] Geometry, contours, proportions, expressions, and depth remain consistent.
- [ ] The composition has one clear focal action or relationship.
- [ ] Background detail supports context without clutter.
- [ ] Negative space and cropping support intended placement.
- [ ] The image remains clear at actual display and thumbnail sizes.
- [ ] It does not resemble an interface control accidentally.

### Characters

- [ ] Named characters use the current approved character sheet.
- [ ] Facial structure, hair, body proportion, age, and recognition cues are preserved.
- [ ] Clothing follows established identity, setting, weather, and activity.
- [ ] Personality is consistent without becoming one-dimensional.
- [ ] Character relationships and relative scale remain correct.
- [ ] Assistive devices and recurring objects are consistent and functional.
- [ ] Any intentional character change has formal approval and versioning.

### Emotional expression

- [ ] Emotion is proportionate, authentic, and supported by context.
- [ ] Facial expression, posture, gaze, hands, and distance work together.
- [ ] Distress is not exaggerated, frightening, comic, or stigmatizing.
- [ ] Anger is not equated with violence or monstrosity.
- [ ] Calm is not equated with compliance or perfect stillness.
- [ ] Happiness is not presented as the only successful outcome.
- [ ] Mixed and varied expressions remain possible.

### Diversity and dignity

- [ ] People are represented naturally rather than as tokens.
- [ ] Ethnicity, culture, skin tone, hair, body, age, gender, and family are credible.
- [ ] Disabled people appear in ordinary contexts as well as disability-related content.
- [ ] Assistive devices and communication tools are researched and accurate.
- [ ] Roles, clothing, settings, and behaviour avoid stereotypes.
- [ ] No child is humiliated, blamed, exposed, or made responsible for adult problems.
- [ ] Relevant lived-experience or cultural review has occurred.

### Context-specific use

- [ ] Classroom scenes model plausible, inclusive, safe activity.
- [ ] Family scenes represent varied care and avoid gendered assumptions.
- [ ] Story scenes maintain narrative, object, setting, and emotional continuity.
- [ ] Infographics communicate the correct relationship and include textual equivalents.
- [ ] Worksheet visuals survive print, grayscale, photocopying, and ordinary use.
- [ ] Marketing and social assets do not sensationalize emotional difficulty.

### Accessibility

- [ ] Meaning does not depend on colour, facial expression, or position alone.
- [ ] Important figures and objects have sufficient visual contrast.
- [ ] Scene complexity and hierarchy support cognitive access.
- [ ] An appropriate alternative-text strategy is documented.
- [ ] Complex visuals include an adequate explanation or description.
- [ ] Essential text is not embedded in the artwork.
- [ ] Animation respects reduced motion and avoids harmful flashing.
- [ ] The placed asset has been tested at relevant sizes, themes, and outputs.

### Localization

- [ ] Cultural assumptions, gestures, objects, clothing, settings, and symbols are reviewed.
- [ ] English, Traditional Chinese, Simplified Chinese, Japanese, and Korean variants are correct where in scope.
- [ ] Localized text uses correct characters and remains outside artwork where possible.
- [ ] Recurring character identity remains unchanged across locales.
- [ ] Mirroring has been reviewed rather than applied automatically.
- [ ] Locale, source relationship, reviewer, and approval are recorded.

### AI and production governance

- [ ] Human or AI provenance and source assets are documented.
- [ ] Rights, consent, licensing, and approved reference use are verified.
- [ ] AI prompts, model or service, changes, edits, and reviewers are recorded where applicable.
- [ ] Anatomy, perspective, object interaction, and accidental text have been inspected.
- [ ] No living artist's distinctive style has been imitated.
- [ ] No event, testimonial, partnership, or documentary reality is fabricated.
- [ ] The asset has current owner, status, version, filename, metadata, and storage location.
- [ ] Final approval includes relevant art, content, accessibility, psychology, and cultural review.

An essential unchecked item means the illustration is not ready for publication.

## Anti-patterns

- **Hyper-realism:** It can make distress feel immediate, reduce stylistic continuity, and create trust or consent concerns.
- **Horror expressions:** Extreme eyes, mouths, shadows, threats, and distortion frighten rather than educate.
- **Inconsistent characters:** Changing face, hair, age, body, or personality breaks recognition and narrative trust.
- **Overcrowded scenes:** Excess objects and people hide the intended action and increase cognitive load.
- **Decorative clutter:** Patterns, sparkles, shapes, and props without meaning compete with content.
- **Emotional stereotypes:** One face, colour, posture, gender, or culture should not define an emotion.
- **Clip-art mixtures:** Unrelated proportions, line styles, palettes, and perspectives make the ecosystem feel ungoverned.
- **Multiple illustration styles:** Local art directions weaken brand continuity and complicate character reuse.
- **Mascot-like children:** Extreme cuteness and proportions can patronize children and trivialize serious topics.
- **Constant smiling:** It denies emotional range and implies visible happiness is the desired state.
- **Monstrous anger:** Red faces, smoke, flames, and violent distortion stigmatize a normal emotion.
- **Token diversity:** Adding one visibly different character without contextual credibility turns representation into display.
- **Random assistive devices:** Devices used inaccurately or inconsistently signal inclusion without respect.
- **Perfect families and classrooms:** Uniform, cheerful environments make real users feel judged and weaken practical credibility.
- **Gendered roles:** Assigning care, emotion, authority, work, or activity by gender reinforces harmful assumptions.
- **Text-heavy artwork:** Embedded language prevents access, translation, reuse, and responsive adaptation.
- **Colour-dependent meaning:** Users lose the lesson under colour-vision difference, grayscale, or print.
- **Unreviewed metaphors:** Simplistic brain, trauma, or emotion diagrams can spread psychological misinformation.
- **AI anatomy errors:** Extra fingers, impossible posture, broken devices, and inconsistent eyes undermine dignity and trust.
- **Style drift across generations:** Subtle changes accumulate until characters and products no longer belong together.
- **Background spectacle:** Detailed environments turn an educational scene into a visual search task.
- **Fear-based marketing imagery:** Distressed children and alarming compositions must not be used to drive attention or conversion.

Repeated anti-patterns indicate weak briefs, references, review, or governance. Correct the production system rather than editing each symptom independently.

## Future Evolution

The illustration system should grow as Healthy Little Minds adds stories, audiences, languages, formats, characters, and evidence. Growth should deepen the world without fragmenting its visual identity.

Evolution may include:

- New recurring and supporting characters.
- Broader settings, cultures, family structures, disabilities, and activities.
- Additional expression, pose, object, environment, and infographic libraries.
- Motion standards derived from the static visual language.
- Better print, mobile, responsive, and accessible variants.
- Localized scene adaptations and new language contexts.
- Improved production and character-consistency tools.

New additions should begin with a documented need and review existing assets. A new character should have a narrative or representational purpose, not merely add visual variety. A new style should solve a format or audience problem that the canonical style cannot meet.

Proposals should include educational purpose, visual references, system fit, accessibility, representation, localization, rights, maintenance, and migration impact. Experimental assets must not silently become canonical through repeated use.

Changes to foundational style or recurring characters require careful versioning. Record what changes, why, which assets are affected, whether old and new versions may coexist, and how future work preserves continuity. Correct harmful representation promptly even when visual migration is substantial.

Maintain a searchable asset registry with character, scene, object, locale, format, version, owner, rights, accessibility, review, and deprecation metadata. Source and exported assets should have a canonical location. Avoid private libraries that future contributors cannot inspect or maintain.

Evaluate the system through user comprehension, emotional safety, recognition, representation, accessibility, production consistency, and maintainability—not the number of illustrations produced. A smaller coherent library is more valuable than a large archive of unrelated images.

Human artistic judgment remains essential even as generation and production tools change. Technology may support continuity, but it cannot define the dignity, context, and educational purpose of the work.

## Closing Reflection

Every Healthy Little Minds illustration should help people understand emotions, relationships, and learning more easily—not simply make a page look attractive.

The strongest images will often feel simple because the difficult decisions have already been made: what matters, what can be removed, how a person is represented, how an emotion remains honest, and how the visual supports real action. When those decisions stay consistent, illustration becomes a dependable part of learning rather than decoration around it.
