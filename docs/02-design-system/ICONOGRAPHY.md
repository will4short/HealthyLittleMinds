# Healthy Little Minds Iconography

## Purpose

Icons are communication tools rather than decoration. They represent actions, destinations, objects, concepts, statuses, and relationships in a compact visual form. Used well, they reduce repeated reading, support scanning, reinforce learning, and make interfaces feel familiar. Used poorly, they create ambiguity and force users to guess.

This handbook defines the philosophy, visual language, semantic usage, accessibility expectations, localization responsibilities, governance, and maintenance standards for icons throughout Healthy Little Minds. It applies to products, educational resources, stories, media, dashboards, documentation, marketing, AI-generated assets, and future applications.

Icons should:

- Reduce effort when a visual cue is faster to recognize than repeated language.
- Improve navigation by reinforcing known destinations and movement.
- Support educational concepts through stable, explained metaphors.
- Provide additional state cues without relying on colour alone.
- Create visual consistency across components, patterns, and platforms.
- Remain readable across devices, print, themes, zoom, and localization.

Icons should never replace necessary language when language is required for clarity. A familiar play symbol may stand alone within a clearly labelled media player. A symbol for “psychological safety plan” is unlikely to be universally understood and requires visible text. The more consequential, unfamiliar, or abstract an action is, the stronger the need for a label and explanation.

The icon system is not an illustration library in miniature. Icons serve focused semantic roles and should disappear into the task. They are not rewards, mascots, scene-setting art, or a way to fill empty space.

This handbook contains no drawing tutorial, SVG, grid, size, or framework specification. Canonical asset files and implementation guidance should express these principles without redefining them.

## Icon Philosophy

### Meaning before decoration

Every icon should have a defined semantic purpose. It should represent an action, object, status, destination, or concept that users need to recognize. Decorative symbols belong to the illustration or brand system and should not be added to the functional icon library.

When an icon appears beside text, it should reinforce the same meaning rather than introduce a second interpretation. When it appears alone, context and familiarity must make its purpose clear.

### Recognition before originality

Use established visual conventions when they are widely understood and appropriate. Familiar symbols for search, play, close, or download reduce learning effort. An original metaphor may look distinctive but fail if users must decode it.

Recognition is contextual and cultural. A convention common to designers may not be familiar to children, caregivers, or users in every region. Validate important meanings with representative users rather than assuming universality.

### Consistency before novelty

Icons should share construction, visual weight, corner treatment, perspective, detail, and state behaviour. A new icon should belong visibly to the same family and use an existing metaphor where that metaphor already has a defined meaning.

Do not replace familiar icons during a visual refresh without evidence that the current symbol is failing. Novelty creates migration, documentation, translation, and recognition costs.

### Simplicity before complexity

An icon should communicate one primary idea. Remove internal detail that disappears at normal use or competes with the silhouette. Combining several objects to represent a complex concept often creates a small illustration that remains ambiguous.

Simplicity must preserve distinguishing features. A worksheet should not become indistinguishable from a generic document, and a transcript should not look identical to captions. Test icons within their actual neighbouring set.

### Accessibility before aesthetics

Icons need sufficient contrast, recognizable form, appropriate labels, usable targets, and alternatives to colour. A visually elegant symbol that is too thin, unfamiliar, or dependent on a subtle state is not suitable.

Accessibility includes cognitive recognition and cultural understanding, not only technical exposure to assistive technology.

### Education before branding

Educational icons should clarify a resource, process, or concept. They should not be shaped around a logo motif if that makes meaning less recognizable. Brand character comes through consistent soft geometry and calm construction without overriding semantic clarity.

Together, these principles keep iconography quiet and dependable. Users should recognize the meaning before noticing the style.

## Educational Role

### Navigation

Icons can reinforce movement, search, saved content, settings, and other repeated destinations. They are most useful when paired consistently with labels during learning and then recognized in compact contexts. They should not turn navigation into a row of unexplained symbols.

### Psychology education

Icons may help categorize or recall concepts such as thoughts, feelings, breathing, relationships, or communication. Abstract psychological concepts are not universally picturable, so labels and definitions remain necessary. Avoid metaphors that imply unsupported biological or clinical facts.

### Teacher resources

Icons can help teachers scan duration, materials, preparation, objectives, discussion, assessment, print, and download information. Stable use across resources reduces preparation effort. The same symbol should not mean “activity” in one lesson and “assessment” in another.

### Parent guides

Icons can separate observation, conversation, routine, support, safety, and further-help sections. They should create orientation without making serious guidance look like a set of simplistic tips. Parent resources must not rely on icon literacy alone.

### Interactive learning

Icons can identify play, pause, repeat, listen, read, choose, reflect, and continue. Interactions need clear feedback and labels where children may not know the symbol. Icons should not score emotions or imply a visually “correct” personal answer.

### Dashboards

Icons can support resource type, collection, saved state, settings, status, filtering, and media. Dashboards already contain dense information, so icon use should reduce repetition rather than add a symbol to every value.

### Worksheets

Icons can mark instructions, writing, discussion, listening, adult support, or optional tasks. They must remain recognizable in grayscale, low-quality printing, and photocopying. Provide a key when the symbol set is unfamiliar.

### Podcasts

Icons can identify audio, episode length, transcript, captions where video exists, play, pause, volume, download, and sharing. Standard media conventions should remain consistent with platform expectations.

Icons support learning when they create stable memory cues. Their meaning should be taught through repeated labelled use, not assumed from appearance.

## Icon Style

The canonical Healthy Little Minds icon style is simple, calm, friendly, and function-led.

### Clean geometry

Icons should use clear silhouettes and intentional relationships among lines and shapes. Construction should feel orderly without becoming rigid or mechanical. Avoid unnecessary angles, ornamental cuts, and tiny internal forms.

### Rounded corners

Gentle corner treatment supports the wider warm visual language. Rounded forms should remain optically clear and appropriate to the object. Do not soften warning, direction, or tool shapes until they lose recognition.

### Balanced proportions

Icons should occupy comparable visual area and feel balanced when placed together. Optical balance matters more than identical geometric dimensions. Tall, wide, circular, and irregular symbols may need different internal distribution to appear equal.

### Minimal detail

Retain the few features that distinguish the concept. Details that disappear, fill in, or create noise at ordinary use should be removed. A more complex educational symbol may require a labelled illustration or diagram instead of an icon.

### Consistent stroke philosophy

The family should use a coherent approach to line weight, terminals, joins, enclosures, gaps, and filled regions. Stroke treatment should remain clear under reduction, zoom, print, and varied displays. Mixing unrelated line systems weakens family recognition.

### Scalable construction

Icons should preserve recognition across intended contexts, from interface controls to resource headings and print. At smaller use, simplify rather than depend on detail. At larger use, do not add arbitrary texture that creates a different style.

### Calm visual language

Avoid aggressive spikes, exaggerated motion lines, vibrating patterns, dramatic shadows, and comic effects. Status icons should communicate severity proportionately without frightening users. The family should support serious subjects while remaining approachable.

Icon style should be evaluated as a set. An individual asset can look successful while feeling too heavy, detailed, or expressive beside the rest of the library. Approved master assets should record category, semantic name, variant, status, version, and review.

## Semantic Categories

Categories organize the library by purpose. They do not imply that every concept listed must have a standalone icon. An icon should be added only when recurring use and recognition justify it.

### Navigation

Navigation icons represent destinations, movement, utilities, and common file actions.

- **Home:** Returns to the primary starting place. Use a familiar home metaphor and do not confuse it with a family or physical-home educational topic in the same context.
- **Back:** Moves to the previous view or higher context according to the interface. Direction must adapt for right-to-left layouts where navigation order changes.
- **Forward:** Moves to the next view or step. Distinguish browser-like movement from submission or play.
- **Search:** Opens or identifies search. Use the established magnifying metaphor and pair it with a label in primary navigation or child-facing contexts.
- **Menu:** Reveals navigation options. It should not be confused with a list-format control and requires an accessible state indicating whether the menu is open.
- **Close:** Dismisses the current overlay, message, or temporary region. Do not use it for deletion. Consequence should be clear when closing may discard work.
- **Settings:** Opens preferences or configuration. Do not use settings as a container for unrelated utilities.
- **Profile:** Represents the current user's account or profile. Avoid gendered or culturally specific facial features in the generic symbol.
- **Bookmark:** Saves or marks content for later. State must distinguish saved and unsaved and should not be confused with a favourite or download unless product language equates them explicitly.
- **Share:** Opens sharing options or transfers a resource. Because share metaphors vary by platform, preserve labels where ambiguity matters.
- **Download:** Saves a file or content to the user's device. Make resulting file type and availability clear in adjacent content.
- **Upload:** Sends a file from the user to the platform. Do not rely only on arrow direction; label and explain accepted files and privacy.
- **Print:** Opens or initiates print preparation. The resource should already be suitable for print; an icon does not guarantee printable quality.

### Learning

Learning icons identify resource formats, activity types, and progress concepts.

- **Book:** Represents reading, a guide, or a book resource. Define its scope so it does not mean every type of content.
- **Lesson:** Identifies a structured teaching resource. It should differ from a generic book or presentation and remain labelled until familiar.
- **Worksheet:** Represents a printable or fillable activity sheet. Distinguish it through visible task structure rather than a generic document outline.
- **Quiz:** Identifies knowledge checks where answers can reasonably be evaluated. Do not use it for emotional reflection or diagnostic screening.
- **Reflection:** Marks private or guided thought. Use an open, non-judgmental metaphor and pair with language because reflection is abstract.
- **Practice:** Identifies rehearsal of a skill. It should not imply competitive training, perfection, or a guaranteed outcome.
- **Checklist:** Represents a set of confirmable items. Do not use it for ranked scoring or to diagnose psychological conditions.
- **Certificate:** Represents a formal certificate or completion record. Do not imply professional qualification or clinical competence.
- **Progress:** Shows movement through a defined process. Avoid using it as a measure of emotional worth or wellness.
- **Goal:** Identifies an intended learning or action outcome. The metaphor should not suggest that every goal is linear or competitive.

### Psychology

Psychology icons require careful scope because complex human experiences cannot be reduced to simple symbols.

- **Brain:** Represents brain-related explanation or cognition, not psychology as a whole. Avoid pseudo-anatomical detail and claims about specific regions without evidence.
- **Emotion:** Represents emotional experience generally. Do not select one facial expression as the universal symbol for emotion.
- **Thought:** Identifies thinking, ideas, or internal language. A thought bubble may be familiar in some contexts but requires labels and cultural review.
- **Feelings:** Represents noticing or discussing feelings. Define its distinction from emotion if both terms appear in the product.
- **Heart:** May represent care, compassion, or affection, but should not imply a literal organ, favourite state, or medical information without context.
- **Breathing:** Identifies breathing awareness or practice. The icon should not imply one posture or that breathing solves every form of distress.
- **Mindfulness:** Represents attentive awareness. Avoid religious appropriation, compulsory meditation poses, or a promise of calm.
- **Stress:** Identifies stress education or response. Avoid explosive, frightening, or stigmatizing imagery.
- **Resilience:** Represents adaptation with support, not toughness or solitary endurance. Because the concept is abstract, visible text is essential.
- **Relationships:** Represents connection among people. Avoid using only a romantic pair or one family structure.
- **Communication:** Identifies speaking, listening, writing, signing, or exchanging meaning. A speech bubble alone should not imply that speech is the only form.
- **Empathy:** Represents perspective and care without suggesting mind-reading or emotional obligation. Use with a label.

### Teachers

Teacher icons help identify classroom context, preparation, resources, and professional learning.

- **Classroom:** Represents a learning environment without assuming one furniture arrangement or level of technology.
- **Teacher:** Identifies educator-facing content. Generic figures should avoid gender, age, and authority stereotypes.
- **Students:** Represents learners as a varied group. Do not depict identical bodies or make one figure visibly separate as the diversity marker.
- **Planning:** Identifies preparation and organization. Keep it distinct from calendar, checklist, and assessment.
- **Assessment:** Represents an educational check or review. It must not be used for diagnosis or emotional scoring.
- **Resources:** Identifies a collection of usable materials. Avoid a metaphor so generic that it duplicates library navigation.
- **Professional Development:** Marks educator learning. The icon must not imply certification unless the offering provides it.
- **Calendar:** Represents dates, schedules, or planned events and should follow platform and locale expectations.
- **Presentation:** Identifies display or presentation material. It should not assume a particular software brand or classroom technology.

### Parents

Parent icons identify family contexts, practical routines, and support.

- **Family:** Represents varied caregivers and family structures. Avoid one mother–father–child silhouette as the universal form.
- **Conversation:** Marks guidance for talking and listening. Support speech, sign, augmentative communication, and other forms through labels and context.
- **Home:** Represents a home context rather than the application start page. Avoid using the same icon for both meanings within one view.
- **Routine:** Identifies a repeated sequence. Distinguish it from schedule and checklist and avoid implying rigidity.
- **Support:** Points to help, guidance, or connection. Hands or figures can carry cultural and ability assumptions, so use labels and review.
- **Safety:** Represents safeguarding or protective guidance. Use a clear, proportionate metaphor without creating fear or promising absolute safety.
- **Nutrition:** Identifies food and eating guidance. Avoid a single culturally specific food, body judgment, or diet symbolism.
- **Sleep:** Represents sleep or bedtime support. Avoid one culturally fixed sleeping arrangement and distinguish it from dark mode.
- **Play:** Identifies playful activity. It should represent broad play rather than one toy, gender, or physical ability.

### Media

Media icons should align with widely recognized controls and accessible platform behaviour.

- **Podcast:** Identifies podcast content or series, distinct from generic audio and microphone recording.
- **Audio:** Marks listenable content. It should not be used as the volume control itself.
- **Video:** Identifies video content or opens a video experience, with duration and caption availability nearby.
- **Transcript:** Opens a textual record of audio or video. Distinguish it from captions and generic article content.
- **Captions:** Toggles or identifies synchronized captions. State should show availability and whether captions are active.
- **Play:** Starts or resumes media. It should change appropriately to pause while playing and expose state to assistive technology.
- **Pause:** Temporarily stops media without resetting position. Do not use stop and pause interchangeably.
- **Volume:** Opens or indicates audio level. Muted state must be distinguishable beyond a small colour change.
- **Microphone:** Represents recording, microphone permission, or spoken input. Context must make clear whether the platform is listening.

### System

System icons reinforce status, assistance, privacy, and platform behaviour.

- **Success:** Confirms completed action or valid state. It must not label a person, emotion, or answer as morally correct.
- **Warning:** Signals a possible consequence requiring attention. It should remain distinct from error and immediate danger.
- **Information:** Identifies neutral explanatory context. Do not place it beside every helper sentence.
- **Error:** Signals a failed action or invalid condition. Pair with text and recovery rather than using the symbol alone.
- **Help:** Opens contextual assistance or support. Distinguish general help from urgent or professional support.
- **Loading:** Communicates temporary activity. Motion should respect user preferences, and text should explain longer waits.
- **Notifications:** Represents updates the user has chosen or needs. It should not imply urgency simply through badge count.
- **Privacy:** Identifies data use, visibility, consent, or privacy settings. Avoid suggesting complete privacy where limitations exist.
- **Security:** Represents account or information protection. Distinguish security from safety and never use the icon as proof that a system is secure.
- **Accessibility:** Identifies accessibility information or preferences, not disabled people as a separate audience. Use recognized, inclusive symbolism and visible language.

Semantic names should remain stable even if visual assets evolve. One icon should not carry unrelated meanings in the same ecosystem, and two icons should not represent the same meaning without a documented platform reason.

## Icons vs Illustrations

Icons and illustrations serve different purposes.

An **icon** is a compact, reusable symbol for one focused action, object, status, destination, or concept. It belongs to a controlled semantic library, appears repeatedly, and should remain recognizable at small sizes.

An **illustration** is a richer visual that can show people, setting, relationships, action, narrative, emotion, or a complex educational concept. It may be unique to a scene or belong to a recurring visual system.

Use an icon when:

- The concept has a stable, repeated role.
- Users benefit from quick recognition.
- The visual must work in controls, metadata, lists, or compact spaces.
- One focused symbol can communicate the meaning with a label where needed.

Use an illustration when:

- Context, people, emotion, or sequence matters.
- The visual needs to teach a relationship or tell a story.
- Representation and setting contribute to understanding.
- A compact symbol would oversimplify the concept.

Do not enlarge a complex icon and call it an illustration or shrink a detailed illustration into an icon. A hybrid may be appropriate for an educational diagram, but it should have a documented role and accessibility plan.

If a concept requires several combined symbols, explanatory text, or a legend to understand, it likely needs an illustration, diagram, or labelled component rather than a new icon.

## Accessibility

Icons must comply with the Accessibility Standard and should reduce rather than create cognitive effort.

### Recognizability

Use familiar metaphors and test them in context. Recognition depends on age, culture, platform experience, and surrounding information. An icon should not be approved because its creator understands it.

### Colour independence

Shape, label, state, and context should carry meaning. Colour may reinforce success, warning, error, selection, or category but cannot be the only difference. Filled and outline states also need sufficient distinction.

### Contrast

Meaningful icons and their state boundaries should meet applicable contrast requirements against every approved background. Thin details and antialiasing can reduce practical visibility even when calculated contrast passes.

### Labels

Interactive icons need accessible names. Visible labels are required when the symbol is unfamiliar, abstract, child-facing, consequential, or one of several similar actions. Tooltips may supplement but cannot provide the only label because they are not equally available to touch, keyboard, and screen-reader users.

Decorative icons should not be announced. Icons accompanying identical visible text usually should not duplicate it in assistive output.

### Touch targets

The interactive target must be larger than the visible glyph where needed and sufficiently separated from nearby actions. Small appearance is not permission for a small target. Focus must remain visible around the complete control.

### Cognitive accessibility

Use one icon consistently for one meaning. Avoid subtle visual distinctions, abstract metaphors, and large unlabelled sets. Introduce unfamiliar educational icons with text and repeated stable use. Do not require users to memorize a unique symbolic language before accessing content.

Test icons with keyboard, screen readers, zoom, high contrast and forced-colour modes, dark theme, grayscale, print, touch, and users with relevant cognitive and visual access needs. Test the complete control and context, not only the asset.

## Localization

Icons can cross language boundaries, but they are not culturally universal. Localization should evaluate metaphor, direction, gesture, object, text, colour, and context.

### English

Do not treat icons familiar in English-language products as automatically global. Labels should use plain language and distinguish similar actions. Avoid letter-based symbols that depend on English words.

### Traditional Chinese

Review metaphors, gestures, writing tools, documents, family structures, and culturally specific objects with relevant local expertise. Avoid embedding characters. If text is unavoidable, use correct Traditional forms and maintain a localized asset.

### Simplified Chinese

Use appropriate Simplified characters only where essential and review regional conventions. Do not assume an icon approved for Traditional Chinese needs no separate cultural or linguistic evaluation.

### Japanese

Review symbols in relation to local platform conventions, school contexts, media controls, maps, gestures, and written forms. Avoid random traditional motifs that turn localization into decoration.

### Korean

Review local digital conventions, education and family contexts, objects, gestures, and Hangul where unavoidable. Japanese, Chinese, and Korean interpretations should not be treated as interchangeable.

### Future RTL languages

Directional navigation, undo or redo, progression, indentation, and some communication icons may need mirroring. Media playback, clocks, brand marks, numbers, and handed cultural objects may not. Review each semantic role rather than mirroring the entire library automatically.

Icons should not use national flags as language selectors. Language, country, and culture are not equivalent. Avoid hand gestures, animals, religious symbols, clothing, or colours with strong local meanings unless the context requires and review supports them.

Localized visible labels and accessible names are part of the icon component and must ship with the language version. Test real interfaces because translated text length changes the relationship between icon and label.

## Icon Governance

### Proposing icons

A proposal should identify a recurring user need, semantic definition, contexts, audience, existing alternatives, proposed metaphor, recognition evidence, accessibility, localization, and expected reuse. Do not begin with an attractive sketch in search of a meaning.

First review the library for an existing icon, synonym, variant, or component pattern. A new icon is unnecessary when visible text or an illustration would communicate more clearly.

### Reviewing

Review should include design, accessibility, content, and localization, plus education or psychology when the icon represents a learning concept. Evaluate recognition, family fit, distinction from neighbouring icons, state behaviour, platform conventions, and real placements.

### Naming

Names should describe semantic purpose rather than appearance. Prefer a stable role such as `download` to a shape-based name such as `arrow-down-tray`. Record aliases and search terms without creating duplicate canonical assets.

One name should map to one meaning. If platforms require different visual expressions, preserve the semantic name and document variants.

### Versioning

Follow Design System semantic versioning. A corrected asset that preserves recognition may be a patch. A new icon or backward-compatible variant is minor. Replacing a familiar metaphor, renaming semantics, or removing an icon may be major and require migration.

### Deprecating

Deprecation should identify reason, replacement, affected components, migration, support period, and removal version. Mark deprecated icons in design assets, documentation, and implementation and stop new use.

### Documenting

Each icon should record semantic name, definition, category, approved contexts, visible-label requirement, accessible name guidance, variants, directionality, localization notes, states, owner, version, status, source, tests, and related icons.

Maintain a searchable registry and canonical source. Asset exports should be generated or controlled so local copies do not drift. Review duplicate meanings, unused assets, recognition problems, and exceptions regularly.

## AI Contribution Guidelines

AI assistants may search the registry, audit usage, identify duplicates, draft semantic descriptions, generate test contexts, or help implement approved assets. They should not treat icon generation as unrestricted visual invention.

AI assistants should:

- Reuse existing icons and semantic roles before proposing additions.
- Avoid inventing new visual metaphors when a recognized convention or visible label works.
- Preserve geometry, corner treatment, visual weight, stroke philosophy, perspective, and calm character.
- Document proposed additions, purpose, alternatives, risks, and governance status.
- Respect accessibility, including labels, targets, contrast, non-colour cues, and state.
- Explain tradeoffs and uncertainty, especially for abstract or culturally sensitive concepts.
- Test the icon within its family, component, theme, translation, and actual use.
- Update registry, documentation, tests, and release notes for approved changes.

AI must not fabricate recognition research, accessibility testing, user approval, cultural universality, or supported states. It should not generate several near-duplicate icons and choose by appearance alone. Abstract psychology icons require education and psychology review.

Generated assets must be checked for inconsistent strokes, geometry, accidental asymmetry, distorted objects, embedded text, style drift, and resemblance to protected third-party icon sets. Rights and licensing require verification.

When instructions request a page-specific icon, the assistant should determine whether an existing icon and label meet the need. If not, it should follow the proposal process rather than silently add an asset. Human review remains mandatory.

## Anti-patterns

- **Decorative icons:** Symbols without semantic purpose create visual noise and make meaningful icons harder to identify.
- **Inconsistent icon families:** Mixed geometry, weight, perspective, and detail make the ecosystem feel ungoverned.
- **Mixing outline and filled styles:** Unexplained style changes look like state differences and weaken family coherence.
- **Culture-specific symbols without review:** A familiar metaphor may be confusing, offensive, or carry another meaning elsewhere.
- **Ambiguous metaphors:** Abstract combinations force users to guess and increase cognitive load.
- **Duplicated meanings:** Several icons for the same role make usage inconsistent and maintenance harder.
- **Icon-only interfaces:** Unlabelled controls exclude users unfamiliar with the symbol and increase errors.
- **One icon with several meanings:** Reusing a heart for care, favourite, health, and feelings makes context unreliable.
- **Tiny internal detail:** Detail disappears at normal use and produces uneven visual weight.
- **Overly literal psychology icons:** Complex experiences cannot be reduced to a brain, face, or heart without explanation.
- **Emotional stereotypes:** Fixed facial symbols imply every person expresses feelings in the same way.
- **Colour-only state:** Users lose selection, status, or severity when colour is unavailable.
- **Inaccessible thin strokes:** Delicate assets fail under reduced vision, print, and low-quality displays.
- **Icon buttons without names:** Assistive-technology users cannot identify the action.
- **Tooltip-only labels:** Tooltips are unreliable for touch, keyboard, magnification, and cognitive access.
- **Small touch targets:** Compact glyphs become difficult or impossible to activate accurately.
- **Automatic RTL mirroring:** Some symbols should mirror and others should not; blanket transformation creates incorrect meaning.
- **Text embedded in icons:** Letter-based assets resist localization and accessibility.
- **Emoji as system icons:** Appearance and meaning vary by platform and cannot provide a controlled family.
- **Third-party mixing:** Pulling icons from several libraries creates style, licence, semantic, and maintenance inconsistency.
- **Page-specific icons:** Local assets bypass governance and rarely receive complete testing.
- **Icons as evidence:** A shield, lock, brain, or certificate does not prove safety, security, science, or qualification.

Repeated anti-patterns should lead to system correction, contributor guidance, or component repair rather than isolated visual cleanup.

## Icon Review Checklist

### Meaning and need

- [ ] The icon represents one defined, recurring semantic purpose.
- [ ] Existing icons, labels, components, and illustrations were evaluated first.
- [ ] The symbol reduces effort or improves recognition in its intended context.
- [ ] The metaphor is recognizable to the intended age and audience.
- [ ] Abstract meaning is supported by visible language.
- [ ] The icon does not imply evidence, safety, or authority it cannot establish.

### Style and family fit

- [ ] Geometry, corners, proportions, weight, terminals, joins, and gaps match the family.
- [ ] The silhouette remains clear and internal detail is necessary.
- [ ] Optical weight is balanced beside neighbouring icons.
- [ ] The asset remains recognizable at every intended use.
- [ ] Outline and filled treatment follows a defined role or state.
- [ ] The icon uses a calm visual language without decorative effects.
- [ ] It does not drift into illustration or diagram complexity.

### Semantic consistency

- [ ] The canonical name describes meaning rather than appearance.
- [ ] The icon has one meaning across products and components.
- [ ] No other current icon duplicates its purpose.
- [ ] Category and relationships to similar icons are documented.
- [ ] Default, hover, focus, active, selected, disabled, loading, and status use are defined where relevant.
- [ ] State differences remain distinguishable without colour alone.

### Accessibility

- [ ] The icon and its meaningful boundaries meet applicable contrast requirements.
- [ ] Interactive use has an accurate accessible name.
- [ ] A visible label is present where recognition or consequence requires it.
- [ ] Decorative use is excluded from assistive output.
- [ ] The complete control has sufficient touch target and separation.
- [ ] Focus is visible and not confused with selected or active state.
- [ ] Meaning survives grayscale, forced colours, zoom, print, and dark theme.
- [ ] Tooltip content is supplemental rather than the only label.
- [ ] Relevant users have evaluated recognition and cognitive clarity.

### Educational use

- [ ] The icon reinforces an educational distinction rather than decorating content.
- [ ] Psychology concepts are accurately scoped and labelled.
- [ ] Emotional icons do not prescribe one universal expression.
- [ ] Teacher and parent resource meanings remain stable across materials.
- [ ] Worksheet icons work in grayscale and ordinary photocopying.
- [ ] Media controls follow recognized behaviour and expose current state.
- [ ] Progress, goals, quizzes, and certificates do not overstate outcomes or qualification.

### Localization

- [ ] Cultural, religious, political, educational, and platform interpretations have been reviewed.
- [ ] English, Traditional Chinese, Simplified Chinese, Japanese, and Korean contexts work where supported.
- [ ] The icon contains no unnecessary language-dependent text.
- [ ] Visible labels and accessible names are localized together.
- [ ] Directionality and future RTL mirroring are documented per icon.
- [ ] Gestures, objects, family forms, and metaphors avoid unsupported universality.
- [ ] Native-language and cultural review occurred in the rendered context.

### Governance and maintenance

- [ ] Proposal evidence, alternatives, intended contexts, and risks are documented.
- [ ] Required design, accessibility, content, education, psychology, and localization reviews are complete.
- [ ] Canonical asset, semantic registry, examples, and tests are updated.
- [ ] Owner, version, status, aliases, source, and licence are recorded.
- [ ] Release type and migration impact follow semantic versioning.
- [ ] Deprecation and replacement are documented where applicable.
- [ ] Product implementations use the canonical asset rather than local copies.
- [ ] AI-assisted work has verified provenance, rights, consistency, and human approval.

An essential unchecked item means the icon is not ready for release.

## Future Evolution

The icon system should expand when products, platforms, languages, educational formats, or repeated user needs require new semantic roles. It should not grow because empty space exists in the library or a new symbol looks appealing.

Before expansion:

1. Define the user need and context.
2. Search canonical icons, aliases, labels, illustrations, and components.
3. Determine whether the need is recurring and suitable for an icon.
4. Research recognized metaphors and cultural variation.
5. Test proposed meanings before investing in a final asset.
6. Complete governance, documentation, accessibility, and localization review.

New icons may begin as experimental when recognition or use remains uncertain. Experimental status needs an owner, approved limited contexts, evaluation plan, and decision date. It should not spread into production through copied assets.

Evolution also includes consolidation and correction. Merge duplicate meanings, improve weak recognition, correct cultural or accessibility problems, and retire unused metaphors. Preserve aliases and migration guidance so consumers can move without creating forks.

Platform-specific expressions may be appropriate when a native convention is more recognizable. Preserve shared semantic naming and documentation while recording the visual variant and permitted platform. Differences should serve users, not implementation preference.

Maintain a searchable inventory of use, adoption, user feedback, recognition issues, duplicates, licence, and deprecations. Review whether labels are still needed, but remove them only when evidence supports recognition across relevant audiences—not merely because the team has become familiar with the icon.

The aim is not the largest possible library. It is a small, coherent visual vocabulary that can grow without asking users to learn a new symbolic language for each product.

---

## Closing Reflection

Icons should quietly reduce effort, improve orientation, and support learning without drawing unnecessary attention to themselves. Their value comes from meaning that remains stable, recognizable, accessible, and appropriate across contexts.

When the icon system works, users do not pause to admire or decode it. They understand where to go, what an action does, and how information relates. That quiet confidence is the standard Healthy Little Minds iconography should protect.
